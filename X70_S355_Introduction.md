# To study the mechanical, microstructural and wear behaviour of dissimilar API X70 and S355 structural steel SMAW welds fabricated by using E9018 electrodes

## Introduction

The sustained growth in global energy consumption has intensified the construction of long-distance hydrocarbon transmission networks, for which high-strength low-alloy (HSLA) steels remain the primary structural material owing to their favourable combination of strength, toughness and weldability [1]. Among these grades, API 5L X70 pipeline steel is widely specified for high-pressure oil and gas transmission because its thermo-mechanically controlled, fine ferrite–acicular ferrite microstructure delivers high tensile strength while minimising wall thickness and installation cost [2]. The in-service integrity of such lines is governed largely by the welded joints, where the non-equilibrium thermal cycle produces pronounced microstructural gradients across the base metal (BM), fusion zone (FZ) and heat-affected zone (HAZ) that dictate the local mechanical response [3]. Numerous investigations have shown that the toughness and strength of X70 weldments are maximised when the weld metal develops a high fraction of fine, interlocking acicular ferrite, whereas coarse grain-boundary and polygonal ferrite degrade these properties [4]. Multi-pass shielded metal arc welding (SMAW) further complicates this picture, since reheating of previously deposited passes creates alternating columnar and refined regions together with a characteristic hardness distribution across the joint [5].

In parallel, S355 is among the most extensively used structural steels for construction, bridges, cranes, offshore platforms and wind-turbine support structures, valued for its moderate strength, excellent weldability and economical production [6]. Welded S355 assemblies in offshore and heavy-engineering service are routinely exposed to fatigue loading, residual stresses and demanding fabrication sequences, and their performance is strongly linked to the HAZ microstructure generated during welding [7]. The increasing use of hybrid steel structures—in which high-pressure pipelines and X70-grade components interface with S355 structural supports, nozzles and transition pieces—has created a practical demand for reliable dissimilar X70/S355 welds. However, joining steels of markedly different chemistry, strength and hardenability introduces well-documented metallurgical difficulties, including weld-pool dilution, elemental partitioning and carbon migration across the fusion boundary [8]. These phenomena, combined with the formation of brittle or softened sub-zones, can produce sharp property mismatches that compromise the structural reliability of the joint [9]. Because X70 possesses a higher carbon equivalent and strength than S355, the two sides of the joint respond differently to the same thermal cycle, so a single traverse across the weld typically reveals an asymmetric hardness distribution with a harder HAZ on the X70 side and a softer HAZ and base metal on the S355 side.

A recurring concern in dissimilar and high-strength steel welds is HAZ softening, in which the sub-critical and inter-critical regions of the lower-hardenability steel lose hardness relative to the base metal, forming a mechanically weak band that localises strain [10]. Conversely, dilution of alloying elements into the fusion zone may either raise weld-metal hardenability—promoting hard transformation products—or shift it toward softer ferritic structures, depending on the filler and base-metal combination [11]. These competing effects have motivated systematic studies of dissimilar X70 joints with austenitic and duplex stainless steels, which have clarified the influence of filler selection and heat input on interfacial microstructure and toughness [12], [13]. Comparable work on X70 welded to pressure-vessel steel SA516 and to super-duplex stainless steel using gas-metal-arc and laser processes has further established the link between weld-zone microstructure, hardness distribution and joint performance [14], [15]. Nonetheless, the dissimilar combination of X70 pipeline steel with S355 structural steel—both ferritic, yet differing substantially in strength and carbon equivalent—remains comparatively unexplored, particularly for the SMAW process that dominates field fabrication and repair.

The choice of welding consumable is decisive for the properties of such a joint. The E9018 low-alloy electrode, classified under AWS A5.5, is attractive for dissimilar X70/S355 welding because its controlled alloy chemistry and low-hydrogen potential favour the development of an acicular-ferrite-rich weld metal with balanced strength and toughness [16]. The deposition sequence and slag–metal reactions characteristic of SMAW strongly influence the resulting weld-metal microstructure and inclusion population [17], which in turn govern the nucleation of acicular ferrite on intragranular non-metallic inclusions—a mechanism repeatedly confirmed to enhance toughness in HSLA weld metal and coarse-grained HAZ [18], [19]. The accumulated literature on acicular ferrite formation demonstrates that fine, chaotically oriented ferrite laths impede cleavage-crack propagation and thereby improve impact resistance [20], while micro-alloying studies on S355-type structural steels highlight the sensitivity of the inter-critically reheated HAZ to toughness loss through martensite–austenite constituents [21].

Beyond strength and toughness, the tribological behaviour of welded joints is increasingly recognised as a critical yet frequently neglected performance criterion for components subject to sliding contact, erosion and abrasive service. Studies of steel weld joints have shown that the different weld regions exhibit distinct wear responses governed by the welding thermal cycle and the resulting microstructure and hardness [22], and that the welding heat input directly modifies the wear resistance of the HAZ in HSLA steels [23]. Investigations across welded cross-sections consistently identify the HAZ as the region of lowest wear resistance, reflecting its heterogeneous and often softened microstructure [24]. Standardised pin-on-disc testing in accordance with ASTM G99 provides a reliable basis for quantifying these differences under controlled dry-sliding conditions [25]. For a dissimilar X70/S355 joint, where hardness varies steeply from one base metal to the other, such localised wear characterisation is particularly important for predicting the component's durability in abrasive or contact-loaded service.

Despite this breadth of research, a combined assessment of the impact toughness, microhardness distribution, microstructural evolution and dry-sliding wear behaviour of dissimilar API X70/S355 SMAW joints fabricated with E9018 electrodes has not been reported. The present study therefore characterises these aspects jointly, correlating the mechanical and wear performance of the BM, FZ and HAZ regions with their microstructures, to identify the governing metallurgical factors and to establish the suitability of E9018-based SMAW for such dissimilar structural–pipeline connections.
