## Abstract

The economic consequences of climate inaction represent a mounting global crisis with profound implications for healthcare systems, workforce productivity, and macroeconomic stability. Recent analyses reveal that climate-driven health impacts could cost the global economy at least $1.5 trillion in lost productivity by 2050 across critical sectors including food and agriculture, the built environment, and healthcare. Heat exposure alone accounted for 640 billion lost labour hours in 2024, equivalent to productivity losses of approximately $1.09 trillion. The World Bank projects that between 2026 and 2050, climate change could cause 14.5 to 15.6 million deaths in low- and middle-income countries, with associated economic costs reaching $8.6 to $20.8 trillion. Delayed climate action could significantly increase transition costs, with a three-year delay raising costs from 0.5% to 1.3% of global GDP by 2030. The healthcare sector faces dual pressures: rising demand from climate-sensitive diseases and workforce productivity losses projected at $200 billion. These findings underscore that climate inaction is economically irrational, because the costs of inaction substantially exceed the investments required for decarbonisation and adaptation, demanding urgent policy responses to protect both population health and economic resilience.

**Keywords:** Climate inaction; Economic costs; Healthcare systems; Productivity loss; Workforce health; Heat stress

## 1. Introduction

Every year in which greenhouse gas emissions continue on their current path adds to a stock of warming that cannot be quickly reversed. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) concludes that global surface temperature has already risen by about 1.1 °C above pre-industrial levels and that every increment of further warming intensifies multiple and concurrent hazards [1]. Its assessment of impacts and adaptation finds that climate change is already damaging human health, livelihoods and infrastructure, and that these losses fall most heavily on people and regions with the least capacity to cope [2]. Climate inaction, understood in this chapter as the failure to reduce emissions and to invest in adaptation at the pace and scale the evidence requires, is therefore a choice with a price. That price is paid in illness and death, in hours of work that cannot be performed, in hospitals that must treat more patients with fewer resources, and in national accounts that grow more slowly than they otherwise would.

The evidence on that price has changed markedly in the past few years. Earlier economic assessments relied mainly on integrated assessment models that expressed damages as a small percentage of global output late in the century. Newer work measures losses that are already occurring and links them directly to health. The 2025 report of the Lancet Countdown on health and climate change estimates that heat exposure caused the loss of 640 billion potential labour hours in 2024, with associated income losses of about US$1.09 trillion, and that the monetised value of heat-related deaths among people older than 65 reached US$261 billion [3]. A 2025 World Economic Forum (WEF) analysis with Boston Consulting Group projects that climate-driven health effects could cost at least US$1.5 trillion in lost productivity by 2050 across three sectors alone: food and agriculture, the built environment, and health and healthcare [4]. The World Bank estimates that climate change could cause 14.5 to 15.6 million deaths in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) between 2026 and 2050, with economic costs between US$8.6 trillion and US$20.8 trillion [5]. Central banks have also entered the debate: the Network for Greening the Financial System (NGFS) warns that a three-year delay in transition policy would raise transition costs from 0.5% to 1.3% of global gross domestic product (GDP) by 2030 [6].

These numbers revive a central argument of the Stern Review, which held that the costs of strong early action are far smaller than the costs of inaction, and that delay makes the problem both more expensive and more dangerous [7]. What is new is the weight of health evidence behind that argument. Health is no longer treated as one damage category among many but as a principal channel through which climate change reaches the economy, and as a domain in which preventive investment yields measurable returns [8]. For a volume concerned with climate-ready preventive healthcare, this matters in two ways. First, the health sector carries a large share of the economic cost of inaction, both as a provider facing rising demand and as an employer whose own workforce is exposed. Second, many of the most cost-effective responses to climate risk, such as heat early-warning systems, occupational heat standards and resilient primary care, are preventive health interventions.

This chapter synthesises current evidence on the economic consequences of climate inaction, with emphasis on health and labour channels. It asks three questions. Through which pathways do climate hazards translate into economic loss? How large are these losses for workforce productivity, mortality, healthcare systems and macroeconomic performance? And how do the costs of continued delay compare with the costs and returns of mitigation and adaptation? Section 2 describes the approach and evidence base. Section 3 sets out a conceptual framework. Sections 4 to 7 examine labour productivity, mortality and human capital, healthcare systems, and macroeconomic and distributional effects. Section 8 compares the cost of delay with the returns to action. Section 9 discusses policy implications, Section 10 sets out limitations and research needs, and Section 11 concludes.

## 2. Approach and Evidence Base

This chapter is a conceptual and evidence-synthesis review rather than an empirical study, so it follows a thematic structure instead of the IMRaD format. The synthesis drew on three bodies of evidence. The first is peer-reviewed literature in climate epidemiology, occupational health and environmental economics, with priority given to systematic reviews, multi-country studies and modelling studies published in high-impact journals. An overview of systematic reviews on the health effects of climate change provided a map of the health outcomes for which evidence is strongest, including heat-related mortality, respiratory and cardiovascular disease, infectious disease and mental health [9]. The second body of evidence comprises flagship assessments by intergovernmental and multilateral organisations, including the IPCC, the World Health Organization (WHO), the World Bank, the International Labour Organization (ILO), the United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP) and the NGFS. The third comprises economic studies by policy institutes and industry bodies that quantify health-related losses at sector level, such as the WEF analysis prepared with Oliver Wyman, which projects 14.5 million additional deaths and US$12.5 trillion in economic losses worldwide by 2050, including US$1.1 trillion in extra costs to healthcare systems [10].

Sources were selected for relevance to at least one of four outcome domains: labour productivity, mortality and morbidity, healthcare system costs, and macroeconomic or fiscal performance. The synthesis emphasised studies published from 2015 onward, reflecting the period after the Paris Agreement in which attribution science and health-economic modelling developed rapidly, but older foundational works were retained where they established methods still in use. The ILO assessment of heat stress and labour productivity, for example, remains the reference point for sector-level projections of working hours lost to heat [11].

Monetary estimates are reported in United States dollars as published by the original authors. They were not converted to a common price year or re-discounted, because the underlying studies differ in base year, discount rate, valuation method and geographic scope. For this reason, the figures presented here should be compared in order of magnitude rather than added together. Where studies report ranges, the full range is shown. ## 3. Conceptual Framework: From Climate Hazards to Economic Loss

Economic damage from climate change reaches households, firms and governments through a chain of linked processes. Figure 1 organises this chain into four stages: climate hazards, health impacts, economic channels and macroeconomic outcomes. The framework is deliberately simple. Its purpose is to show that health is not a side effect of climate change but the main route through which several hazards reach the economy.

The first stage consists of physical hazards. Extreme heat and humid heat are the most pervasive, but floods, tropical cyclones, droughts, wildfires and shifts in the geographic range of disease vectors are also important. The second stage consists of health impacts. Hot weather raises the risk of heat exhaustion, heat stroke, acute kidney injury and cardiovascular and respiratory events, and it worsens existing chronic conditions [12]. Climatic hazards also aggravate infectious diseases. A systematic assessment found that 218 of 375 known human pathogenic diseases, or 58%, had at some point been aggravated by at least one climatic hazard [13]. Floods and storms cause injury, displacement and mental distress, while drought and crop failure raise the risk of undernutrition.

The third stage translates these health effects into economic channels. Sick or heat-stressed workers work fewer hours or work less productively. Health systems face higher demand and higher costs. Damaged facilities and broken supply chains interrupt care and production. Premature deaths and childhood undernutrition erode human capital, reducing the stock of skills and labour available in future decades. The fourth stage aggregates these channels into macroeconomic outcomes: slower growth, pressure on public budgets, stress in insurance and financial markets, and wider inequality. Disaster losses are especially damaging for poor households. The World Bank estimates that natural disasters cause annual losses in well-being equivalent to US$520 billion in consumption and push around 26 million people into extreme poverty each year [14].

The framework also contains a feedback loop. Lost output and fiscal strain reduce the resources available for mitigation and adaptation, which raises future exposure. This mechanism explains why delay compounds costs. Adaptation also has a cost of its own, and studies that ignore it understate damages. A widely cited global analysis of mortality valued both the deaths caused by warming and the money societies spend to protect themselves, such as air conditioning, and found that under a high-emissions scenario the combined mortality-related cost could reach about 3.2% of global GDP by 2100 [15]. In other words, even where adaptation prevents deaths, it diverts resources that would otherwise support consumption or investment.

## 4. Workforce Productivity and the Economics of Heat

### 4.1 Physiological basis of heat-related productivity loss

Labour is the channel through which heat most directly reduces output. When heat stress rises, the body sheds heat by sweating and by increasing blood flow to the skin. If the environment is hot and humid, these mechanisms become insufficient and core body temperature rises. Workers protect themselves, often unconsciously, by slowing their pace, taking longer breaks or stopping work altogether. Occupational health research has established that work capacity declines as wet-bulb globe temperature rises, and that the decline is steepest for heavy physical work performed outdoors or in poorly ventilated buildings [16]. Heat also impairs concentration and raises the risk of workplace injuries, so the effects extend to indoor and cognitive tasks.

Humidity matters as much as temperature. Analyses that account for humidity and direct sunlight find that labour losses among outdoor workers in the tropics are substantially larger than earlier estimates based on temperature alone [17]. Heat also affects how many people are able to work, not only how productively they work. A multi-model study that combined effects on labour productivity and labour supply projected the largest declines in total labour capacity in tropical, low-income regions, where the share of outdoor work is high and access to cooling is limited [18].

### 4.2 Magnitude of current and projected losses

The magnitude of labour losses is now measured as well as modelled. According to the Lancet Countdown, the 640 billion potential labour hours lost to heat exposure in 2024 set a new record, and the associated income loss of US$1.09 trillion fell disproportionately on agricultural and construction workers in countries with low and medium human development [3]. The ILO projected in 2019 that heat stress would cause the loss of 2.2% of total working hours worldwide by 2030, equivalent to 80 million full-time jobs, with agriculture accounting for about 60% of hours lost and construction for about 19% [11].

Economic modelling shows how these hours translate into output. A global computable general equilibrium analysis found that heat-induced declines in worker productivity reduce GDP most in South and Southeast Asia and sub-Saharan Africa, and that the losses spread through trade to regions with lower direct exposure [19]. High-income countries are not immune. Labour losses from extreme heat in the United States attributable to climate change have been estimated at roughly US$100 billion per year, with losses projected to grow substantially by mid-century without adaptation [20].

Heat also damages workers' health directly. The ILO estimates that more than 2.4 billion workers, about 70% of the global workforce, are likely to be exposed to excessive heat at some point in their working lives, and that excessive heat is associated with nearly 23 million occupational injuries and almost 19,000 work-related deaths each year [21]. These figures matter economically because injuries generate medical costs, compensation claims and lost experience, and because worker deaths remove skills that took years to build.

### 4.3 Sector-level costs to 2050

The WEF and Boston Consulting Group analysis disaggregates the projected losses to 2050 by sector [4]. In food and agriculture, climate-related illness and heat could cause US$740 billion in lost output and more than 600,000 worker deaths. In the built environment, projected productivity losses are US$570 billion, with up to 120,000 worker deaths. In health and healthcare, losses are estimated at about US$200 billion. The comparison shows that a single year of heat-related income loss already exceeds the cumulative sector-level losses projected for food and agriculture over the next quarter century, although the two estimates use different methods and should not be summed. ### 4.4 Regional perspective: South Asia and other tropical economies

The global totals hide a strong geographic concentration. South Asia, and India in particular, accounts for a large share of the working hours that heat removes from the world economy, because the region combines high temperatures and humidity with a large agricultural and construction workforce [11]. In these economies, a large part of employment is informal, so lost hours translate directly into lost daily wages, with no sick pay or insurance to cushion the loss. Workers who cannot afford to stop often continue under dangerous conditions, which raises the risk of heat illness and long-term kidney damage [16].

Humid heat makes the situation worse. Estimates that account for humidity show that losses for outdoor workers in tropical regions are larger than those calculated from air temperature alone, and these regions are where outdoor work is most common [17]. When the effects on both productivity and labour supply are combined, the largest declines in labour capacity are projected for tropical, low-income countries [18]. The economic consequences extend beyond the directly affected sectors. Lower output from farms and building sites raises food and construction costs, and trade links carry these losses to other regions [19].

For a country such as India, the policy implication is that heat is an economic as well as a public health problem. Protecting outdoor workers, adjusting working hours during the hottest periods, and expanding access to shade, water and cooling are measures that protect both health and national output. Because many of the workers at risk are informal, these measures need to be delivered through municipalities, employers and community organisations as well as through formal labour regulation.

## 5. Mortality, Morbidity and the Erosion of Human Capital

### 5.1 Temperature-related mortality

Mortality is the largest single component of most estimates of the health costs of climate change. A multi-country analysis of temperature-related mortality projected that, under high-emissions scenarios, increases in heat-related deaths would outweigh reductions in cold-related deaths in most regions, with the largest net increases in warmer and poorer regions [22]. A global assessment covering 2000 to 2019 attributed about 5.08 million deaths per year, or 9.43% of all deaths, to non-optimal ambient temperatures, and found that heat-related mortality was rising as the planet warmed [23]. Attribution science now separates the share of these deaths caused by human influence. Across 732 locations in 43 countries, 37% of warm-season heat-related deaths between 1991 and 2018 were attributable to anthropogenic climate change [24].

Single heatwaves can be very costly. During the summer of 2022, an estimated 61,672 people died from heat-related causes in Europe, despite the continent's comparatively strong health systems and early-warning capacity [25]. Such events show that wealth does not remove vulnerability, particularly in ageing populations. The Lancet Countdown's valuation of heat-related deaths among older adults, at US$261 billion in 2024, reflects this demographic exposure [3].

### 5.2 Infectious disease and long-term burdens

Climate change also extends the reach of infectious diseases. Warmer temperatures, altered rainfall and urban growth are expanding the areas suitable for mosquito vectors. A multi-model intercomparison study projected that, under high-emissions and high-population-growth scenarios, up to about 4.7 billion additional people could be at risk of malaria and dengue by the 2070s compared with 1970 to 1999 [26]. Each additional case generates direct medical costs and indirect costs through lost work and schooling. Repeated infections and undernutrition in early childhood reduce educational attainment and adult earnings, so their costs persist for decades.

### 5.3 Valuing lives lost to emissions

Economists have developed methods to link emissions directly to mortality. The mortality cost of carbon estimates the number of deaths caused by an additional tonne of carbon dioxide. One such estimate found that adding 4,434 tonnes of carbon dioxide in 2020, roughly the lifetime emissions of 3.5 average Americans, causes one excess death globally over the century, and that the 2020 baseline emissions pathway could lead to about 83 million cumulative excess deaths by 2100 [27]. Including these deaths raises the optimal carbon price considerably. The broader point is that mortality is not an abstract future risk but a quantifiable consequence of each year's emissions.

Figure 3 compares the two most comprehensive projections of climate-attributable deaths and economic losses to mid-century. The World Bank projection for 69 LMICs and the WEF and Oliver Wyman projection for the world as a whole arrive at similar mortality figures, about 14.5 to 15.6 million additional deaths, although their scopes differ [5,10]. Their economic estimates diverge more widely, reflecting different assumptions about the value of a statistical life, morbidity costs and discounting.

## 6. Healthcare Systems Under Dual Pressure

### 6.1 Rising demand and the threat to universal health coverage

Healthcare systems face two pressures at once. Demand rises because climate change increases the incidence of heat illness, injuries, respiratory and cardiovascular disease, infectious disease and mental ill-health. At the same time, the capacity to meet that demand falls, because health workers are exposed to heat and disease, facilities are damaged by floods and storms, power and water supplies fail, and supply chains for medicines are disrupted. Climate change therefore threatens the goal of universal health coverage, since rising costs push systems to ration care or shift costs onto households [28]. Out-of-pocket payments then become a route by which climate shocks push families into poverty.

### 6.2 Evidence on healthcare costs of climate-sensitive events

Event-based studies provide concrete evidence of these costs. An analysis of six climate-sensitive events in the United States between 2000 and 2009, including heatwaves, wildfires, floods, a hurricane and ozone pollution episodes, estimated health-related costs of about US$14 billion, most of which arose from premature deaths [29]. A follow-up study of ten climate-sensitive events in the United States in 2012 estimated health-related costs of about US$10 billion, including the costs of deaths, hospitalisations and emergency department visits [30]. These estimates are conservative, because they omit many outcomes that are difficult to measure, such as long-term mental health effects and the disruption of routine care.

The health sector also contributes to the problem. Health care's climate footprint has been estimated at 4.4% of global net greenhouse gas emissions, so health systems that fail to decarbonise add to the hazards they must later treat [31]. This creates an opportunity: actions that cut the sector's emissions, such as energy efficiency, renewable power and leaner supply chains, often reduce operating costs and improve resilience at the same time.

### 6.3 Hidden costs: disrupted routine care and mental health

Many of the costs that climate events impose on health systems do not appear in emergency statistics. When a flood closes a clinic or a heatwave overwhelms an emergency department, routine services are postponed. Immunisation sessions are missed, antenatal visits are cancelled, and people with diabetes, hypertension, kidney disease or cancer lose access to medicines and treatment. These interruptions lead to complications and admissions in the following months, which are rarely attributed to the original event. Event-based cost studies therefore capture only part of the burden, as their authors acknowledge [30].

Mental health is a further hidden cost. Systematic reviews link floods, wildfires, heatwaves and drought to increases in anxiety, depression, post-traumatic stress and, in some settings, suicide [9]. People who lose homes, crops or livelihoods may experience lasting distress, and the resulting loss of functioning reduces their ability to work and care for others. Mental health services in most LMICs are already under-resourced, so additional demand is often unmet and its costs are borne by families. Health systems that face higher costs without additional resources must ration care or shift costs to patients, which threatens the goal of universal health coverage [28]. Including these hidden costs would raise estimates of the economic burden of inaction considerably.

### 6.4 The workforce and the case for prevention

The health workforce is itself at risk. The projected loss of about US$200 billion in productivity in health and healthcare by 2050 reflects illness, heat stress and absence among health workers, many of whom work in buildings without adequate cooling [4]. Shortages of nurses and community health workers are already acute in many LMICs, so any further loss of working capacity reduces access to care.

Prevention offers the most cost-effective response. Ahmedabad, India, introduced South Asia's first heat action plan in 2013, combining early warnings, public awareness, training of health workers and changes in hospital preparedness. An evaluation estimated that the plan helped avoid about 1,190 deaths per year in the summers following its introduction [32]. The WHO operational framework for building climate-resilient and low-carbon health systems sets out ten components, from leadership and governance to service delivery and financing, that health systems can use to plan such measures [33]. ## 7. Macroeconomic, Fiscal and Distributional Consequences

### 7.1 Temperature and economic growth

The health and labour effects described above add up to measurable effects on national output. A landmark study using data from 166 countries found that economic productivity rises with temperature up to an annual average of about 13 °C and declines steeply at higher temperatures [34]. Because many poor countries are already above this optimum, further warming reduces their growth, while some cooler countries may gain at first. The same study projected that unmitigated warming could reduce average global incomes by roughly 23% by 2100 relative to a world without climate change.

### 7.2 Inequality between and within countries

The distribution of these losses is highly unequal. Historical warming has already increased the ratio between the per capita output of the richest and poorest tenths of countries by about 25% compared with a world without warming [35]. Extreme heat alone may have cost the global economy between US$5 trillion and US$29.3 trillion between 1992 and 2013, with the lowest-income regions losing about 6.7% of GDP per capita per year compared with about 1.5% in the highest-income regions [36]. Within countries, losses fall most heavily on outdoor workers, informal workers, older people and those without access to cooling or health insurance. For a book focused on equity, this distributional pattern is central: the costs of inaction are borne mainly by those who contributed least to the problem and have the fewest means to protect themselves.

### 7.3 Aggregate and fiscal risks

Insurance and financial institutions have reached similar conclusions. The Swiss Re Institute estimated that, in a severe scenario of 3.2 °C of warming, global GDP could be up to 18% lower by mid-century than in a world without climate change, with the largest losses in Asia [37]. The NGFS warns that regional climate disasters could cause GDP losses of up to 6% in Asia and 12.5% in Africa within the next three to five years, with knock-on effects on food systems, supply chains and financial stability [6]. Governments face falling tax revenue at the same time as rising spending on disaster relief, health and social protection, so climate shocks also threaten debt sustainability, especially in countries that already have high debt and limited access to finance.

### 7.4 The social cost of carbon and tail risks

The social cost of carbon summarises these damages as the monetary value of harm caused by one additional tonne of carbon dioxide. An updated estimate that incorporated recent advances in climate science, discounting and damage modelling, including mortality, arrived at about US$185 per tonne in 2020 dollars, roughly 3.6 times the value previously used by the United States government [38]. Mortality was the largest single component of this estimate, underlining the importance of health in climate economics. Central estimates, however, do not capture the full risk. Climate change carries the possibility of low-probability, high-impact outcomes, such as the crossing of tipping points, and economic analysis shows that when such fat-tailed risks are present, conventional cost-benefit analysis can substantially understate the value of reducing emissions [39]. A precautionary approach is therefore justified, because the downside of inaction is not bounded in the way standard models assume.

## 8. The Cost of Delay Versus the Returns on Action

### 8.1 Why delay raises costs

Delay increases costs for three reasons. First, emissions accumulate, so each year of inaction commits the world to more warming and more damage. Second, capital invested today in high-emissions infrastructure locks in emissions for decades or must later be retired early, creating stranded assets. Third, a later transition must be faster and therefore more disruptive. The NGFS quantifies this effect: a three-year delay in transition policy would more than double transition costs, from 0.5% to 1.3% of global GDP by 2030, and would halve the effectiveness of transition efforts [6]. The same logic applies to adaptation. Every year in which heat action plans, resilient facilities or occupational standards are not in place produces avoidable deaths and losses.

### 8.2 Returns on adaptation

The returns on adaptation are large. The Global Commission on Adaptation estimated that investing US$1.8 trillion globally between 2020 and 2030 in five areas, namely early-warning systems, climate-resilient infrastructure, improved dryland agriculture, mangrove protection and resilient water resources, could generate US$7.1 trillion in net benefits, with benefit-cost ratios ranging from about 2:1 to 10:1 [40]. Early-warning systems, which are central to preventive heat-health action, were among the most cost-effective. Despite these returns, finance falls far short of needs. UNEP estimates that developing countries will need US$310 billion to US$365 billion per year for adaptation by 2035, about twelve times the roughly US$26 billion in international public adaptation finance that flowed to them in 2023 [41].

### 8.3 Health co-benefits of mitigation

Mitigation also generates immediate health benefits that are often omitted from cost estimates. Burning fossil fuels causes air pollution, so reducing emissions improves air quality and reduces deaths from respiratory and cardiovascular disease. A modelling study of pathways consistent with the Paris Agreement found that the value of health co-benefits from reduced air pollution exceeds the cost of mitigation, with ratios of co-benefits to costs between 1.4 and 2.45 for a 2 °C pathway, depending on the scenario [42]. These benefits occur locally and quickly, unlike the avoided climate damages, which are global and long-term. They therefore strengthen the case for early action from the perspective of national self-interest.

### 8.4 Mobilising resources

Resources for action exist but are poorly allocated. The International Monetary Fund estimates that fossil fuel subsidies reached US$7 trillion in 2022, about 7.1% of global GDP, when both explicit subsidies and the unpriced costs of pollution and climate damage are counted [43]. Explicit subsidies alone amounted to about US$1.3 trillion. Even a partial reform of these subsidies would free resources many times greater than the annual adaptation finance gap. ## 9. Policy Implications for Climate-Ready Preventive Healthcare

The evidence reviewed in this chapter leads to five implications for policy. First, the economic case for climate action should place health at its centre. Health ministries and finance ministries often work separately, yet the largest monetised costs of inaction arise from deaths, illness and lost labour [3,5]. Budget planning that ignores these costs systematically underestimates the returns on mitigation and adaptation. National climate plans, adaptation plans and medium-term fiscal frameworks should incorporate estimates of health-related losses and of the health co-benefits of action [42].

Second, preventive heat-health measures deserve priority because they are inexpensive and their benefits are fast. Heat action plans that combine early warnings, community outreach, training for health workers and cooling centres have reduced mortality where they have been introduced [32]. Occupational heat standards, including rules on rest breaks, shade, water and work scheduling, protect the labour productivity on which household incomes depend [21]. Cool roofs, urban greening and passive building design reduce exposure at the community level [12].

Third, health systems must be made both resilient and low-carbon. The WHO operational framework provides a structure for assessing vulnerabilities, planning investments and monitoring progress [33]. Facilities in flood-prone or hot areas need reliable power, cooling and water, and they need protocols for continuing essential services during extreme events. Decarbonisation measures such as on-site solar generation and efficient cooling can reduce both emissions and operating costs [31].

Fourth, policy must address inequality explicitly. The costs of inaction fall most heavily on low-income countries, outdoor and informal workers, women carrying unpaid care burdens, older people and people with chronic disease [35,36]. Social protection systems that can scale up quickly after a shock, health insurance that covers climate-sensitive conditions, and targeted support for the households most exposed to heat can prevent climate shocks from becoming poverty traps [14]. International finance needs to close the adaptation gap, with a larger share delivered as grants rather than loans to countries already burdened with debt [41].

Fifth, financial supervisors and investors should treat climate-related health risk as a material risk. The NGFS warnings show that central banks already regard climate inaction as a threat to macroeconomic and financial stability [6]. Disclosure standards could require firms with large outdoor or heat-exposed workforces, such as agriculture, construction and health care, to report on heat-related productivity risk and on their adaptation measures [4].

## 10. Limitations and Research Needs

This synthesis has several limitations. The estimates come from studies that use different methods, base years, discount rates and valuation approaches, so they cannot be added together or compared precisely. Many estimates rely on the value of a statistical life, which raises ethical questions when applied across countries with very different income levels, because it can make deaths in poor countries appear less costly. Morbidity, mental health, disrupted education and losses in the informal economy remain poorly measured, which means that most aggregate estimates are likely to understate true costs [9]. The models also differ in how they represent adaptation, and estimates that include adaptation costs alongside avoided deaths offer a more complete picture than those that do not [15].

Several research needs follow. There is a need for better data on health outcomes and labour losses in LMICs, especially for informal workers and women, and for longitudinal studies of how climate shocks affect human capital over the life course. Health economic evaluations of preventive interventions such as heat action plans, cooling in health facilities and occupational standards remain scarce, and more evidence from different settings would help ministries prioritise investments. Methods that combine attribution science with health economics could also help to quantify the share of current losses caused by human-induced warming [24], which matters for loss and damage negotiations. Finally, research on tail risks and compound events is needed, because conventional estimates do not capture the full range of possible outcomes [39].

## 11. Conclusion

The economic consequences of climate inaction are no longer a distant projection. They appear in national accounts, hospital budgets and household incomes today. Heat alone cost the world economy an estimated 640 billion potential labour hours and US$1.09 trillion in income in 2024 [3]. Climate-driven health effects could cost at least US$1.5 trillion in lost productivity across three sectors by 2050 [4], and between 14.5 and 15.6 million deaths in LMICs over the same period, at an economic cost of up to US$20.8 trillion [5]. Healthcare systems are exposed on two sides, through rising demand and through the vulnerability of their own workforce and infrastructure.

The comparison between the costs of inaction and the costs of action is unfavourable to delay. Postponing transition policy by three years would more than double transition costs [6]. Investment in adaptation yields returns several times greater than its cost [40], and the health co-benefits of mitigation alone can exceed mitigation costs [42]. The resources needed to close the adaptation finance gap are small compared with what governments currently spend on fossil fuel subsidies [43].

For preventive healthcare, the conclusion is direct. Protecting health from climate change is one of the most effective ways to protect economies from climate change. Heat-health action plans, occupational protections, resilient and low-carbon health facilities, and social protection for the most exposed groups are investments that pay back quickly. Treating them as optional or postponing them is not a cautious choice. On the evidence reviewed here, it is the most expensive option available.
