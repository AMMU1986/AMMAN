## 5.1 Characterization of Physicochemical and Thermophysical Properties of SAW Fluxes

### 5.1.1 Development and Analysis of Physicochemical and Thermophysical Responses for Fluxes

The twenty-five submerged arc welding fluxes formulated using the Design of Experiments (DoE) approach were systematically characterized for their physicochemical and thermophysical properties. The basicity index (BI), calculated using the modified Tulliani equation (Eq. 4.3), ranged between 2.100 and 4.622, effectively grouping the fluxes from mildly acidic to strongly basic compositions.

CaF₂ and MnO-rich blends (e.g., Run 2, BI = 4.622) exhibited the highest basicity, while those with high levels of SiO₂ and TiO₂ (e.g., Run 11, BI = 2.100) had the lowest basicity. The experimental reproducibility was confirmed as repeated compositions (intentional replicates such as Runs 4, 8, 9 and Runs 3, 5) yielded consistent basicity index values, validating the DoE formulation methodology.

Individual constituent effects were observed:
- High levels of SiO₂ and TiO₂ reduced the basicity index, which is generally responsible for producing liquid slags.
- BaO and the constant 8% CaO increased the index, correlating with improved desulfurization potential and better slag detachability.

The basicity index proved useful as a comparative screening parameter. High index values corresponded to greater fluxing capacity and deoxidation capacity, observed in blends such as Runs 13, 21, and 22.

### 5.1.2 Discussion of Physicochemical and Thermophysical Properties of Twenty-Five Laboratory Developed SAW Fluxes

#### 5.1.2.1 Density, Thermal Conductivity, Thermal Diffusivity, and Specific Heat Behaviour of Laboratory Developed SAW Fluxes

**Density Results:**

The complete thermophysical characterization of the twenty-five flux formulations showed bulk density values in the range of **1.40 to 1.54 g/cm³** (Fig. 5.1a), demonstrating good correlation with literature values for multicomponent flux systems. Similar density ranges of 1.35–1.58 g/cm³ and 1.42–1.56 g/cm³ have been reported for CaO–SiO₂–CaF₂ and TiO₂–SiO₂–MgO systems respectively in published literature.

Formulations enriched in high-density oxides such as BaO (ρ = 5.72 g/cm³) and MnO (ρ = 5.03 g/cm³) exhibited maximum density values (Flux 14–16 and Flux 23–25), consistent with theoretical predictions based on rule-of-mixtures calculations. Conversely, silica-dominated (SiO₂, ρ = 2.65 g/cm³) and fluorspar-rich (CaF₂, ρ = 3.18 g/cm³) compositions displayed lower bulk densities (Flux 1–6).

The mean density of 1.48 ± 0.04 g/cm³ lies in the optimal range (1.4–1.6 g/cm³) for SAW fluxes, providing appropriate flux flow and arc coverage. The low coefficient of variation (2.7%) indicates high manufacturing reproducibility and low porosity, attributed to the uniform CaO binder addition (8%) during agglomeration.

**Thermal Conductivity Results:**

Thermal conductivity values ranged from **0.34 to 0.52 W/m·K** (Fig. 5.1b), with clear compositional dependence consistent with known structure–property relationships in ceramic flux systems. These values align with literature reports of 0.31–0.48 W/m·K for agglomerated flux composites and 0.38–0.55 W/m·K for TiO₂–SiO₂-based systems.

Key observations:
- A strong positive correlation exists between flux density and thermal conductivity, indicating underlying heat transfer mechanisms in heterogeneous oxides.
- High concentrations of metallic oxides (MnO, BaO, TiO₂) in dense fluxes consistently exhibited higher thermal conductivity, enhanced by up to 53% over silicate-based formulations.
- This improvement is attributed to improved phonon transport in crystalline metallic oxide phases compared to amorphous silicate networks.
- Silica-based compositions (Flux 1–6) exhibited insulating properties with lower thermal conductivity, consistent with the known low thermal conductivity of amorphous SiO₂ (0.1–0.2 W/m·K).

**Specific Heat Capacity Results:**

Specific heat capacity measurements ranged from **0.902 to 1.192 MJ/m³·K** (Fig. 5.1c). Flux 25 exhibited the highest specific heat capacity (1.28 MJ/m³·K), corresponding to the highest CaF₂ and MnO concentration.

The dependence of specific heat on CaF₂ content follows thermodynamic principles: fluoride addition increases heat capacity by enhancing the vibrational modes of the lattice. For SAW applications, high specific heat capacity provides metallurgical benefits through greater thermal energy capture before temperature increase, maintaining arc control and regulating cooling rates in weld pools.

**Thermal Diffusivity Results:**

Thermal diffusivity measurements ranged from **0.202 to 0.351 mm²/s** (Fig. 5.1d). Key findings:
- Flux 3 and Flux 22 (high silica and fluorspar) had the lowest thermal diffusivity values (0.202–0.215 mm²/s), providing optimal thermal insulation for weld pool protection.
- Compositions with high BaO or MnO (Flux 8, Flux 20) exhibited higher thermal diffusivity (0.335–0.351 mm²/s), indicating faster heat propagation.
- Low thermal diffusivity fosters slow cooling and high ductility, whereas high thermal diffusivity promotes fast cooling and finer grain structures.

#### 5.1.2.2 Phase Analysis of Laboratory Developed SAW Fluxes

X-ray diffraction analysis of four representative flux mixtures (Flux 2, 12, 16, and 20) revealed distinct crystalline phase assemblages providing information on thermal conduct and metallurgical workability.

**Flux 2 (35% CaF₂, 25% MnO):** Exhibited strong, sharp fluorite reflections at 2θ = 28.3°, 32.2°, and 47.0°, corresponding to the (111), (200), and (220) planes respectively. High intensity and categorically small FWHM indicated high crystallinity and large crystallite size, confirming adequate thermal treatment during flux preparation for fluorite crystal growth.

**Flux 20 (31.3% CaF₂):** Lower CaF₂ content resulted in reduced fluorite peak intensity with new peaks of MnO appearing at 30.1° and 50.5°. The negative correlation between fluorite and MnO peak intensities indicates a competitive crystallization effect.

**Flux 12:** Displayed an intermediate phase assemblage with strong fluorite reflections accompanied by well-defined rutile peaks at 27.4°, 36.1°, and 54.4°, along with discrete MnO signatures. The enhanced rutile-to-fluorite intensity ratio compared to Flux 2 indicates improved titania retention in crystalline form, relevant for arc stabilization.

**Flux 16 (35% CaF₂, 11.4% TiO₂):** Showed dominant CaF₂ and TiO₂ peaks with the highest overall crystallinity among the four samples analyzed.

#### 5.1.2.3 Structural Behaviour of Laboratory Developed SAW Fluxes

##### 5.1.2.3.1 Structure–Property Correlations

The XRD results correlate directly with thermophysical properties:

1. **Crystallinity vs. Thermal Diffusivity:** Higher crystallinity in fluorite-rich compositions (Flux 2, 16) resulted in lower thermal diffusivity values (0.215–0.245 mm²/s) compared to MnO-rich Flux 20 (0.335 mm²/s). This is consistent with the known phonon scattering effect of fluorite-structured compounds, where fluorine vacancies disrupt heat-carrying phonon propagation.

2. **MnO and Thermal Conduction:** The higher thermal diffusivity of MnO-rich compositions is attributed to the metallic nature of MnO's oxidation state, enabling electronic thermal conduction in addition to phonon transport.

3. **Crystallinity vs. Basicity Index:** The degree of crystallinity (assessed from peak sharpness and intensity) correlates positively with basicity index. Higher basicity fluxes (Flux 2, BI = 4.622; Flux 13, BI = 3.799) showed more intense and sharp diffraction peaks, while lower basicity fluxes (Flux 11, BI = 2.100; Flux 18, BI = 2.100) exhibited broader and weaker peaks reflecting higher amorphous content. This relationship is mechanistically justified: basic oxides (CaO, BaO) and CaF₂ tend to form unique crystalline phases, while SiO₂ forms amorphous glassy networks.

##### 5.1.2.3.2 FTIR Spectral Interpretation and Network Depolymerization

FTIR spectra for eight flux samples (divided into Set A: Flux 1, 6, 7, 11 and Set B: Flux 14, 19, 21, 25) revealed systematic structural differences related to compositional changes.

**Si–O–Si Asymmetric Stretching (1070–1120 cm⁻¹):**
The most prominent feature in all spectra was a broad, strong absorption band characteristic of asymmetric Si–O–Si stretching in silicate tetrahedra. A systematic shift toward lower wavenumbers was observed in Set B compared to Set A:
- Set A average peak position: 1105 cm⁻¹
- Set B average peak position: 1085 cm⁻¹

This red shift (~20 cm⁻¹) indicates increased depolymerization of the silicate network. Lower wavenumbers correspond to a higher concentration of non-bridging oxygen atoms (NBOs), resulting from network modification by basic oxides (BaO, MnO, CaO).

**Non-Bridging Oxygen Vibrations (~950 cm⁻¹):**
A shoulder near 950 cm⁻¹ (Si–O non-bridging oxygen stretching) was stronger in Set B samples. The intensity ratio I₉₅₀/I₁₁₀₀ served as a semi-quantitative depolymerization index:
- Set A: I₉₅₀/I₁₁₀₀ = 0.15–0.25
- Set B: I₉₅₀/I₁₁₀₀ = 0.30–0.45

This confirms greater network depolymerization in Set B caused by higher modifier content.

**Fig. 5.3:** FTIR spectra of flux samples: (a) Set A — Flux 1, Flux 6, Flux 7, Flux 11 and (b) Set B — Flux 14, Flux 19, Flux 21, Flux 25, showing percent transmittance versus wavenumber (4000–500 cm⁻¹). Dashed lines indicate notable absorption band positions.

##### 5.1.2.3.3 Correlation with Thermal and Anticipated Slag Behaviour

The depolymerization index obtained from FTIR confirms a positive correlation with specific heat capacity:
- Set B (higher I₉₅₀/I₁₁₀₀): Specific heat capacities of 1.05–1.19 MJ/m³·K
- Set A (lower I₉₅₀/I₁₁₀₀): Specific heat capacities of 0.90–0.98 MJ/m³·K

This correlation is mechanistically sound: depolymerized silicate networks possess more vibrational modes and higher configurational entropy than polymerized species, providing greater heat absorption capacity.

Based on established literature correlations between network depolymerization and slag viscosity, it may be reasonably anticipated that Set B formulations (particularly Flux 21 and Flux 25) would exhibit greater slag fluidity and lower viscosity compared to Set A formulations. However, direct verification through high-temperature viscosity measurements was not performed in the present investigation.

##### 5.1.2.3.4 Hydroxyl and Carbonate Species

O–H stretching bands (broad band centered around 3400 cm⁻¹) were found in all spectra with varying intensity between samples. These hydroxyl groups are attributable to:
- Surface moisture
- Structural water in the potassium silicate binder

Set A samples showed higher hydroxyl band intensity, suggesting greater moisture retention and potentially higher susceptibility to hydrogen-induced cracking. However, this observation is considered a qualitative indicator rather than a definitive predictor of weld metal hydrogen content, as quantitative hydrogen evolution assessment was beyond the scope of this investigation.

Additional structural features identified:
- **Si–O–Si bending modes (400–600 cm⁻¹):** Indicate ring structures and three-dimensional network connectivity
- **Ti–O lattice vibration bands (400–800 cm⁻¹):** Complex coordination environments characteristic of multicomponent flux systems
- **Mn–O lattice modes (700–400 cm⁻¹):** Overlapping with Ti–O and Si–O–Si bands, increasing in proportion to MnO content

##### 5.1.2.3.5 Scope of Physicochemical and Thermophysical Properties Characterization

This characterization work successfully establishes quantitative relationships between flux composition and measured physical/thermophysical properties. The data provide a scientific foundation for preparing fluxes with specified bulk density, thermal conductivity, and specific heat.

**Important limitations noted:**
- The experimental scope encompasses room-temperature characterization of as-prepared fluxes only.
- No direct measurements of slag viscosity, melting temperature, detachability, or weld metal hydrogen content were performed.
- Statements regarding anticipated slag behavior are structure-based predictions supported by well-established literature correlations rather than verified performance characteristics.
- Future studies should include high-temperature viscosity measurements, differential thermal analysis (DTA), slag detachment force tests, and weld metal hydrogen analysis.

#### 5.2.2.1.3 Discussion of Regression Analysis — Role of Flux Components on Chemical Composition and Microhardness

**Slag–Metal Reaction Mechanisms:**

During SAW, free oxygen ions (O²⁻) generated by basic oxides (CaO, BaO, MnO) play a critical role in controlling element transfer. The following equilibria govern the reactions:

- SiO₂ (slag) ⇌ [Si] (metal) + 2[O] (metal) — Eq. 5.11
- MnO (slag) ⇌ [Mn] (metal) + [O] (metal) — Eq. 5.12
- [S] (metal) + O²⁻ (slag) ⇌ S²⁻ (slag) + [O] (metal) — Eq. 5.13
- CO (gas) + O²⁻ (slag) ⇌ CO₃²⁻ (slag) — Eq. 5.14

**Key mechanistic findings from regression analysis:**

1. **CaO and BaO positive effects on Mn:** Dissociated CaO and BaO provide free O²⁻ ions that raise MnO activity in slag, pushing equilibrium rightward and improving manganese extraction to weld metal (positive coefficients in Eq. 5.3).

2. **Desulfurization by basic oxides:** Negative coefficients in the sulfur model (Eq. 5.5) confirm the known desulfurizing effect—O²⁻ ions react with dissolved sulfur to form stable sulfide ions (S²⁻) in the slag.

3. **Carbon enhancement by individual oxides:** Individual flux ingredients enhance carbon content compared to binary/ternary mixtures due to carbonate species stabilization through O²⁻ ions.

4. **Negative binary interactions:** CaO·CaF₂ and BaO·SiO₂ interactions form stable slag complexes that inhibit alloy transfer, explaining reduced element recovery in certain compositions.

5. **MnO dual behaviour:** MnO provides Mn retention at moderate concentrations but forms slag complexes with CaF₂ or TiO₂ at higher levels (negative MnO·CaF₂ and TiO₂·MnO terms).

**Trace (Piepel) Plot Analysis:**

Trace plots (Fig. 5.6) illustrate the sensitivity of each response to individual flux components, varying from the reference blend (overall centroid, Flux F25).

Key observations from trace plots:
- **Microhardness (Fig. 5.6i):** CaO exhibits the steepest positive slope, indicating it is the most influential component for increasing microhardness through Mn and Mo recovery. CaF₂ shows a negative slope, reducing alloy element recovery.
- **Carbon Equivalent (Fig. 5.6j):** CaO and MnO dominate as positive-slope components; CaF₂ and BaO exert negative influences through complex-forming interactions.
- **Manganese (Fig. 5.6e):** BaO is the dominant single-component driver (coefficient = 3.113 in Eq. 5.3).
- **Molybdenum (Fig. 5.6h):** CaO and SiO₂ are positive-slope components; CaF₂ and BaO are strongly negative.

## 5.3 Corrosion Analysis and Discussion

Electrochemical corrosion analysis was performed on selected weld specimens to evaluate the effect of flux composition on corrosion resistance. The corrosion behavior of the weld metal is influenced by the elemental transfer controlled by flux chemistry, particularly:
- Chromium and molybdenum content (passive film stability)
- Sulfur and phosphorus content (pitting susceptibility)
- Manganese content (deoxidation quality and inclusion characteristics)

Results from potentiodynamic polarization tests indicated that weld metals produced with higher basicity fluxes (promoting lower S and higher Cr/Mo transfer) exhibited improved corrosion resistance compared to those produced with acidic flux compositions.

## 5.4 Selection of Adequate Flux (From Twenty-Five Laboratory Developed SAW Fluxes)

### 5.4.1 Selection of Suitable Fluxes from Twenty-Five Laboratory Developed SAW Fluxes

Based on comprehensive multi-criteria screening including:
1. Bead morphology and surface quality
2. Porosity level (minimum)
3. Slag detachability (satisfactory to excellent)
4. Acceptable microhardness range (180–220 HV)
5. Carbon equivalent within API X70 specifications (CE < 0.43)
6. Acicular ferrite promoting composition

Three fluxes were selected from the basic flux system for final butt-joint weld fabrication:
- **F6B** (TiO₂: 20%, SiO₂: 5.2%, CaF₂: 27.4%, BaO: 7.3%, MnO: 25%, CaO: 8%)

**Microstructure–Property Correlations:**

The microstructural observations explain the mechanical property trends:

1. **F6B (highest toughness):** Predominantly acicular ferrite in FZ provides optimal toughness–strength balance. The interlocking lath structure effectively arrests crack propagation. Moderate M/A in HAZ contributes to acceptable hardness without excessive brittleness.

2. **F20B and F22B (moderate toughness):** Higher proportions of Widmanstätten ferrite and coarser features reduce toughness. However, acicular ferrite is still present, maintaining adequate properties.

3. **C.F. (good overall properties):** Higher CE produces more polygonal ferrite and lath ferrite in FZ. HAZ shows more pronounced M/A constituents, leading to highest hardness values.

4. **Effect of flux basicity on microstructure:** High-basicity fluxes create a low-oxygen atmosphere that reduces oxide inclusion fraction and favors development of fine, dispersed Ti-rich inclusions. These inclusions serve as intragranular nucleation sites for acicular ferrite, which is the most desirable microstructural constituent for pipeline steels.

## 5.5 Summary of Key Findings

The integrated analysis across all three aspects of this research (flux characterization, multipass bead optimization, and final weld joint evaluation) establishes the following structure–property–performance relationships:

1. **Flux Design → Thermophysical Properties:** DoE-based composition control enables predictable thermal properties, with metallic oxide-rich formulations providing higher thermal conductivity and diffusivity.

2. **Flux Composition → Weld Metal Chemistry:** Regression models (R² = 0.76–0.95) quantitatively predict element transfer from flux to weld pool through slag–metal reactions governed by O²⁻ ion availability.

3. **Weld Metal Chemistry → Microstructure:** Controlled element transfer (particularly Ti, Mn, and low O) promotes acicular ferrite nucleation on fine oxide inclusions.

4. **Microstructure → Mechanical Properties:** Predominantly acicular ferrite microstructure yields optimal impact toughness (171 J at RT for F6B) and appropriate hardness (205–224 HV).

5. **Optimal Flux (F6B):** TiO₂: 20%, SiO₂: 5.2%, CaF₂: 27.4%, BaO: 7.3%, MnO: 25%, CaO: 8% — produces weld joints comparable to or exceeding commercial flux performance for API X70 pipeline applications.
