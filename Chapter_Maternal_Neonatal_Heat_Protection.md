## Abstract

Extreme heat is now a routine feature of pregnancy and early life in much of South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa, yet preventive health systems rarely treat pregnant women, fetuses and newborns as a distinct heat-vulnerable population. This chapter is a narrative, policy-oriented review that brings together physiological, epidemiological, qualitative and policy evidence to explain why this group is at high risk and why current adaptation frameworks fail to protect it. Meta-analytic evidence links each 1 °C rise in heat exposure, and especially heatwaves, to higher odds of preterm birth and stillbirth, while hospital data from four African countries associate extreme heat in the week before birth with a one-third increase in perinatal mortality. At the same time, only about half of heat-health action plans name pregnant individuals as at risk, and fewer than two in five mention newborns. We propose an integrated preventive model that uses antenatal care, community health worker networks, labour wards and postnatal contacts as delivery platforms for heat-risk screening, early-warning response and clinical cooling. The model is supported by culturally grounded community engagement, structural measures such as cooling access and income protection, and governance reforms that give maternal health actors a formal place in climate-health planning. We close with research priorities and a compact set of facility and community indicators for tracking maternal heat resilience. Protecting mothers and newborns from heat is a practical test of whether climate-ready health care can deliver equity.

## 1 Introduction

Heat is the most direct way in which a warming climate reaches the human body. Its health burden is usually described in terms of older adults, outdoor workers and people with chronic disease, and heat-health planning has been built largely around these groups (Ebi et al., 2021). Pregnant women and newborns appear in that picture inconsistently, often as one item in a list of "vulnerable groups" with no specific actions attached. The omission matters because pregnancy and the neonatal period are short, biologically sensitive windows in which a single hot week can change the course of a life. A preterm birth or stillbirth triggered by heat is not a temporary illness that resolves when temperatures fall. It carries consequences for the child, the mother and the household that last for decades.

Three developments make this an urgent concern for preventive health systems. First, the epidemiological evidence has matured. Large systematic reviews now show consistent associations between heat exposure and preterm birth, stillbirth and other adverse outcomes across many countries (Chersich et al., 2020; Lakhoo et al., 2025). Second, new studies from low- and middle-income countries, where most births and most heat exposure occur, show that these risks are present and probably larger in settings with limited cooling, water and health care (Dimitrova et al., 2024; Hanson et al., 2024). Third, the policy gap has been documented. Reviews of heat-health action plans show that mothers and newborns are frequently missing from them, and that the interventions that do exist focus on awareness messages rather than service delivery or structural change (Czerniewska et al., 2025).

This chapter responds to that gap. It asks three questions. Why are pregnant women, fetuses and newborns at particular risk from heat? Why have existing adaptation frameworks failed to protect them? And what would a preventive health system look like if it treated maternal and newborn heat protection as a core function rather than an afterthought? The chapter draws on evidence from India and sub-Saharan Africa in particular, because these regions combine high and rising heat exposure, a large share of the world's births, and health systems that already deliver maternal care at scale through antenatal clinics and community health workers.

The chapter proceeds in four parts. Section 3 reviews the physiological and epidemiological evidence and examines the invisibility of mothers and newborns in heat-health action plans. Section 4 sets out operational pathways for integrating heat protection into antenatal care, facility readiness and community outreach. Section 5 turns to the sociocultural and structural conditions that determine whether those pathways reach the poorest women. Section 6 proposes governance reforms, research priorities and a monitoring framework. Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Approach and Scope

This is a narrative, policy-oriented review rather than a systematic review. We prioritised four types of evidence: (i) systematic reviews and meta-analyses of heat and maternal, fetal and neonatal outcomes; (ii) multi-country and multi-site epidemiological studies from low- and middle-income countries; (iii) qualitative, ethnographic and co-design studies that document how pregnant women, families and health workers experience and respond to heat, with emphasis on Burkina Faso, Kenya and India; and (iv) policy and programme documents, including reviews of heat-health action plans, Indian national and state guidance, and recent normative products from United Nations agencies. Evidence was included when it addressed pregnancy, the postpartum period or the neonatal period directly, or when it described heat adaptation measures that could be adapted to these groups.

We use "maternal and newborn heat protection" to mean the set of clinical, community, structural and governance measures that reduce heat exposure, heat strain and heat-related harm among women during pregnancy and the postpartum period and among infants in the first 28 days of life. We use "heat-health action plan" for any formal plan that sets out preparedness, warning and response measures to reduce the health effects of heat at national, subnational or city level. The proposals in Sections 4 to 6 are a synthesis of the evidence reviewed and the authors' judgement; where they go beyond published evidence, we say so.

## 3 The Evidence Gap: Why Maternal and Neonatal Heat Vulnerability Demands Urgent Attention

### 3.1 Physiological and Epidemiological Vulnerability of Pregnant Women and Neonates to Heat

**Physiological mechanisms.** Pregnancy changes the body's heat balance in ways that narrow the margin for coping with hot conditions. Metabolic heat production rises as the fetus and placenta grow, the fetus itself generates heat that must be dissipated through the mother, and the increase in body mass reduces the ratio of skin surface to body volume through which heat is lost. Cardiovascular adaptations, including expanded plasma volume and higher cardiac output, help the pregnant woman to thermoregulate under ordinary conditions. An expert review concluded that pregnant women can usually thermoregulate appropriately, but that under extreme heat several harmful processes may occur: reduced placental blood flow as blood is redirected to the skin, dehydration, and an inflammatory response that may trigger preterm labour (Samuels et al., 2022). Dehydration also reduces circulating volume and can contribute to haemoconcentration, while cardiovascular strain may interact with hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (Chersich et al., 2023; Kuehn & McCormick, 2017).

Direct physiological measurement in low-resource settings supports these mechanisms. In a cohort of pregnant subsistence farmers in The Gambia, environmental heat stress during ordinary agricultural work was associated with maternal heat strain and with signs of fetal strain, including changes in fetal heart rate and umbilical blood flow (Bonell et al., 2022). The study is important because it shows that the relevant exposure is not only the heatwave recorded at a weather station but also the daily heat of physical labour performed by women who cannot stop working during pregnancy.

Newborns face a different set of constraints. Their thermoregulatory systems are immature, they have a large surface area relative to body mass, their capacity to sweat is limited, and they lose fluid rapidly. Above all, they depend entirely on caregivers to move them out of the heat, to feed them and to keep them hydrated. A systematic review of ambient temperature and infant health found evidence that high temperatures are associated with adverse infant outcomes, and noted that the evidence base from the hottest and poorest settings remains thin (Lakhoo et al., 2022). **Epidemiological evidence.** The epidemiological literature has grown rapidly over the past decade. An early global meta-analysis found that the odds of preterm birth rose by about 5% for each 1 °C increase in temperature and by 16% during heatwaves, with a similar per-degree increase for stillbirth (Chersich et al., 2020). A larger review of 198 studies across 66 countries confirmed the association with preterm birth, reporting odds ratios of 1.04 per 1 °C and 1.26 during heatwaves, and also found associations with stillbirth, obstetric complications, congenital anomalies and gestational diabetes (Lakhoo et al., 2025). The same review noted that 63% of studies came from high-income countries, which means that the populations most exposed to heat are the least studied.

Evidence from low- and middle-income settings is now emerging and points in the same direction. An analysis of Demographic and Health Survey data from 14 lower-middle-income countries found that higher maximum temperatures and a narrower day–night temperature range in the last week before birth increased the risk of preterm birth and stillbirth (McElroy et al., 2022). Using survey data from 29 low- and middle-income countries for 2001–2019, Dimitrova et al. (2024) estimated that 4.3% of neonatal deaths were associated with non-optimal temperatures and that climate change was responsible for about one-third (32%) of heat-related neonatal deaths. In sub-Saharan Africa, a case–crossover study of 138,015 singleton births in 16 hospitals in Benin, Malawi, Tanzania and Uganda found that an increase in mean temperature from the 75th to the 99th percentile during the week before childbirth was associated with 34% higher odds of perinatal death (Hanson et al., 2024). Almost half of the stillbirths in that study occurred during labour, which points directly to the quality and conditions of intrapartum care as a target for prevention.

India provides complementary evidence on occupational exposure. A prospective cohort of 800 pregnant women doing moderate to heavy physical work in Tamil Nadu found that 47% were exposed to workplace heat above safe thresholds. Heat-exposed women had more than twice the odds of miscarriage and of any adverse pregnancy outcome compared with unexposed women (Rekha et al., 2024). **Effects on health services.** Heat also acts through the health system. In Burkina Faso, ethnographic work showed that heat affected pregnant women's physical and mental well-being, their ability to work and their relationships with partners and families, and that some customs, such as keeping a newborn indoors for the first 40 days, can increase exposure (Kadio et al., 2024). A companion study of maternal and neonatal health care in the same setting examined how heat affects the availability and quality of services (Kouanda et al., 2026). Stakeholders interviewed in this programme of research reported that extreme heat strained relationships between caregivers and patients, particularly in facilities with inadequate cooling or cramped spaces (Center for Studies in Demography and Ecology, 2019). Women and health workers both experience hot facilities as places of discomfort, and that experience shapes whether and where women seek care.

These risks are not distributed evenly. Women who are poor, who do physical work outdoors, who live in poorly ventilated housing, or who lack reliable water and electricity face more exposure and have fewer ways to reduce it. Adolescent mothers and women with chronic hypertension or diabetes face higher clinical risk. Heat therefore compounds existing inequities in maternal and newborn health in low-resource settings, which already account for the great majority of maternal deaths, stillbirths and neonatal deaths worldwide (Roos et al., 2021).

### 3.2 The Invisibility of Mothers and Newborns in Heat-Health Action Plans

The strength of the epidemiological evidence contrasts with the weak position of mothers and newborns in heat policy. A scoping review and content analysis of heat-health action plans found that most plans named children as a key population (83%), but only 52% named pregnant individuals, 39% named newborns and 14% named postpartum or breastfeeding individuals (Czerniewska et al., 2025). Where plans did include activities for maternal, newborn and child health, the most common category was information, education and awareness raising (77%), followed by improving care in health services or school settings (59%). The authors concluded that plans need targeted strategies for these groups and that structural and policy changes are needed alongside behaviour-change messages (Czerniewska et al., 2025).

The intervention literature shows a similar pattern. A mapping of interventions to reduce the effects of air pollution and extreme heat on maternal, newborn and child health identified 76 items, of which 57 addressed air pollution, 18 addressed extreme heat and one addressed both (Strong et al., 2025). Most interventions were from the Global North. The settings where heat risk to mothers and newborns is greatest therefore have the least evidence on what works. Both findings are shown in Figure 2.

Several mechanisms explain why this invisibility persists. The first is institutional. Heat-health planning is usually led by disaster management agencies, environmental health units or public health surveillance programmes, while maternal and newborn health sits in separate reproductive and child health departments with their own budgets, indicators and reporting lines. Neither side has a clear mandate to bring the two together. The second is clinical. Most obstetric and neonatal guidelines say little about heat, so frontline health workers have no standard protocol for screening, counselling or managing heat illness in pregnancy. Where national heat-illness guidance exists, as in India, it may include a section on pregnant women, but it is aimed at general medical officers rather than midwives and obstetric teams (National Centre for Disease Control, n.d.). The third is conceptual. Planners often assume that general public warnings are enough, but generic advice to stay indoors and drink water does not help a woman who must work, fetch water or travel to a clinic in the afternoon heat. Nor does it tell health workers what to do differently for her. The fourth is informational. Routine health information systems seldom link birth outcomes to temperature data, so the burden remains statistically invisible to the managers who allocate resources.

The cost of this invisibility is high. Antenatal care is one of the most widely used preventive services in low- and middle-income countries, and community health workers in India and many African countries already visit pregnant women at home. These platforms reach women repeatedly over several months, and they could deliver heat protection at low marginal cost. Leaving them out of heat planning is a missed opportunity for prevention whose consequences extend across generations.

## 4 From Evidence to Action: Operationalising Maternal Heat Protection within Health Systems

This section moves from diagnosis to prescription. We propose an integrated preventive model in which a heat-health early warning system feeds alerts into four contact points along the continuum of care: antenatal care, community and household outreach, labour and delivery, and postnatal and newborn care (Figure 3). Each contact point has a defined set of heat-protective actions. The model rests on four health-system enablers: a trained workforce, heat-resilient facilities, data and surveillance, and financing and social protection. It adapts the adaptation framework proposed by Chersich et al. (2023), which groups responses into behaviour change, health-system interventions, built-environment and cooling solutions, and structural, policy and financing measures.

### 4.1 Climate-Responsive Antenatal Care and Health Facility Readiness

**Antenatal care as a delivery platform.** Antenatal contacts provide repeated opportunities to identify women at high heat risk and to prepare them for the hot season. Heat-risk screening can be added to routine history-taking with a few questions: the woman's occupation and working hours, the type of housing and roof, access to drinking water, electricity and a fan, the distance to the facility, and whether she has hypertension, diabetes or a history of preterm birth. Women who are adolescents, who work outdoors or in hot indoor settings, or who have hypertensive disorders can be flagged for closer follow-up during heat alerts.

Counselling should be practical and specific. It should explain the warning signs of heat illness in pregnancy (dizziness, headache, confusion, reduced fetal movements, contractions), the importance of drinking water regularly, how to rest and cool down during the hottest hours, and when to seek care. In Kenya, pregnant women described symptoms that match heat exhaustion, including dizziness, fatigue, dehydration, insomnia and irritability, and some interpreted heat-related palpitations as a sign of high blood pressure (Scorgie et al., 2023). Counselling that names these experiences and explains what they mean is more likely to be understood and acted upon than generic advice. Messages should be developed with communities. A co-design workshop in Kilifi, Kenya, showed that women, family members, community leaders and health workers can identify locally feasible interventions when given the space to do so (Lusambili et al., 2023).

Antenatal registration can also serve as the entry point for early warning. When a woman registers, her mobile number, or that of a family member, can be added to a list that receives heat alerts and advice. Where mobile access is limited, the antenatal register gives community health workers a list of pregnant women to visit when an alert is issued.

The current WHO antenatal care model recommends a minimum of eight contacts during pregnancy (World Health Organization, 2016). In much of South Asia and the Sahel, several of these contacts fall in the hot season for most women, so the schedule already provides the repeated touchpoints that heat protection needs. The first contact can be used for screening and enrolment, contacts in the months before the hot season for preparedness counselling, and contacts during the hot season for checking symptoms, blood pressure and hydration. Heat-related content therefore does not require new visits. It requires a few additional minutes at visits that already take place, a short section on the antenatal card, and training so that midwives and nurses feel confident giving the advice.

**Facility readiness.** Health facilities are themselves places of heat exposure. Labour can last many hours, and women in labour often cannot leave the room, drink freely or control ventilation. The Burkina Faso research highlights how heat in facilities affects the experience of care and relationships between patients and providers (Center for Studies in Demography and Ecology, 2019; Kouanda et al., 2026). We argue that facility readiness standards for maternal care should include heat explicitly. Minimum standards should cover shaded waiting areas, safe drinking water for women and companions, cross-ventilation or fans in antenatal, labour and postnatal rooms, passive cooling of roofs and walls, and back-up power for fans and essential equipment. Many of these measures are low cost. Reflective roof coatings, shading and ventilation can reduce indoor temperatures without air conditioning (Jay et al., 2021).

**Clinical protocols and rapid cooling.** India's response to severe heatwaves provides a model that can be adapted for obstetric care. In June 2024 the Union Ministry of Health and Family Welfare asked states to assess facility preparedness, check ambulance availability and set up dedicated heat stroke rooms in health facilities (The Economic Times, 2024b). In Rajasthan, where temperatures regularly exceed 45 °C, the state health department cancelled leave for doctors, nurses and paramedical staff during the May 2024 heatwave and instructed hospitals to prepare for the prevention and treatment of heat stroke (The Economic Times, 2024a). In 2025 the state equipped ambulances to provide cold-water immersion cooling for heat stroke patients during transport (News18 Hindi, 2025).

These measures were designed for the general population. Their value for maternal care depends on adapting them. Heat stroke rooms should be accessible to pregnant women and staffed by personnel who can monitor the fetus during cooling. Rapid-cooling protocols should state how to position a pregnant woman to avoid compression of the vena cava, how to monitor fetal heart rate and contractions, and when to refer for obstetric assessment. Ambulance teams should be trained to recognise heat illness in pregnancy and in newborns. National training materials that already address heat illness in pregnant women can be extended for midwives and nurses (National Centre for Disease Control, n.d.).

### 4.2 Community-Level Surveillance and Targeted Outreach for High-Risk Mothers

Many of the women at highest risk from heat do not reach facilities when they need to. Distance, cost, work demands and family decisions all limit access, and heat itself makes travel harder. Community health worker networks are the most promising way to reach these women. In India, Accredited Social Health Activists (ASHAs) are already responsible for identifying pregnant women, encouraging antenatal care and institutional delivery, and making home visits after birth. Many African countries have comparable cadres, such as community health volunteers in Kenya. These workers know which households have pregnant women and newborns, and they are trusted by families.

We propose that community health workers take on three heat-specific tasks. First, during the pre-season period, they should map pregnant women and newborns in their catchment area who face high heat risk, using the same criteria as antenatal screening. Second, when a heat alert is issued, they should prioritise visits or phone calls to these households to check that the woman has water and a cool place to rest, to repeat key warning signs, and to arrange transport if needed. Third, they should record heat-related symptoms and referrals in a simple format so that the data can be aggregated at the district level. These tasks fit the existing work of community health workers, but they require training, clear protocols, and recognition that alert days increase workload.

India's experience in 2024 shows that health departments can place large workforces on a heat footing at short notice: staff leave was cancelled, facilities were told to prepare for heat stroke, and states were asked to review the readiness of every district (The Economic Times, 2024a, 2024b). Community health workers are a natural extension of this surge capacity, because they are already present in every village and urban ward. The opportunity lies in giving them a defined maternal and newborn component rather than leaving them to deliver general messages. This proposal is an extrapolation from existing practice and should be tested through implementation research (Section 6.2).

Heat early warning systems are the trigger for this outreach. India's city and state heat action plans, beginning with Ahmedabad in 2013, link meteorological forecasts to colour-coded alerts and predefined actions by different agencies (Knowlton et al., 2014). An evaluation of the Ahmedabad plan suggested that all-cause mortality during hot periods fell after implementation (Hess et al., 2018). Extending such systems to maternal and newborn health would mean adding pregnant women and newborns to the list of groups that receive targeted actions, routing alerts to antenatal clinics and community health workers, and sending messages to women enrolled through antenatal registration.

Digital channels have limits. In many low-income households women have less access to mobile phones than men, and phones may be shared or controlled by others. Literacy, language and battery charging can also be barriers. Alerts should therefore be delivered through several channels at once: text or voice messages where feasible, community health worker visits, women's groups, local radio and announcements at village meetings. The aim is to ensure that a heat alert reaches the woman herself, and the people who influence her daily decisions.

The newborn needs specific attention in community outreach. Home visits in the first weeks after birth are an opportunity to check whether the baby is kept in a cool, ventilated space, whether the baby is over-wrapped, and whether breastfeeding is frequent enough to maintain hydration. Community health workers can teach families to recognise early signs of dehydration and overheating, such as fewer wet nappies, dark urine, a dry mouth, unusual sleepiness or irritability, and hot skin, and to seek care promptly. Exclusive breastfeeding usually provides enough fluid for young infants even in hot weather, so advice should encourage more frequent feeds rather than giving water, which can displace breast milk and carries a risk of contamination. Where customs keep the mother and newborn indoors for several weeks after birth (Kadio et al., 2024), the advice should focus on making that indoor space cooler, for example through ventilation, shading and moving the bed away from hot walls or roofs, rather than on challenging the custom itself.

## 5 Building Resilience from the Ground Up: Community, Cultural and Structural Dimensions

Clinical and outreach measures will only work if women and families can act on them. This section examines the sociocultural norms and material conditions that determine whether heat protection is accessible, acceptable and effective.

### 5.1 Sociocultural Practices and Barriers to Heat Adaptation Among Pregnant Women

Qualitative research from sub-Saharan Africa shows that pregnant women are acutely aware of heat and have their own ways of coping with it. In Kilifi, Kenya, women described exhaustion, sleeplessness and irritability during hot periods, and reported that they observed more miscarriages and preterm births in the heat (Scorgie et al., 2023). They used strategies such as bathing, resting in the shade and changing the timing of household tasks, but their ability to do so was limited by workload and household expectations. In Burkina Faso, women reported that heat affected their ability to work and their relationships with partners and families, and that some postpartum customs increased the risk of heat exposure for the mother and newborn (Kadio et al., 2024).

These findings suggest three implications for intervention design. First, interventions should build on protective practices that communities already use. Many cultures in India and Africa have traditions of cooling foods and drinks, afternoon rest and bathing during hot seasons. Health workers can affirm these practices where they are safe and connect them to clinical advice, for example by encouraging regular fluid intake through familiar drinks. Second, interventions should address practices that increase risk, such as confining the mother and newborn to a hot, unventilated room during the postpartum period, or over-wrapping the baby. These conversations require respect and negotiation rather than instruction. Co-design approaches in Kenya and Burkina Faso have shown that communities can propose acceptable modifications when they are involved in developing them (Lusambili et al., 2023). Third, interventions must recognise gendered labour. Pregnant women in low-income households often continue fetching water, cooking over open fires, working in fields or market stalls and caring for children throughout pregnancy. Advice to rest during the hottest hours is unrealistic unless someone else takes on these tasks.

Family decision-makers play a decisive role. In many households in India, spouses and mothers-in-law influence whether a pregnant woman can rest, what she eats and drinks, whether she can spend money on transport, and when she goes to a health facility. In parts of sub-Saharan Africa, partners and older female relatives play similar roles. Heat protection messages should therefore be directed to these family members as well as to the woman herself. Antenatal care visits that include partners, community health worker visits that involve the household, and women's groups that include older women are all ways of reaching them. The goal is to make heat protection a household responsibility.

### 5.2 Structural Interventions: Cooling Access, Housing and Financial Protection

Behavioural advice cannot overcome a lack of material resources. A woman cannot keep cool in a tin-roofed house with no electricity, or rest during a heatwave if missing a day's work means her family will not eat. Structural interventions address these conditions directly.

**Cooling and housing.** Simple changes to the built environment can reduce indoor temperatures. Reflective or white roof coatings, shading, improved ventilation and the use of building materials that store less heat can lower indoor heat exposure, and electric fans can help when temperatures are not extreme and humidity allows sweat to evaporate (Jay et al., 2021). Urban measures such as tree planting and shaded public spaces reduce exposure for women who travel on foot. These interventions are usually planned at the household, neighbourhood or city level, without reference to pregnancy. Linking them to maternal health programmes would allow them to be targeted. For example, households with a registered pregnant woman or newborn could be prioritised in cool-roof programmes, housing upgrades or the distribution of fans in the hottest districts. Health facilities should be early beneficiaries of such programmes, since cooling a labour ward protects many women each year.

**Financial protection.** Heat imposes direct and indirect costs on families. Buying a fan or paying for electricity costs money, transport to a facility in the heat may require a hired vehicle, and lost working days reduce income. These costs fall hardest on women in the informal economy. Innovations in social protection are beginning to address them. In India, the Self-Employed Women's Association (SEWA) and partners launched a programme in 2024 that combines parametric heat insurance with cash assistance for women informal workers. The programme pays out automatically when temperatures exceed preset thresholds (Climate Resilience for All, 2024). Following the May 2024 heatwave, payouts reached more than 46,000 women (Swiss Re, 2025). In the pilot design, beneficiaries received a cash payment when temperatures reached 40 °C, with larger payments at higher thresholds (The Economic Times, 2024c).

These schemes were not designed specifically for pregnant women, but they offer a model. A heat-triggered payment to a pregnant woman could allow her to stop working on dangerous days, buy water or ice, or pay for transport to an antenatal visit. Existing maternity benefit schemes, such as conditional cash transfers for antenatal care and institutional delivery, could include a heat supplement during the hot season, or could release payments earlier in pregnancy in high-heat districts. Such adaptations would need careful evaluation, but they would link social protection directly to the period of greatest biological risk.

Without attention to these structural determinants, clinical and behavioural measures will remain out of reach for the poorest and most vulnerable mothers. The four categories of the adaptation framework proposed by Chersich et al. (2023) need to be pursued together, because counselling without cooling or income protection will widen inequities rather than reduce them.

## 6 Governance, Policy and the Path Forward: Institutionalising Maternal Heat Protection

Isolated projects can show what is possible, but sustained protection requires institutional change. This section proposes governance reforms and a monitoring framework, summarised in Figure 4.

### 6.1 Integrating Maternal Health into Heat Action Plans and Climate-Health Governance

**Mandatory inclusion.** The first reform is simple. Every national and subnational heat-health action plan should name pregnant women, postpartum and breastfeeding women, and newborns as priority groups, and attach specific actions, responsible agencies, indicators and budget lines to each. The evidence that only half of plans name pregnant individuals, and fewer name newborns, shows that this cannot be assumed (Czerniewska et al., 2025). Inclusion should go beyond awareness messages to cover service delivery, facility standards and structural measures.

**Intersectoral coordination.** India offers a governance model that can be adapted. Its National Action Plan on Climate Change and Human Health provides for multisectoral task forces on climate change and health at national and state levels and for state action plans that include heat-related illness (Ministry of Health and Family Welfare, 2019). These structures bring together health, meteorology, disaster management, labour, water and urban development departments. They rarely include maternal and newborn health programme managers, obstetric and paediatric professional bodies, or reproductive health advocates. We propose that maternal and newborn health programmes hold a formal seat on these task forces, that state heat-health action plans include a maternal and newborn chapter, and that district committees include the officer responsible for reproductive and child health. Similar arrangements can be made in African countries through national climate-health platforms and reproductive health technical working groups.

**Alert thresholds.** Heat alerts are usually triggered by thresholds derived from the relationship between temperature and all-cause mortality in the general population, which is dominated by older adults. These thresholds may be too high for pregnancy. In the African hospital study, the excess risk of perinatal death was associated with temperatures above the 75th percentile of the local distribution in the week before birth, not only with rare extremes (Hanson et al., 2024), and the multi-country analysis found that heat in the final week before birth mattered most for preterm birth and stillbirth (McElroy et al., 2022). We therefore suggest that heat-health action plans consider a lower or earlier trigger for maternal and newborn actions, such as alert-day visits to high-risk women and checks of labour-ward cooling, than for general public warnings. Setting these thresholds locally will require joint analysis by meteorological services and maternal health programmes, using linked temperature and birth outcome data where available.

**Clinical guidelines.** Heat-specific clinical guidance for obstetric and neonatal care is lacking in most settings. National guidance on heat-related illness should be complemented by guidance developed with obstetric, midwifery and paediatric professional bodies. Such guidance should cover heat-risk assessment in antenatal care, management of heat illness in pregnancy, intrapartum care during heat events, neonatal thermal care in hot conditions, and facility standards for cooling and water. Professional associations can also advocate for these changes and include heat in pre-service and in-service training curricula.

**Financing.** Governance reforms need dedicated funding. Heat-health action plans should include budget lines for maternal and newborn measures, such as facility cooling upgrades, community health worker training and alert-day visits. Climate adaptation finance, including national adaptation plans and international climate funds, should recognise maternal and newborn health facilities as priority infrastructure.

### 6.2 Research Priorities and a Monitoring Framework for Maternal Heat Resilience

**Research priorities.** The evidence base on interventions is thin. Only 19 of the 76 items identified in a recent mapping addressed extreme heat, and most interventions came from high-income countries (Strong et al., 2025). Four research priorities follow. First, implementation research is needed to test the feasibility, acceptability, cost and effectiveness of heat-adapted antenatal care, community health worker outreach and facility cooling in high-burden settings in India and sub-Saharan Africa. Second, studies should evaluate heat-triggered financial protection for pregnant women, including whether payments change work behaviour, care-seeking and birth outcomes. Third, physiological research should define safe heat exposure thresholds for pregnant women doing physical work, building on cohort studies in The Gambia and Tamil Nadu (Bonell et al., 2022; Rekha et al., 2024). Fourth, health information research should develop methods to link routine birth and neonatal outcome data with temperature data, so that heat-related harm can be monitored in real time.

**A monitoring framework.** Progress cannot be measured without indicators. In October 2026, WHO, the World Meteorological Organization, UNICEF and UNFPA published the first set of priority indicators to monitor the impact of heat exposure on maternal, newborn and child health (World Health Organization et al., 2026). The core indicators are preterm birth and outpatient attendance, with additional indicators including hypertension in pregnancy, late stillbirths in facilities, low birth weight, under-five mortality in facilities and primary school attendance. These indicators measure outcomes. Health systems also need process and readiness indicators that show whether protective measures are being delivered. We propose a compact set of facility and community indicators that complements the international set (Table 4). Indicators should be collected each hot season and disaggregated by income, caste or ethnicity, age and urban or rural residence, so that inequities are visible.

## 7 Conclusion

The evidence reviewed in this chapter supports three conclusions. First, pregnant women, fetuses and newborns are a distinct heat-vulnerable population. Heat raises the risk of preterm birth, stillbirth and perinatal death, and these risks are concentrated in the low- and middle-income settings where heat exposure is highest and protective resources are fewest. Second, existing heat-health action plans and adaptation programmes largely overlook this population, because of siloed governance, absent clinical guidance, reliance on generic warnings and gaps in data. Third, health systems already have the platforms needed to protect mothers and newborns. Antenatal care, community health workers, labour wards and postnatal visits reach women repeatedly during the period of greatest risk, and they can deliver heat-risk screening, targeted alerts and clinical cooling at modest cost.

Turning these platforms into a protective system requires action at every level. Facilities need cooling, water and protocols. Community health workers need training and alert-triggered tasks. Families need culturally grounded advice that engages the people who make household decisions. The poorest women need cooling access and income protection so that they can follow the advice they receive. Governments need to name mothers and newborns in heat-health action plans, give maternal health programmes a seat in climate-health governance, fund the measures, and monitor whether they reach the women who need them most.

Climate-ready health care promises to protect people from a hotter world while reducing inequity. Mothers and newborns provide a clear test of that promise. They are biologically sensitive, socially constrained and, in many places, already underserved. If health systems can protect them from heat, the same approaches will strengthen protection for everyone else.

This review has limitations. It is a narrative synthesis rather than a systematic review, so the selection of studies reflects the authors' judgement and may omit relevant evidence, particularly grey literature and studies published in languages other than English and French. The epidemiological studies reviewed are observational and use different exposure metrics, heat thresholds and outcome definitions, which limits direct comparison. Some of the evidence on Indian heat response comes from news reports and government statements rather than formal evaluations. Finally, the integrated model, the governance tiers and the indicator set proposed here are informed by evidence but have not been tested as a package. They should be read as a framework for implementation research and policy dialogue, to be adapted and evaluated in specific settings.
