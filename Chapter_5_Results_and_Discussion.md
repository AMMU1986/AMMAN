# Chapter 5: Results and Discussion

## 5.1. Characterization of Physicochemical & Thermophysical Properties of SAW Fluxes

### 5.1.1. Development and Analysis of Physicochemical and Thermophysical Responses for Fluxes

In the present study, 25 submerged arc welding fluxes were systematically designed and characterized by Design of Experiments (DoE) approach with fixed CaO content addition using a ternary diagram of SiO₂–TiO₂–CaO–BaO–MnO. In order to carefully investigate the bulk density, thermal properties and phase structure/composition as a function of SiO₂, TiO₂, CaF₂, BaO and MnO a DOE approach was used. The coordination between the thermal properties, structural properties and phase behaviour of 25 formulated fluxes were determined based on detailed XRD and FTIR results.

Although SAW has been used in marine/offshore construction, the literature mostly deals with the conventional CaO–SiO₂–CaF₂ or Al₂O₃-based flux system. The research into multi-component fluxes (including BaO and MnO) along with the correlation of thermophysical properties (thermal conductivity, specific heat, diffusivity) and phase chemistry (XRD) and molecular structure (FTIR) of the investigated fluxes is limited. Furthermore, the study of the presence of sustainable red ochre in the arc-stabilizing additives of SAW fluxes has not been carried out yet. The present work aims to fill these gaps by establishing a structure–property–performance relationship for the 25 fluxes developed in the quaternary system SiO₂–TiO₂–CaF₂–BaO with addition of fixed amount of CaO using a Design of Experiments (DoE) approach with red ochre.

### 5.1.2. Discussion About Physicochemical and Thermophysical Properties of Twenty Five Laboratory Developed SAW Fluxes

#### 5.1.2.1. Density, Thermal Conductivity, Thermal Diffusivity and Specific Heat Behaviour of Laboratory Developed SAW Fluxes

The entire thermophysical characterization of the 25 different submerged arc welding flux formulations showed a density of 1.40 to 1.54 g/cm³ (Figure 5.1a), which shows good correlation with the literature values of such multicomponent flux systems. Sharma et al. reported similar range of density of 1.35-1.58 g/cm³ for CaO-SiO₂-CaF₂ flux and is reported in the range of 1.42-1.56 g/cm³ in TiO₂-SiO₂-MgO systems by Kowalski and Nowak.

Maximum density values (Flux 14–Flux 16 and Flux 23–Flux 25), as predicted theoretically using rule-of-mixtures calculations for the formulations, were obtained when the high density oxides such as BaO (ρ = 5.72 g/cm³) and MnO (ρ = 5.03 g/cm³) were added. In contrast, silica-dominated (SiO₂) and fluorspar-rich (CaF₂) compositions had lower bulk densities (Flux 1 - Flux 6). This compositional-density relationship is consistent with the general compositional-density relationship in flux metallurgy as shown by Singh et al. for their similar relationship between bulk density of flux and oxide density with R² correlation coefficient values more than 0.85. The mean density value of 1.48 ± 0.04 g/cm³ is an optimum one with respect to the compaction properties which is suitable for SAW application with proper performance measure like flourishing flux flow and arc coverage. This is the optimum value suggested by Kumar et al. who observed that the SAW flux in the range of 1.4–1.6 g/cm³ of density exhibited the best arc stability and slag detachability. The rather close density structure (coefficient of variation = 2.7%) points towards low porosity and high manufacturing reproducibility, owed to the uniform addition of CaO binder (8%) when undergoing agglomeration.

The outcome of the thermal conductivity (Figure 5.1b) showed that the range of materials was 0.34 to 0.52 W/m·K and an indication of compositional dependence was apparent, in line with the known structure-property relationships within ceramic flux systems. The values obtained are comparable with the values reported in literature, Sharma and Chhibber reported thermal conductivity of similar agglomerated flux composites as 0.31–0.48 W/m·K, Kumar et al. reported thermal conductivity of TiO₂-SiO₂ as 0.38–0.55 W/m·K. Strong correlation between flux density and thermal conductivity suggests the processes of heat transfer in the heterogeneous oxides. High concentrations of the metallic oxides (MnO, BaO, and TiO₂) in dense fluxes always had high thermal conduction properties with thermal conductivity values enhanced by a maximum of 53% over silicate-based formulations. This improvement is caused by improved phonon transport properties of crystalline metallic oxide phases to amorphous silicate networks.

However, silica-based compounds (Flux 1–Flux 6) exhibited insulating properties having lower values of thermal conductivity, as confirmed by the known lower values of thermal conductivity of amorphous SiO₂ (0.1–0.2 W/m·K). This is a compositional effect of which Kang and Morita have studied extensively and shown that values of thermal conductivity reduce drastically with increases in SiO₂ content especially when the ratio of the CaO/SiO₂ decreases below one.

*Fig. 5.1: Variation of thermophysical properties of flux number with (a) density, (b) thermal conductivity, (c) specific heat, and (d) thermal diffusivity.*

Measurements of specific heat capacity between 0.902 and 1.192 MJ/m³·K of flux formulation (Figure 5.1c), of which Flux 25 had the highest specific heat capacity of 1.28 MJ/m³·K that was the highest in the CaF₂ and MnO concentration. SAW applications with high specific heat capacity have high metallurgical benefits in that it helps in greater thermal energy capture prior to an increase in temperature. This property is important for maintaining the arc stability and controlling the cooling speed of the weld pool which is crucial for eliminating thermal shock and the uniform slag layer structure. The specific heat density of CaF₂ follows the general thermodynamic rules that a lattice with more vibrational modes has a higher heat capacity. Thermal diffusivity measurements varied between 0.202 and 0.351 mm²/s (Figure 5.1d). The minimum values of thermal diffusivity were found for the fluxes with highest level of silica and fluorspar (0.202–0.215 mm²/s) which is the best thermal insulation capacity required to maintain the weld pool protection. The values obtained are similar to the experiments carried out by Negi and Chattopadhyaya on silicate dominated SAW fluxes where they have reported thermal diffusivities in the range of 0.18–0.23 mm²/s. Compositions containing a high percentage of BaO or MnO (Flux 8 and Flux 20) and hence having higher thermal transport properties had higher rate of heat propagation (0.335–0.351 mm²/s). The high thermal diffusivity slows down the cooling speed and increases ductility, while the low thermal diffusivity speeds up the cooling speed and increases smooth grain structures.

#### 5.1.2.2. Phase Analysis of Laboratory Developed SAW Fluxes

X-Ray diffraction of four typical flux mixtures have demonstrated the clearance of the crystalline phase assemblage which provides in-depth data of the thermal conduct and metallurgical workability during the welding process (Figure 5.2). In Figure 5.2a, Flux 2, containing 35% CaF₂ and 25% MnO (Table 4.2), exhibits strong, sharp fluorite reflections at 2θ = 28.3°, 32.2°, and 47.0°, corresponding to the (111), (200) and (220) planes respectively. The high intensity and categorically small full width at half maximum (FWHM), means high crystallinity and a large crystallite size, which suggests that appropriate thermal treatment was applied during the preparation of the flux, allowing for good formation of fluorite crystal growth. The lower CaF₂ content (31.3%) (Table 4.2) in Flux 20 leads to lower intensity peaks of fluorite, and new peaks of MnO at 30.1° and 50.5°. The negative correlation between the peak of fluorite and MnO suggests the crystallization effect as the amount of MnO added to the specimen may have also promoted the formation of other crystal phases. The intermediate phase assemblage for Flux 12 shows strong fluorite reflections and well defined rutile peaks at 27.4°, 36.1° and 54.4° and discrete MnO signatures. In comparison with Flux 2, the intensity ratio of rutile/fluorite is higher in Flux 12, demonstrating better titania retention in a crystalline state, pertinent to arc stabilization.

#### 5.1.2.3. Structural Behaviour of Laboratory Developed SAW Fluxes

##### 5.1.2.3.1. Structure–Property Correlations

The results of a XRD can be related to the thermophysical properties described in Section 5.1.2.1. In particular, the degree of crystallinity of fluorite (Flux 2 and 16) led to a decrease in the value of thermal diffusivity (0.215-0.245 mm²/s) compared to the other MnO-rich Flux 20 (0.335 mm²/s). This is in line with the phonon scattering effect observed in fluorite structured compounds, as vacancies of the fluorine atoms break the propagation of heat-carrying phonons. Higher thermal diffusivity of the MnO rich compositions on the other hand can be attributed because the oxidation state of MnO is metallic which allows electronic thermal conduction.

In addition, the qualitative assessment of degree of crystallinity made from the sharpness and intensity of peaks in the XRD profiles is inversely proportional to the basicity index. The results showed that the diffraction peaks were generally stronger and sharper in the higher basicity fluxes (such as Flux 2 and 13) as compared to lower basicity fluxes (such as Flux 11 and 18) which showed broader and lower intensities of diffraction peaks, corresponding to higher amorphous content. This relationship is mechanistically feasible because of the ability of basic oxides (CaO, BaO) and CaF₂ to be in a unique crystalline phase and SiO₂ to form amorphous glassy networks.

##### 5.1.2.3.2. FTIR Spectral Interpretation & Network Depolymerization

FTIR spectra of the 8 flux samples are presented in Figure 5.3 and the spectra are analysed to provide a molecular level understanding of the structural features. Similar to oxide-based welding flux systems the spectra are interpreted. Typical absorption bands characteristic of the vibrations in the silicate network, hydroxyl groups and metal-oxygen bonds are found for all of the samples. The most notable peak on all the spectra is a broad and intense line around 1100 cm⁻¹, typical of the asymmetric Si-O-Si stretching in the silicate tetrahedra. It is noticed that in a systematic manner this is shifted towards lower wavenumbers in Flux 14, 19, 21, 25 (set B) as compared to the other fluxes (set A: Flux 1, 6, 7, 11). There was an average shift in peak position from 1105 cm⁻¹ for set A to 1085 cm⁻¹ for set B. The increased depolymerization of silicate network is quantitatively analyzed and described by the fact that the number of Si-O-Si bonds is proportional to the degree of Polymerization as a consequence of this red shift. Lower wavenumbers indicate a higher concentration of non-bridging oxygen atoms (NBOs) which are the effect of the network modification embedded in it by the basic oxides (BaO, MnO, CaO). The non-bridging oxygen (Si-O) stretching vibrations are assigned to a shoulder at 950 cm⁻¹, which is more intense in the samples from set B. The intensity ratio I₉₅₀/I₁₁₀₀ may be used as a semi-quantitative measure to quantify the depolymerization in the networks. The average calculated ratio is in the range 0.15–0.25 in set A, and 0.30–0.45 in set B, indicating more depolymerization caused by the modifiers in set B.

##### 5.1.2.3.3. Correlation with Thermal and Anticipated Slag Behavior

The depolymerization index, obtained from FTIR confirms the trend of positive correlation with the specific heat capacity values obtained from Section 5.1.2.1. The specific heat capacities of set B with higher ratio of I₉₅₀/I₁₁₀₀ were in the range of 1.05–1.19 MJ/m³·K, and set A were in the range 0.90–0.98 MJ/m³·K. The correlation makes good sense on a mechanical ground since the depolymerised silicate networks seem to have more vibration modes and higher configurational entropy than do more polymerised species, thereby providing a better capacity for absorbing heat.

*Figure 5.3: FTIR spectra of samples Flux 1, Flux 6, Flux 7, Flux 11 (A) and Flux 14, Flux 19, Flux 21, Flux 25 (B) showing percent transmittance versus wavenumber (4000–500 cm⁻¹). Notable absorption differences observed in fingerprint and functional group regions.*

It has been found that the action of slag, and its relation to the extent of network depolymerization, have been well established in the previous literature and that an increase in the depolymerization of the network will result in a decrease in slag viscosity and in a decrease of slag melting temperature. Hence based on the FTIR evidence, it may be reasonably assumed that greater fluidity would be available in the formulations set B in particular the formulation Flux 21 and Flux 25 in comparison with the formulations set A and thus lower viscosity of the slags would be expected. In the present investigation however, high temperature viscosity and thermal analysis experiments were not directly carried out to verify this.

##### 5.1.2.3.4. Hydroxyl and Carbonate Species

The O-H stretching bands are found in all the spectra and they are of broad band shape with their centre around 3400 cm⁻¹, the intensity of which varies between the different samples (Figure 5.3). These hydroxyl groups are likely due to moisture on the surface and/or structural water in the binder (potassium silicate solution) which can influence the hydrogen content of the weld metal. Higher the hydroxyl band in the set A samples, the more likely for moisture retention, thus potentially higher the possibility for hydrogen induced cracking.

The systematic FTIR analysis of welding flux systems was carried out by Park and Min and it has been shown that the Si-OH bending vibrations in the 950–930 cm⁻¹ region are linearly related to the hydrogen solubility in molten slags. The Complementary Si-O-Si bending complexes between 400 and 600 cm⁻¹ indicate the presence of ring structures and three-dimensional network connectivity of the silicate structure; the intensity and structure of these bands are quantitative measures of the extent of polymerization of the network structure, and the influence of the modifier content.

These characteristic lattice vibration bands of TiO₂ can be adjusted in strength and spectral properties by varying the amount of the lower concentration phase. The long vibration envelope of TiO is reminiscent of the complex environments of coordination typical of the multicomponent flux systems. The presence of the MnO in the structure causes further lattice modes in the 700–400 cm⁻¹ region overlapping the Ti-O and Si-O-Si band; the characteristics increase with increasing amount of MnO in the structure, indicating changes in the oxidation state. Kim et al. further suggested that the effects of MnO addition are manifested by free oxygen ions, which cause the changes in the silicate network, and by its direct effects on the thermal and hydrogen dissolution behavior.

##### 5.1.2.3.5. Scope of Physicochemical and Thermophysical Properties Characterization

This study successfully sets up the quantitative relationships between the flux composition and the measured physical and thermophysical properties by using the systematic approach. The data presented here provide good scientific basis for the development of fluxes having desired characteristics such as bulk density, thermal conductivity and specific heat. The resultant slag behavior can be hypothesized based on the structural evidence obtained from XRD and FTIR measurements, such as the ratio of I₉₅₀/I₁₁₀₀ which is a good indicator of the fluidity of the silicate network in the final slag, or the crystallinity of the phases such as fluorite which can influence the thermal transport of the resultant slag.

The slags viscosity, melting temperature or detachability is not directly measured in the present study. Thus, claims of better slag fluidity or reductions in melting temperatures or improved arc stability as a result of certain compositional changes should be understood as inferences based on the thermophysical and structural information and not as substantiated process results. The experimental range used in this study includes room-temperature properties of as-prepared fluxes, while there were no direct measurements of the properties of the slag, slag melting temperature, detachability, and hydrogen content of weld metal. Weld metal hydrogen analysis is not the only future study needed; high-temperature viscosity measurements, differential thermal analysis (DTA) for melting behavior, slag detachment force tests and the recording of actual arcs while performing the SAW are also necessary.

## 5.2. Multi-Pass Bead on Plate Experimentation for Twenty Five Laboratory Developed SAW Fluxes

### 5.2.1. Bead Profile Analysis

After the multi-pass bead on plate experimentation, twenty-five different beads were visually examined (Figure 5.4) to observe the bead morphology, whether or not the bead was porous, and the ease of slag detachability. Bead profile analysis showed that the composition of the flux has a great influence on the weld bead geometry, surface quality and characteristics of detachment of slag. Generally, fluxes with higher basicity index gave more smooth bead profile with better slag detachability, whereas, lower basicity fluxes gave more rough surface finish with sticky slag.

Visual inspection results indicated that the bead morphology (smooth and uniform) combined with minimum porosity (no observable porosity on the bead surface) and satisfactory slag detachability (slag fully self-peels or easily removed manually) was best attained by the flux formulations F4, F6, F10, F20 and F22. Three fluxes from the basic flux system of the present were selected for further investigations based on this preliminary screening.

##### 5.2.2.1.1. Regression Models of Chemical Composition and Microhardness for Twenty Five Laboratory Developed SAW Fluxes

The least squares regression models were derived for the different values of the proportions of the weld metal constituents for the empirically determined weld metal composition. These second and third order mixture models involved linear, binary and ternary interaction terms in order to model the individual and synergistic interaction of TiO₂, SiO₂, CaF₂, BaO, CaO and MnO. The regression equations (Eqs.). The quantitative dependence of flux chemistry on the weld metal output (C, Si, Mn, P, S, Mo, Ni and Cr contents), microhardness (MH) and carbon equivalent (CE) are given by 9-18.

The regression equations of the experiments were derived with the aid of mixture design methodology. In mixture experiments, the dependent variables are subjected to a summation constraint: Σt_i = 85 wt% (Equation 5.1), where t_i is the weight fraction of SiO₂, TiO₂, CaF₂, BaO, MnO and CaO. Because of this limitation, the variables are not independent and standard polynomial regression cannot be used. The reason is that Scheffé canonical mixture models are employed in which the intercept term is incorporated in the linear term.

There are general forms of those applied in this study as:

- Linear mixture model: ŷ = Σ(β_i·t_i)
- A quadratic mixture model is a model of the form ŷ = Σβ_i·t_i + Σβ_ij·t_i·t_j.
- Special cubic mixture model: ŷ = Σβ_i·t_i + Σβ_ij·t_i·t_j + Σβ_ijk·t_i·t_j·t_k

The coefficient vector was calculated from the equation: β = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy (Equation 5.2) using the design matrix X and the vector of experimental responses y. The developed equations enable the prediction of the composition of weld metal, microhardness and carbon equivalent directly from the composition of the flux without using experiments that are trial and error.

##### 5.2.2.1.3. Discussion of Regression Analysis of Flux Components on Chemical Composition and Microhardness

The constituents of flux with a very significant synergistic effect on the chemistry of the weld metal. Free oxygen ions (O²⁻) have an important influence on the elements transfer and elemental chemistry of the weld metal during the slag-metal reactions that occur in the submerged arc welding (SAW) process. Basic compounds such as CaO, BaO and MnO of the flux, dissociate in the molten slag to produce O²⁻ ions, which are strong electron donors that alter the chemical potential of oxygen at the slag-metal interface.

```
SiO₂ (slag) ⇌ [Si] (metal) + 2[O] (metal)                                   (19)
MnO (slag) ⇌ [Mn] (metal) + [O] (metal)                                     (20)
[S] (metal) + O²⁻ (slag) ⇌ S²⁻ (slag) + [O] (metal)                         (21)
```

Compared to the work on high basicity of slag and modified oxygen potential in the weld pool the main effect coefficients of basic oxides were found to be positive, especially for basic oxides CaO on the Mn and Cr models. On the other hand, negative interaction terms of CaO·CaF₂ and BaO·SiO₂ suggest the formation of stable slag complexes which hinder the alloy transfer. The sulfur model strongly negative primary coefficients, known as the desulfurizing effect of basic oxides. For medium concentrations, MnO has a dual behaviour, that is, it is retained in this concentration range, but when combined with CaF₂ or TiO₂ it forms slag complexes with the MnO·CaF₂ and TiO₂·MnO terms very negative, as previously reported for SAW and is expected for high oxidizing or fluoride-rich slag.

Trace (Piepel) plots are well suited to illustrate the sensitivity of each element to individual flux constituents (Figure 5.6). CaO shows the largest positive slope for microhardness, suggesting that it is the most effective individual component to enhance the microhardness, mainly by facilitating the recovery of Mn and Mo in the weld pool. The low deviation from the reference blend has a moderately positive trend in BaO, but it levels off at higher deviations and goes negative at even higher deviations, due to the negative binary interaction between BaO and SiO₂. CaF₂ always has a negative slope indicating the formation of stable slag complexes that decrease the recovery of the alloy elements, thereby decreasing hardenability.

## 5.3. Corrosion Analysis & Discussion

The electrochemical corrosion behaviour of the API X70 SAW weldments were investigated to verify the impact of flux chemistry on corrosion resistance of the weld metal and heat-affected zone in simulated marine atmosphere. The potentiodynamic polarization tests were performed in 3.5 wt.% NaCl solution at room temperature using a three-electrode electrochemical cell, to mimic the aggressive marine/offshore service conditions to which pipeline weldments are subjected during service. The corrosion potential (E_corr), corrosion current density (i_corr) and corrosion rate were calculated from Tafel extrapolation of the polarization curves.

The corrosion behaviour is greatly affected by the chemistry of the weld metal, such as the percentage of Mn, Mo, Cr and Si transferred from the flux due to slag-metal reactions. Sharma and Chhibber found that the electrochemical properties of SAW weldments are directly related to the flux composition which influences the weld metal chemistry and inclusion morphology.

The following flux-dependent mechanisms are the most important in the corrosion resistance hierarchy of the tested weldments:

(i) **Chromium and Molybdenum Transfer:** Greater quantity of Cr and Mo in the weld metal increases the tendency to form stable passive films (Cr₂O₃ and MoO₃) which are more resistant to breakdown in chloride containing environments. The laboratory had fluxes that transferred significant amounts of Mo to the weld metal (F6B: 0.392%, F20B: 0.401%, F22B: 0.355%), which would give higher pitting resistance in comparison to fluxes that do not contain Mo. In the passive film, the Mo ion increases the pitting potential, making it more resistant to the localized attack by chloride.

(ii) **Sulfur and Phosphorus Removal:** The basic fluxes with high CaF₂ and CaO content can effectively remove the sulphur and phosphorus from the weld metal through the following ionic exchange reactions: [S]_metal + (O²⁻)_slag = (S²⁻)_slag + [O]_metal. The amount of sulfur is significantly lower in the laboratory flux weldments (F6B: 0.0024%, F20B: 0.0019%, F22B: 0.0011%) as compared with the commercial flux (CF: 0.0070%), which benefits the corrosion resistance of the weldments by reducing the amount of inclusion formation of MnS at grain boundaries. In chloride media, acidic local environment is created by electrochemical dissolution of MnS inclusions which are known to be preferential sites for initiation of pitting.

(iii) **Inclusion Morphology and Distribution:** The oxygen potential controlled by the flux basicity affects the size, shape and distribution of the oxide inclusions in the weld metal. Fine, dispersed inclusions (high basicity fluxes) present a lower threat of corrosion resistance than do large, angular and/or clustered inclusions (low basicity fluxes) which may dissolve preferentially, forming micro-pits.

(iv) **Microstructural Uniformity:** This is the case for the acicular ferrite dominated microstructures (promoted by basic fluxes like F6B), where the small size and random orientation of each acicular ferrite grain helps to reduce galvanic potential differences between adjacent ferrite grains, resulting in more uniform behaviour during corrosion. In contrast, microstructures with high contents of large areas of grain boundary ferrite or Widmanstatten ferrite show preferential corrosion along the ferrite-pearlite or ferrite-bainite boundaries.

It was found that the optimized composition of the low sulfur (0.0024%) with Mo (0.392%) and mainly acicular ferrite microstructure offered the most noble corrosion potential and lowest corrosion current density amongst the laboratory fluxes as indicated by the polarization results. The intermediate corrosion resistance with very low sulfur content (0.0019% for F20B and 0.0011% for F22B) but slightly lower Mn content which slightly reduced passive film stability for flux weldments. The commercial flux (CF) weldment, which has the highest Mo content (0.412%), was found to have a relatively higher sulfur level (0.0070%) that slightly balances the positive alloying effect, and thus the corrosion resistance of the weld is similar to or slightly less than that of F6B.

The polarization results in sea water (3.5 wt.% NaCl) establish a clear corrosion-resistance hierarchy among the tested weldments: **F6B > CF > F20B > F22B** (Figure 5.7a). The F6B weldment exhibits the most noble corrosion potential and the lowest corrosion current density, followed closely by the commercial flux (CF), while F20B and F22B display progressively more active potentials and higher corrosion current densities.

If there are regions of the weldment with different electrochemical potential such as HAZ, weld metal and base metal, galvanic corrosion can be initiated at these interfaces, forming corrosion cells that could cause localized material deterioration. The flux composition affects the weld metal chemistry and the weld metal microstructure, and thus is a critical factor in reducing these galvanic differences and providing the weld metal with a uniform corrosion resistance throughout the weldment. The results show that the basic fluxes developed in the laboratory can yield weldments with the corrosion resistance that is similar or superior to those produced using commercial weld fluxes, lending support to the systematic design of the flux for marine and offshore pipeline applications.

## 5.4. Selection of Adequate Flux (From Twenty Five Laboratory Developed SAW Fluxes)

### 5.4.1. Selection of Suitable Fluxes from Twenty Five Laboratory Developed SAW Fluxes

The selection of appropriate fluxes for the fabrication of final weld joint was done systematically using multi-criteria evaluation process. Following the multi-pass bead on plate experiment, twenty-five different beads were visually inspected as shown in Figure 5.8 and their bead morphology, porous or not and the detachability of the slag was noted. For further investigation, three fluxes from the present basic flux system were selected based on the preliminary screening that they demonstrated good bead morphology, minimum porosity and satisfactory slag detachability characteristics.

Therefore the fluxes of grade F6B, F20B and F22B of the basic system were selected for making the final submerged arc weld joints. The performance and characteristics of these three selected laboratory fluxes were then compared by comparing the weld joint produced by them to the weld joint produced with a commercial flux (labelled as C.F.). The compositions were developed using the extreme vertices design methodology of McLean and Anderson and selected based on the ternary equilibrium phase diagrams SiO₂-CaO-CaF₂, SiO₂-TiO₂-MnO and SiO₂-BaO-B₂O₃ (Figure 5.9).

After the formulation of flux, multi-pass bead-on-plate welding experiments were then conducted. Various mineral constituents like silica (SiO₂), titanium dioxide (TiO₂), calcium fluoride (CaF₂), barium oxide (BaO), manganese oxide (MnO) and calcium oxide (CaO) powders were used to make up the fluxes. Finally, API X70 steel base plates with a butt welding configuration with single V groove (60° included angle, 2 mm root gap) were prepared as shown in Figure 5.10 for their experimentation on butt weld joint. All the experimental welding was conducted with direct current electrode positive (DCEP) polarity at a current of 440 A, a voltage of 28 V and a welding speed of 13 inch/min with nozzle-to-tip distance of 20 mm.

### 5.4.4. Weld Impact Specimen Fractography Analysis

The fractography of the impact tested specimens of API X70 submerged arc welding (SAW) weldments suggests the complex interaction between weld flux chemistry, weld microstructures and fracture mechanisms in the parent metal (PM), heat affected zone (HAZ) and fusion zone (FZ). Systematic fractographic analysis proves that these regions show different failure modes under impact loading which is directly related to their very specific microstructural features and to the welding flux used. The highest impact toughness was observed for the parent metal (PM) with the charpy energy average at room temperature at 350 J (Fig. 5.12a) and a well-developed dimple structure and microvoid coalescence in the fracture surface.

The fusion zone has the most complicated fractographic features and has the lowest impact toughness as compared to the parent metal and HAZ region. The basic flux (F6B) (Figure 5.12b) and the commercial flux (CF) (Figure 5.12h) performed very well with an impact toughness of 171 J and 159 J respectively at room temperature, in the weld zone, fractography reveals a combination of ductile characteristics. Brittle fracture with some facet tore surfaces were observed at -55°C for both fluxes (Figure 5.12c and Figure 5.12i). In contrast, F20B and F22B fluxes cause fusion zone fracture surfaces (at room temperature and -55°C) with larger cleavage facets and smaller shear lip area, indicating that they exhibit lower fracture resistance (Figures 5.12d–g).

The HAZ has transitional fractographic behaviour between the parent metal and fusion zone. The coarse-grained HAZ (CGHAZ) region next to the fusion line exhibits the most brittle properties relating to cleavage fracture that contain martensite and austenite (M-A) constituents and large prior austenite grains. Fractographic analysis of HAZ specimens from welds made with basic fluxes (F6B) (Figures 5.12j–k) and commercial fluxes (CF) (Figures 5.12l–m), shows an improvement in toughness with an increased ductile fracture area as compared to other fluxes. The F6B flux is found to give 385 J impact energy in the HAZ at room temperature while 28 J at -55°C with fractography demonstrating intense microvoid coalescence.

The influence of inclusions on fractography of the HAZ is also quite significant as inclusions are responsible for grain boundary pinning, and microstructural refinement of the HAZ microstructure occurs due to the presence of fine-sized, well-dispersed inclusions which favour ductile fracture while large-sized, clustered inclusions coarsen the HAZ structure and promote cleavage-dominated fracture.

### 5.4.5. Weld Specimen Microhardness & Microstructure Analysis

In API X70 SAW joints, using various fluxes to assess the microhardness of the joints reveals a complex interaction between the basicity of the flux and other factors like weld-metal composition and microstructural changes due to thermal cycle that the flux causes in the parent material, weld metal and heat-affected zone (HAZ). Table 5.5 shows the microhardness of fusion zone and heat affected zone of SAW weldments made with laboratory prepared and commercial fluxes.

| Flux | Max. FZ (HV) | Min. FZ (HV) | Max. HAZ (HV) | Min. HAZ (HV) |
|------|--------------|--------------|---------------|---------------|
| F6B | 224 | 205 | 251 | 208 |
| F20B | 215 | 195 | 229 | 201 |
| F22B | 202 | 184 | 225 | 205 |

The as received API X70 steel has a fine-grained ferrite-pearlite microstructure with a microhardness range of 190–220 HV giving them a balanced strength-toughness relationship that is good enough to serve in high pressure applications. In the course of submerged arc welding the influence of the applied thermal field is manifested in the modification of the fraction phases and grain morphology within the HAZ and weld metal in the welded area and leads to the formation of typical microhardness distribution across the cross-section of the HAZ and weld metal.

The maximum microhardness observed in fusion zone for laboratory prepared and commercial flux was 233 HV and 224 HV respectively while in HAZ it was 257 HV and 251 HV, respectively. Basic prepared fluxes like F6B, F20B and F22B are likely to form more basic weld that would result in more interaction between slag and metal and reduce the number of oxygen and hydrogen atoms in weld and create more refined weld-metal microstructure, and inhibit the formation of coarse martensite and fresh bainite in the fusion zone.

The general microhardness distribution of F6B welded joints is medium and even with an HV of 205–224 in the weld metal, which can be attributed to the prevailing acicular ferrite, as well as the fine dispersed bainitic constituents (Figure 5.13b). As seen in the previous studies, it has been confirmed that an increase in the basic oxide content in the flux formulation leads to a decrease in the amount of oxygen in the weld metal and a reduction in the number of oxide inclusions that enhances the number of nucleation sites for the formation of acicular ferrite. Weld-metal hardness is averagely lower (195-215 HV for F20B, 184-202 HV for F22B) for the more CaO/CaF₂-rich formulations (F20B and F22B) as a result of better deoxidation and refinement in the weld-metal microstructure. Commercial flux (CF) exhibits good hardness value in fusion zone (210-233 HV) which is slightly higher than all the laboratory prepared flux, while in HAZ region it exhibits good hardness value (211-257 HV).

The flux system used critically controls the microstructural behavior of API X70 submerged arc welding (SAW) weldments, and the high-basic fluxes F6B, F20B and F22B produce a highly evolved and desirable microstructural evolution when compared to commercial SAW fluxes. The basicity of the flux in the fusion zone affects the oxygen potential and inclusion population. High-basicity fluxes with high content of basic oxides (CaO, MgO) to oxidative oxides (SiO₂, TiO₂) produce a low oxygen atmosphere and result in a reduction of the percentage of oxide inclusions and a formation of finely-dispersed complex inclusions, which are rich in titanium. The inclusions serve as preferred nuclei for the growth of acicular ferrite (AF), an intergranular microstructural form in the shape of fine, non-aligned laths of ferrite, which is highly resistant to cleavage fracture (as illustrated in Figure 5.13a–i).

Acicular ferrite with small amount of polygonal ferrite and bainite constitute the fusion zone of weldments produced by the use of simple fluxes like F6B, F20B, F22B and CF. Lower basicity indices of the fluxes or lower rutile content in the fluxes results in higher weld metal oxygen content leading to a coarser distribution of inclusions, and a formation of less desirable microstructures like grain boundary ferrite (GBF) and Widmanstatten ferrite (WF). The heat-affected zone (HAZ) which undergoes a complex thermal cycle without melting, has microstructural changes that show a similar sensitivity to the choice of flux, but by indirect mechanisms. In most cases the HAZ for API X70 steels consists of a number of sub-regions, including the coarse-grained HAZ (CGHAZ) adjacent to the fusion line where the highest temperature is experienced and which is susceptible to austenite grain coarsening, and the fine-grained HAZ (FGHAZ) which is normalizing temperature. The microstructure of the CGHAZ of welds made using simple fluxes like F6B, F20B, F22B and commercial flux (CF) is generally bainite and acicular ferrite.

Martensite-austenite (M-A) constituents and fine bainite developing from the rapid cooling near the fusion line are a common cause of a slight increase in hardness of the coarse-grained HAZ (CGHAZ). It is the opinion of the authors that appropriate levels of microhardness in the parent metal, weld metal and HAZ can be achieved in many situations with F6B, F20B and F22B basic fluxes as well as commercially tuned SAW fluxes, provided that the heat input, preheat and after-weld thermal regimes are maintained within an acceptable range of values that provides for the prevention of excessive hardening of the weldment or softening of random regions which potentially could lead to loss of in-service integrity of API X70 pipeline systems.

Microstructural examination showed that the basic fluxes prepared in the laboratory, especially F6B, were favouring the development of acicular ferrite, which is the most desired microstructure to allow superior toughness in the fusion zone while quenched polygonal ferrite (QPF), grain boundary ferrite (GBF) and martensite-austenite (M/A) phases developed in the heat-affected zone. The mechanical characterization showed that the impact toughness values of the F6B flux weldment were 171 J at room temperature and 21 J at -55°C in the fusion zone and 385 J at room temperature and 28 J at -55°C in the HAZ compared to similar or even higher values for the commercial flux (159 J at room temperature and 18 J at -55°C in the fusion zone and 385 J at room temperature and 28 J at -55°C in the HAZ). The distribution of the hardness was good in all the weldments, and no report on excessive hardening or unwanted softening occurred to interfere with in service performance. The basic fluxes using laboratory-made procedures, particularly F6B, demonstrated mechanical and microstructural performance that proves they could be used as possible alternatives in pipeline welding applications. The results demonstrate the strength of the application of the mixture design and ternary phase diagram based method in the realization of the flux system specific with high strength low alloy steels.
