# Chapter 5: Results and Discussion

## 5.1. Characterization of Physicochemical and Thermophysical Properties of SAW Fluxes

### 5.1.1. Development and Analysis of Physicochemical and Thermophysical Responses of the Fluxes

In the present study, 25 submerged arc welding (SAW) fluxes were systematically designed and characterized through a Design of Experiments (DoE) approach with a fixed CaO addition, using the SiO₂–TiO₂–CaF₂–BaO–MnO system. This strategy follows the flux-formulation methodology established for shielded metal arc welding (SMAW) coatings by Kumar and Chhibber (2022) and Kumar et al. (2023), who demonstrated that regression- and neural-network-based DoE models can predict the physicochemical and thermophysical responses of formulated fluxes with high fidelity. The bulk density, thermal properties, and phase structure of the 25 fluxes were investigated as functions of SiO₂, TiO₂, CaF₂, BaO, and MnO content, consistent with the multi-component flux design frameworks reported by Sharma and Chhibber (2019a, 2019b). The coordination among thermal, structural, and phase behaviour was resolved using detailed X-ray diffraction (XRD) and Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) analyses, an approach also adopted by Kim et al. (2015) for the TiO₂–MnO–SiO₂–Al₂O₃ system.

Although SAW is widely applied in marine and offshore construction, the published literature predominantly addresses conventional CaO–SiO₂–CaF₂ or Al₂O₃-based flux systems (Sharma & Chhibber, 2019c, 2019d; Jackson, 1973). Studies that couple multi-component fluxes containing BaO and MnO with a simultaneous correlation of thermophysical properties (thermal conductivity, specific heat, diffusivity), phase chemistry (XRD), and molecular structure (FTIR) remain limited (Kumar & Chhibber, 2024a; Kim et al., 2015). Furthermore, the incorporation of sustainable red ochre as an arc-stabilizing additive in SAW fluxes has not previously been reported (Zhang et al., 2022). The present work addresses these gaps by establishing a structure–property–performance relationship for the 25 fluxes developed in the SiO₂–TiO₂–CaF₂–BaO system with a fixed CaO addition, using a DoE approach that incorporates red ochre.

### 5.1.2. Physicochemical and Thermophysical Properties of the Twenty-Five Laboratory-Developed SAW Fluxes

#### 5.1.2.1. Density, Thermal Conductivity, Thermal Diffusivity, and Specific Heat Behaviour

The thermophysical characterization of the 25 SAW flux formulations yielded bulk densities of 1.40–1.54 g/cm³ (Figure 5.1a), which correlate well with values reported for comparable multi-component flux systems (Sharma & Chhibber, 2019a). Sharma and Chhibber (2019a) reported a similar density range of 1.35–1.58 g/cm³ for TiO₂–SiO₂–MgO and SiO₂–MgO–Al₂O₃ fluxes, while Sharma, Kumar, and Chhibber (2019) reported 1.42–1.56 g/cm³ for MgO–TiO₂–SiO₂ systems. The close agreement between the present measurements and these prior studies confirms that the agglomeration route and binder fraction used here produce fluxes of comparable compaction to established formulations.

The maximum densities (Flux 14–Flux 16 and Flux 23–Flux 25) were obtained when high-density oxides such as BaO (ρ = 5.72 g/cm³) and MnO (ρ = 5.03 g/cm³) were added, in agreement with the rule-of-mixtures behaviour described for agglomerated fluxes by Houldcroft (1989) and Davis and Coe (1977). Conversely, silica- and fluorspar-rich compositions (Flux 1–Flux 6) exhibited the lowest bulk densities. This compositional–density relationship is consistent with the flux-metallurgy trends reported by Singh, Khan, and Siddiquee (2013), who showed that flux bulk density scales with constituent oxide density. The mean density of 1.48 ± 0.04 g/cm³ falls within the 1.4–1.6 g/cm³ window that Kanjilal, Pal, and Majumdar (2006) associated with stable arc coverage and good slag detachability in agglomerated SAW fluxes. The low coefficient of variation (2.7%) indicates low porosity and high manufacturing reproducibility, which Bang, Park, Jung, and Lee (2009) similarly attributed to uniform binder distribution during agglomeration.

Thermal conductivity ranged from 0.34 to 0.52 W/m·K (Figure 5.1b) and displayed a clear compositional dependence, in line with the structure–property relationships reported for ceramic flux systems by Kumar et al. (2023). The present values agree closely with those of Sharma and Chhibber (2019a), who reported 0.31–0.48 W/m·K for agglomerated flux composites, and with Kanjilal et al. (2006), who reported 0.38–0.55 W/m·K for TiO₂–SiO₂-based fluxes. The strong positive correlation between flux density and thermal conductivity reflects the heat-transfer behaviour of heterogeneous oxide assemblages described by Kumar and Chhibber (2024b). Dense fluxes with high concentrations of MnO, BaO, and TiO₂ exhibited thermal-conductivity enhancements of up to 53% relative to silicate-based formulations, an effect that Callister and Rethwisch (2020) attributed to superior phonon transport in crystalline metallic-oxide phases compared with amorphous silicate networks.

Silica-rich formulations (Flux 1–Flux 6) behaved as insulators, with low thermal conductivity consistent with the intrinsically low conductivity of amorphous SiO₂ (0.1–0.2 W/m·K) noted by Callister and Rethwisch (2020). This compositional effect mirrors the observations of Jung and Min (2012) and Jung, Kim, Sohn, and Min (2010), who reported that conductivity and interfacial behaviour of calcium-silicate melts decline markedly as SiO₂ content increases and the CaO/SiO₂ ratio falls below unity.

*Figure 5.1. Variation of thermophysical properties with flux number: (a) density, (b) thermal conductivity, (c) specific heat, and (d) thermal diffusivity.*

Volumetric specific heat capacity ranged from 0.902 to 1.192 MJ/m³·K (Figure 5.1c), with Flux 25 exhibiting the highest value at the greatest CaF₂ and MnO content, consistent with the trends reported by Kanjilal et al. (2006) and Sharma and Chhibber (2019a). A high specific heat is metallurgically advantageous in SAW because it promotes greater thermal-energy storage before a temperature rise, aiding arc stability and cooling-rate control (Golovko & Potapov, 2011). Thermal diffusivity varied from 0.202 to 0.351 mm²/s (Figure 5.1d). The lowest diffusivities (0.202–0.215 mm²/s) occurred for the silica- and fluorspar-rich fluxes, providing the thermal-insulation capacity required for effective weld-pool protection (Kumar & Chhibber, 2024b). These values agree with the 0.18–0.23 mm²/s range that Sharma and Chhibber (2019b) reported for silicate-dominated SAW fluxes. Compositions rich in BaO or MnO (Flux 8 and Flux 20) showed the highest diffusivities (0.335–0.351 mm²/s). As Kou (2003) noted, higher diffusivity slows weld-metal cooling and favours ductility, whereas lower diffusivity accelerates cooling and refines grain structure.

#### 5.1.2.2. Phase Analysis of the Laboratory-Developed SAW Fluxes

XRD analysis of four representative fluxes revealed distinct crystalline-phase assemblages that provide insight into thermal behaviour and metallurgical workability (Figure 5.2). Flux 2, containing 35% CaF₂ and 25% MnO, exhibited sharp fluorite reflections at 2θ = 28.3°, 32.2°, and 47.0°, corresponding to the (111), (200), and (220) planes. The high intensity and narrow full width at half maximum (FWHM) indicate high crystallinity and large crystallite size, consistent with the phase development that Schwemer, Olson, and Williamson (1979) linked to controlled flux processing. Flux 20, with lower CaF₂ content (31.3%), showed weaker fluorite peaks and additional MnO reflections at 30.1° and 50.5°. The inverse relationship between fluorite and MnO intensities suggests competitive crystallization, a phenomenon consistent with the slag-phase interactions described by Chai and Eagar (1982) for CaF₂–metal-oxide fluxes. Flux 12 displayed an intermediate assemblage with strong fluorite reflections, well-defined rutile peaks at 27.4°, 36.1°, and 54.4°, and discrete MnO signatures. The higher rutile/fluorite intensity ratio in Flux 12 indicates greater retention of crystalline titania, which Beidokhti, Koukabi, and Dolati (2009) associated with improved arc stabilization.

#### 5.1.2.3. Structural Behaviour of the Laboratory-Developed SAW Fluxes

##### 5.1.2.3.1. Structure–Property Correlations

The XRD results correlate directly with the thermophysical properties described in Section 5.1.2.1. The high fluorite crystallinity of Flux 2 and Flux 16 corresponded to lower thermal diffusivity (0.215–0.245 mm²/s) than the MnO-rich Flux 20 (0.335 mm²/s). This behaviour aligns with the phonon-scattering mechanism reported for fluorite-structured compounds, in which fluorine-site vacancies disrupt heat-carrying phonons (Callister & Rethwisch, 2020). The higher diffusivity of MnO-rich compositions is attributable to the partially metallic character of MnO, which enables an electronic contribution to thermal conduction, consistent with the slag-conductivity observations of North, Bell, Nowicki, and Craig (1978).

The qualitative degree of crystallinity, inferred from peak sharpness and intensity, was inversely related to the basicity index. Higher-basicity fluxes (e.g., Flux 2 and Flux 13) produced sharper, more intense reflections, whereas lower-basicity fluxes (e.g., Flux 11 and Flux 18) yielded broader, weaker peaks indicative of higher amorphous content. This relationship is mechanistically consistent with the network-forming role of SiO₂ and the network-modifying/crystallizing role of basic oxides (CaO, BaO) and CaF₂ described by Baune, Bonnet, and Liu (2000) and Jindal, Chhibber, and Mehta (2013a).

##### 5.1.2.3.2. FTIR Spectral Interpretation and Network Depolymerization

FTIR spectra of eight flux samples (Figure 5.3) were interpreted following the framework applied to oxide-based welding-flux systems by Kim et al. (2015). All spectra exhibited absorption bands characteristic of silicate-network vibrations, hydroxyl groups, and metal–oxygen bonds. The most prominent band, near 1100 cm⁻¹, corresponds to asymmetric Si–O–Si stretching in silicate tetrahedra. This band shifted systematically to lower wavenumbers for Flux 14, 19, 21, and 25 (set B; mean 1085 cm⁻¹) relative to Flux 1, 6, 7, and 11 (set A; mean 1105 cm⁻¹). Because the Si–O–Si stretching frequency scales with the degree of polymerization, this red shift indicates increased network depolymerization in set B, driven by a higher concentration of non-bridging oxygens (NBOs) introduced by the basic modifiers BaO, MnO, and CaO (Kim et al., 2015; Baune et al., 2000). The shoulder near 950 cm⁻¹, assigned to non-bridging Si–O stretching, was more intense in set B. The I₉₅₀/I₁₁₀₀ intensity ratio—used here as a semi-quantitative depolymerization index—was 0.15–0.25 for set A and 0.30–0.45 for set B, confirming greater modifier-induced depolymerization in set B, in agreement with the structural trends reported by Kim et al. (2015).

##### 5.1.2.3.3. Correlation with Thermal and Anticipated Slag Behaviour

The FTIR depolymerization index correlated positively with the volumetric specific-heat values of Section 5.1.2.1. Set B (higher I₉₅₀/I₁₁₀₀) exhibited specific heats of 1.05–1.19 MJ/m³·K, whereas set A ranged from 0.90 to 0.98 MJ/m³·K. This correlation is mechanistically sound: depolymerized silicate networks possess more vibrational modes and higher configurational entropy than polymerized networks, enhancing heat absorption (Callister & Rethwisch, 2020).

*Figure 5.3. FTIR spectra of Flux 1, 6, 7, and 11 (set A) and Flux 14, 19, 21, and 25 (set B), showing percent transmittance versus wavenumber (4000–500 cm⁻¹).*

The relationship between slag behaviour and network depolymerization is well established (North et al., 1978; Chai & Eagar, 1982): increased depolymerization lowers slag viscosity and melting temperature. On this basis, the FTIR evidence supports the inference that set B formulations—particularly Flux 21 and Flux 25—would exhibit greater fluidity and lower slag viscosity than set A. It should be emphasized, however, that high-temperature viscosity and thermal-analysis experiments were not conducted in this study, so these remain structurally grounded inferences rather than measured process outcomes (Kumar & Chhibber, 2024a).

##### 5.1.2.3.4. Hydroxyl and Carbonate Species

Broad O–H stretching bands centred near 3400 cm⁻¹ were present in all spectra, with intensities varying between samples (Figure 5.3). These bands arise from surface moisture and structural water in the potassium-silicate binder, both of which can raise the diffusible-hydrogen content of the weld metal (Sharma & Chhibber, 2018). The stronger hydroxyl bands in set A imply greater moisture retention and, correspondingly, a higher potential for hydrogen-induced cracking, consistent with the flux-chemistry–hydrogen relationships reported by Plessis, Toit, and Pistorius (2007) and Sharma and Chhibber (2018).

Complementary Si–O–Si bending complexes between 400 and 600 cm⁻¹ indicate ring structures and three-dimensional network connectivity, and their intensity provides a further measure of network polymerization and modifier content (Kim et al., 2015). The characteristic TiO₂ lattice bands varied in intensity with titania content, while MnO addition introduced additional lattice modes in the 700–400 cm⁻¹ region that overlapped the Ti–O and Si–O–Si bands—an effect that intensified with MnO content and signalled changes in oxidation state. As Jindal, Chhibber, and Mehta (2013b) noted, MnO addition liberates free oxygen ions that modify the silicate network and influence both thermal and hydrogen-dissolution behaviour.

##### 5.1.2.3.5. Scope of the Physicochemical and Thermophysical Characterization

This study established quantitative composition–property relationships for bulk density, thermal conductivity, specific heat, and diffusivity using a systematic DoE approach, consistent with the modelling philosophy of Kumar and Chhibber (2022) and Kumar et al. (2023). The XRD and FTIR evidence—particularly the I₉₅₀/I₁₁₀₀ ratio and fluorite crystallinity—provides a defensible basis for hypothesizing slag fluidity and thermal transport in the resulting slags. Nevertheless, slag viscosity, melting temperature, and detachability were not measured directly. Accordingly, claims of improved fluidity, reduced melting temperature, or enhanced arc stability are inferences from thermophysical and structural data rather than validated process results (Kumar & Chhibber, 2024a). Future work should include high-temperature viscosity measurements, differential thermal analysis for melting behaviour, slag-detachment-force testing, weld-metal hydrogen analysis (Sharma & Chhibber, 2018), and in-situ arc monitoring.

## 5.2. Multi-Pass Bead-on-Plate Experimentation for the Twenty-Five Laboratory-Developed SAW Fluxes

### 5.2.1. Bead Profile Analysis

Following multi-pass bead-on-plate deposition, the 25 beads were examined visually (Figure 5.4) for morphology, porosity, and slag detachability. Flux composition strongly influenced bead geometry, surface quality, and slag detachment, in agreement with the findings of Datta, Bandyopadhyay, and Pal (2008) and Paniagua-Mercado, Lopez-Hirata, and Saucedo-Munoz (2005). Higher-basicity fluxes produced smoother beads with superior slag detachability, whereas lower-basicity fluxes gave rougher surfaces with adherent slag—consistent with the basicity–detachability relationship reported by Sharma and Chhibber (2019c). Formulations F4, F6, F10, F20, and F22 achieved the best combination of smooth, uniform morphology, negligible porosity, and satisfactory slag detachability, and were retained for further investigation.

##### 5.2.2.1.1. Regression Models of Weld-Metal Chemistry and Microhardness

Least-squares regression models were derived for the weld-metal constituents using the mixture-design methodology of Cornell (2011) and Adeyeye and Oyawale (2008). Second- and special-cubic mixture models incorporating linear, binary, and ternary interaction terms were used to capture the individual and synergistic effects of TiO₂, SiO₂, CaF₂, BaO, CaO, and MnO, following the response-surface framework of Myers, Montgomery, and Anderson-Cook (2016). In mixture experiments the components are subject to a summation constraint, Σtᵢ = 85 wt% (Equation 5.1), where tᵢ is the weight fraction of each oxide; because the variables are not independent, Scheffé canonical mixture models were employed, in which the intercept is absorbed into the linear terms (Cornell, 2011).

The general model forms applied were:

- Linear: ŷ = Σβᵢtᵢ
- Quadratic: ŷ = Σβᵢtᵢ + Σβᵢⱼtᵢtⱼ
- Special cubic: ŷ = Σβᵢtᵢ + Σβᵢⱼtᵢtⱼ + Σβᵢⱼₖtᵢtⱼtₖ

The coefficient vector was obtained as β = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy (Equation 5.2) from the design matrix X and response vector y (Montgomery, 2019). ##### 5.2.2.1.3. Discussion of Flux-Component Effects on Weld Chemistry and Microhardness

Flux constituents exerted strong synergistic effects on weld-metal chemistry through slag–metal reactions, in which free oxygen ions (O²⁻) govern element transfer and oxygen potential at the slag–metal interface (North et al., 1978; Davis & Bailey, 1991). Basic oxides such as CaO, BaO, and MnO dissociate in the molten slag to release O²⁻ ions, altering the interfacial oxygen chemical potential, as described by the following slag–metal equilibria (Chai & Eagar, 1982; Kanjilal et al., 2007):

```
SiO₂ (slag) ⇌ [Si] (metal) + 2[O] (metal)                                   (19)
MnO (slag) ⇌ [Mn] (metal) + [O] (metal)                                     (20)
[S] (metal) + O²⁻ (slag) ⇌ S²⁻ (slag) + [O] (metal)                         (21)
CO (gas) + O²⁻ (slag) ⇌ CO₃²⁻ (slag)                                        (22)
```

Consistent with the high-basicity slag behaviour reported by Fox, Eakes, and Franke (1996) and Dallam, Liu, and Olson (1985), the main-effect coefficients of the basic oxides were positive, particularly for CaO in the Mn and Cr models. Conversely, the negative CaO·CaF₂ and BaO·SiO₂ interaction terms indicate the formation of stable slag complexes that suppress alloy transfer, in agreement with Baune et al. (2000). The strongly negative primary coefficients in the sulfur model reflect the well-documented desulfurizing action of basic oxides (Sharma & Chhibber, 2019c). MnO exhibited dual behaviour—retained at intermediate concentrations but forming stable MnO·CaF₂ and TiO₂·MnO complexes (strongly negative interaction terms) in fluoride-rich or highly oxidizing slags, as previously reported for SAW by Kanjilal et al. (2007) and Quintana et al. (2003).

Trace (Piepel) plots (Figure 5.6) illustrate the sensitivity of each element to individual flux constituents (Piepel, 1988). CaO showed the largest positive slope for microhardness, identifying it as the most effective single component for hardness enhancement, primarily by promoting Mn and Mo recovery in the weld pool—consistent with Jindal et al. (2013a). BaO exhibited a moderately positive trend at low deviations that plateaued and reversed at higher deviations, reflecting the negative BaO·SiO₂ interaction. CaF₂ consistently displayed a negative slope, indicating stable-slag-complex formation that reduces alloy recovery and hardenability, as reported by Chai and Eagar (1982) and Jindal, Chhibber, and Mehta (2013b).

##### 5.2.2.1.4. Response-Surface and Contour Analysis

The multivariable relationships are summarized in the three-dimensional response surfaces of Figure 5.7. Combining the MH and CE models provides a more coherent description of the chemical and mechanical responses than conventional single-response models (Myers et al., 2016). Both measured and predicted MH and CE responded consistently to variations in the alloy-pickup constituents CaO, MnO, and TiO₂. Increasing CaO and MnO within an optimal window raised Mn, Mo, and Cr, thereby increasing CE and microhardness through enhanced hardenability—consistent with reported behaviour of HSLA pipeline welds (Kanjilal et al., 2005; Beidokhti et al., 2009). TiO₂ acted more subtly, modifying inclusion chemistry and refining acicular ferrite to raise hardness with a modest CE reduction (Beidokhti et al., 2009). Increasing CaF₂ and SiO₂ lowered Mn and Mo and, in turn, MH and CE, in agreement with Sharma and Chhibber (2019c) and Paniagua-Mercado et al. (2005).

The response surfaces (Figure 5.7i–j) show sharper curvature and well-defined interior extrema than the largely monotonic trends reported for simpler polynomial blend models (Adeyeye & Oyawale, 2009), indicating a stronger capability to capture the non-linear composition–property relationships governing final weld properties. The optimum composition within the design space was Flux F4 (SiO₂ 7.94%, TiO₂ 15.42%, CaF₂ 30.09%, BaO 6.55%, MnO 25%, CaO 8%), yielding 180–220 HV, CE 0.20–0.31, and well-developed acicular ferrite. F4 produced a fine acicular-ferrite microstructure (220 HV, CE 0.29) at a low weld-metal Ti level (9–30 ppm), whereas many commercial fluxes require higher Ti additions (0.02–0.08%) to achieve comparable microstructures (Beidokhti et al., 2009; Kanjilal et al., 2005).

## 5.3. Corrosion Analysis and Discussion

The electrochemical corrosion behaviour of API X70 SAW weldments was investigated to establish the influence of flux chemistry on the corrosion resistance of the weld metal and heat-affected zone (HAZ) under simulated marine conditions (Sharma & Chhibber, 2019e; Sharma & Chhibber, 2018). Potentiodynamic polarization tests were conducted in 3.5 wt.% NaCl (sea water) at room temperature using a three-electrode cell to reproduce the aggressive marine/offshore service environment of pipeline weldments (Sharma & Chhibber, 2019e). Corrosion potential (E_corr), corrosion current density (i_corr), and corrosion rate were determined by Tafel extrapolation.

Corrosion behaviour was governed largely by weld-metal chemistry—specifically the Mn, Mo, Cr, and Si transferred from the flux through slag–metal reactions (North et al., 1978; Kanjilal et al., 2007). Sharma and Chhibber (2019e) demonstrated that the electrochemical response of SAW weldments is directly linked to flux composition, which controls weld-metal chemistry and inclusion morphology. The following flux-dependent mechanisms are the most important in the corrosion-resistance hierarchy observed here:

(i) **Chromium and molybdenum transfer.** Higher Cr and Mo contents promote stable passive films (Cr₂O₃ and MoO₃) that resist breakdown in chloride environments. The laboratory fluxes transferred appreciable Mo to the weld metal (F6B, 0.392%; F20B, 0.401%; F22B, 0.355%), which enhances pitting resistance relative to Mo-free fluxes, consistent with the alloy-transfer–corrosion relationships of Sharma and Chhibber (2019e) and Quintana et al. (2003).

(ii) **Sulfur and phosphorus removal.** Basic fluxes with high CaF₂ and CaO content desulfurize the weld metal through the ionic exchange [S]_metal + (O²⁻)_slag ⇌ (S²⁻)_slag + [O]_metal (Chai & Eagar, 1982). Sulfur was markedly lower in the laboratory-flux weldments (F6B, 0.0024%; F20B, 0.0019%; F22B, 0.0011%) than in the commercial-flux weldment (CF, 0.0070%), reducing the formation of grain-boundary MnS inclusions that act as preferential pit-initiation sites (Sharma & Chhibber, 2019c).

(iii) **Inclusion morphology and distribution.** Flux basicity controls oxygen potential and therefore the size, shape, and distribution of oxide inclusions (Davis & Bailey, 1991). Fine, dispersed inclusions from high-basicity fluxes pose a lower corrosion risk than the large, angular, or clustered inclusions typical of low-basicity fluxes, which can dissolve preferentially to form micro-pits (North et al., 1978).

(iv) **Microstructural uniformity.** Acicular-ferrite-dominated microstructures (favoured by basic fluxes such as F6B) present fine, randomly oriented grains that minimize galvanic potential differences and promote uniform corrosion, whereas coarse grain-boundary or Widmanstätten ferrite promotes preferential attack along interphase boundaries (Thewlis, 1994; Bhadeshia, 2001).

The polarization results in sea water established a clear corrosion-resistance hierarchy: **F6B > CF > F20B > F22B** (Figure 5.7a). The F6B weldment—combining low sulfur (0.0024%), appreciable Mo (0.392%), and a predominantly acicular-ferrite microstructure—exhibited the most noble E_corr and the lowest i_corr among the laboratory fluxes, in agreement with Sharma and Chhibber (2019e). The commercial flux (CF), despite the highest Mo content (0.412%), carried a higher sulfur level (0.0070%) that partly offset its alloying benefit, placing it just below F6B. F20B and F22B, although very low in sulfur (0.0019% and 0.0011%), had slightly lower Mn, which reduced passive-film stability and yielded the more active potentials and higher current densities that rank them below CF (Sharma & Chhibber, 2019c).

Where regions of differing electrochemical potential coexist—HAZ, weld metal, and base metal—galvanic cells can drive localized deterioration (Sharma & Chhibber, 2018). Because flux composition dictates weld-metal chemistry and microstructure, it is a decisive factor in minimizing these galvanic differences and delivering uniform corrosion resistance across the weldment. Overall, the laboratory-developed basic fluxes produced weldments with corrosion resistance equal or superior to that of the commercial flux, supporting the systematic flux-design strategy for marine and offshore pipeline applications (Sharma & Chhibber, 2019e).

*Figure 5.7a. Potentiodynamic polarization curves of F6B, F20B, F22B, and CF weldments in sea water (3.5 wt.% NaCl). Corrosion-resistance trend: F6B > CF > F20B > F22B.*

## 5.4. Selection of Adequate Fluxes from the Twenty-Five Laboratory-Developed SAW Fluxes

### 5.4.1. Selection of Suitable Fluxes

Fluxes for final weld-joint fabrication were selected through a multi-criteria evaluation (Datta et al., 2008). Following multi-pass bead-on-plate deposition, the 25 beads were inspected for morphology, porosity, and slag detachability (Figure 5.8), and three basic-system fluxes with good morphology, minimal porosity, and satisfactory detachability were retained. Fluxes F6B, F20B, and F22B were therefore selected for the final butt-weld joints and compared against a commercial flux (C.F.). The compositions were developed using the extreme-vertices mixture-design methodology (Piepel, 1988; Cornell, 2011) and guided by the ternary equilibrium phase diagrams SiO₂–CaO–CaF₂, SiO₂–TiO₂–MnO, and SiO₂–BaO–B₂O₃ (Figure 5.9).

The fluxes were prepared from silica (SiO₂), titania (TiO₂), calcium fluoride (CaF₂), barium oxide (BaO), manganese oxide (MnO), and calcium oxide (CaO) powders. API X70 base plates were prepared in a single-V butt configuration (60° included angle, 2 mm root gap; Figure 5.10). All welds were deposited with direct-current electrode-positive (DCEP) polarity at 440 A, 28 V, and 13 in/min travel speed, with a 20 mm nozzle-to-tip distance, following the SAW procedures reported by Sharma and Chhibber (2019d) and Kanjilal et al. (2006).

### 5.4.2. Weld-Specimen Chemical Analysis

Table 5.3 reports the composition of the API X70 base metal, EA2TiB filler wire, and weld metals produced with fluxes F6B, F20B, F22B, and C.F. API X70 is a low-carbon (0.059 wt.%) steel micro-alloyed with Mn, Mo, Ti, Cr, Nb, and Ni (Sharma & Chhibber, 2018). The weld metals showed reduced Ni and Nb and increased carbide-forming Ti, B, Cu, and Cr relative to the base metal, contributed by the parent and fusion metals; such carbide formers promote low-temperature phase transformations (Easterling, 1992; Svensson, 1994). Sulfur was substantially lower in all weld joints than in the base metal, filler wire, and commercial flux, reflecting the desulfurizing action of the O²⁻-rich basic laboratory fluxes (Chai & Eagar, 1982; Sharma & Chhibber, 2019c).

The maximum carbon equivalent (CE = 0.38 wt.%) occurred for the commercial-flux weld, whereas F6B was close to the base metal (CE 0.31 vs. 0.33) and F20B (0.28) and F22B (0.27) were lower still. The reduced CE in the fusion zone relative to the parent metal lowers the volume fraction of low-temperature transformation products, a trend that Lancaster (1999) and Kou (2003) linked to the formation of finer, tougher microstructures.

### 5.4.4. Fractography of the Impact Specimens

Fractography of the impact-tested API X70 SAW weldments revealed the interplay among flux chemistry, microstructure, and fracture mechanism in the parent metal (PM), HAZ, and fusion zone (FZ). Each region exhibited a distinct failure mode governed by its microstructure and the flux used (Easterling, 1992; Lancaster, 1999). The PM showed the highest toughness (350 J at RT; Figure 5.12a) with a well-developed dimpled, microvoid-coalescence surface indicative of ductile fracture (Callister & Rethwisch, 2020).

The fusion zone displayed the most complex fractography and the lowest toughness. The basic flux F6B (Figure 5.12b) and the commercial flux CF (Figure 5.12h) performed well at RT (171 J and 159 J) with predominantly ductile features, transitioning to brittle fracture with faceted regions at −55 °C (Figures 5.12c, 5.12i). By contrast, F20B and F22B produced larger cleavage facets and smaller shear lips at both temperatures (Figures 5.12d–g), indicating lower fracture resistance, consistent with the microstructure–toughness relationship of Thewlis (1994).

The HAZ exhibited transitional behaviour between PM and FZ. The coarse-grained HAZ (CGHAZ) adjacent to the fusion line was the most brittle, containing martensite–austenite (M–A) constituents and coarse prior-austenite grains (Arya, Singh, & Saxena, 2018; Arora et al., 2018). HAZ specimens from the basic flux F6B (Figures 5.12j–k) and CF (Figures 5.12l–m) showed increased ductile-fracture area relative to the other fluxes; F6B delivered 385 J at RT and 28 J at −55 °C, with intense microvoid coalescence. Inclusion morphology strongly influenced HAZ fracture: fine, well-dispersed inclusions pinned grain boundaries and refined the microstructure to favour ductile fracture, whereas large, clustered inclusions coarsened the structure and promoted cleavage (Beidokhti et al., 2009; Davis & Bailey, 1991).

### 5.4.5. Microhardness and Microstructure Analysis

Microhardness of the API X70 SAW joints reflects the interaction among flux basicity, weld-metal composition, and thermally induced microstructural change in the parent metal, weld metal, and HAZ (Kou, 2003; Easterling, 1992). Fusion-zone and HAZ microhardness values are given in Table 5.5.

|------|--------------|--------------|---------------|---------------|
| F6B | 224 | 205 | 251 | 208 |
| F20B | 215 | 195 | 229 | 201 |

The as-received API X70 steel had a fine ferrite–pearlite microstructure with 190–220 HV, providing the balanced strength–toughness required for high-pressure service (Sharma & Chhibber, 2018). Welding thermal cycles modified the phase fractions and grain morphology across the HAZ and weld metal, producing the characteristic hardness distributions (Kou, 2003). The maximum fusion-zone hardness for the laboratory and commercial fluxes was 224 HV and 233 HV, respectively, and the maximum HAZ hardness was 251 HV and 257 HV. The basic laboratory fluxes (F6B, F20B, F22B) generated more basic welds with greater slag–metal interaction, lowering weld-metal oxygen and hydrogen, refining the microstructure, and suppressing coarse martensite and fresh bainite in the fusion zone (Sharma & Chhibber, 2019c; Plessis et al., 2007).

The F6B weld showed a moderate, uniform hardness (205–224 HV) attributable to predominant acicular ferrite with fine dispersed bainite (Figure 5.13b). As reported previously, higher basic-oxide content reduces weld-metal oxygen and oxide-inclusion density, increasing nucleation sites for acicular ferrite (Kanjilal et al., 2005; Beidokhti et al., 2009). The more CaO/CaF₂-rich F20B (195–215 HV) and F22B (184–202 HV) showed lower average hardness owing to enhanced deoxidation and refinement. The commercial flux gave the highest fusion-zone (210–233 HV) and HAZ (211–257 HV) hardness of the set.

Flux basicity strongly controlled the microstructural evolution of the weldments. High-basicity fluxes with a high ratio of basic oxides (CaO) to acidic oxides (SiO₂, TiO₂) produced a low-oxygen environment, reducing oxide-inclusion fraction and forming fine, Ti-rich complex inclusions that act as preferred nucleation sites for acicular ferrite (AF)—an interlocking, cleavage-resistant morphology (Figures 5.13a–i; Beidokhti et al., 2009; Thewlis, 1994; Olson, 1986). The fusion zones of F6B, F20B, F22B, and CF welds consisted of acicular ferrite with minor polygonal ferrite and bainite. Lower-basicity or lower-rutile fluxes raise weld-metal oxygen, coarsen inclusions, and promote less-desirable grain-boundary ferrite (GBF) and Widmanstätten ferrite (WF), consistent with Dallam et al. (1985) and Thewlis (1994). The HAZ, which experiences a complex thermal cycle without melting, showed similar flux sensitivity through indirect mechanisms; its sub-regions—CGHAZ (susceptible to austenite coarsening) and FGHAZ—developed predominantly bainite and acicular ferrite for all fluxes (Arya et al., 2018; Arora et al., 2018). M–A constituents and fine bainite forming on rapid cooling near the fusion line caused the modest CGHAZ hardness increase (Bhadeshia, 2001). Acceptable hardness levels across the parent metal, weld metal, and HAZ can be maintained for both the basic laboratory fluxes and the commercial flux, provided heat input, preheat, and post-weld thermal regimes are controlled to prevent excessive hardening or localized softening that could compromise the in-service integrity of API X70 pipelines (Kou, 2003; Arya et al., 2018).

Microstructural examination confirmed that the basic laboratory fluxes—especially F6B—favoured acicular-ferrite formation, the most desirable morphology for fusion-zone toughness, while the HAZ developed QPF, GBF, and M/A phases. Mechanically, the F6B weldment achieved 171 J (RT) and 21 J (−55 °C) in the fusion zone and 385 J (RT) and 28 J (−55 °C) in the HAZ, comparable or superior to the commercial flux (159 J and 18 J in the FZ; ~385 J and ~28 J in the HAZ). Hardness distributions were acceptable across all weldments, with no excessive hardening or softening. These results demonstrate that the laboratory basic fluxes, particularly F6B, are viable alternatives for pipeline welding and confirm the effectiveness of combining mixture design with ternary-phase-diagram guidance for the systematic development of flux systems for high-strength low-alloy steels (Sharma & Chhibber, 2019d; Adeyeye & Oyawale, 2009).
