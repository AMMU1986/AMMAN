# Chapter 6. Artificial Intelligence for Thermal Engineering

## Abstract

Artificial intelligence has moved, over the course of little more than a decade, from the periphery of thermal engineering to a position of central importance, and the analysis and design of nanofluid heat exchangers has been among the fields most transformed by its adoption. The reasons are rooted in the difficulties documented in the preceding chapters, namely the resistance of nanofluid properties and convective behaviour to first-principles prediction, the high dimensionality of the design space, the expense of both experiment and high-fidelity computation, and the scatter of the experimental database, all of which are conditions under which data-driven methods that learn relationships directly from data offer advantages that mechanistic modelling struggles to match. This chapter introduces artificial intelligence as a discipline and as a toolset for thermal engineering, at a level of generality that prepares the reader for the more detailed treatment of machine-learning fundamentals and applications in the chapters that follow. It begins with the fundamentals and the historical evolution of artificial intelligence, clarifying the nested relationship among artificial intelligence, machine learning and deep learning and the vocabulary that the field employs. It then surveys the artificial-intelligence techniques that have been applied to heat-transfer and fluid-flow problems, from artificial neural networks and support-vector methods to tree-based ensembles and evolutionary optimisation, and it characterises the kinds of problem each is suited to solve. Finally, it examines the advantages, limitations and opportunities of artificial-intelligence-based thermal modelling with a candour that is essential if the methods are to be applied soundly rather than fashionably, and it situates the data-driven paradigm within the broader practice of thermal engineering as a complement to, rather than a replacement for, physical understanding and mechanistic modelling.

## 6.1 Fundamentals and Evolution of Artificial Intelligence

Artificial intelligence, in the broadest sense, denotes the endeavour to construct systems that perform tasks which, if performed by a human, would be said to require intelligence, and the field has passed through several distinct phases since its formal inception in the mid-twentieth century. The earliest phase was dominated by symbolic approaches, in which intelligence was to be achieved by encoding human knowledge as explicit rules and manipulating symbols according to those rules, an approach that produced the expert systems of the nineteen-seventies and nineteen-eighties and that achieved notable successes in narrow, well-structured domains but that foundered on the difficulty of encoding the vast and often tacit knowledge required for less structured tasks [73]. The limitations of the symbolic approach, together with the growing availability of data and computational power, drove a shift toward the statistical and data-driven approaches that dominate the field today, in which the system learns the relationships it needs from examples rather than being programmed with them explicitly, a paradigm known as machine learning.

The contrast between the two paradigms is worth drawing sharply, because it illuminates why the data-driven approach is so well matched to the difficulties of nanofluid thermal engineering. The symbolic, knowledge-based approach demands that the relationships governing a problem be articulated in advance, as rules or equations that a human expert supplies, and it is powerful precisely where such articulation is possible, as in the well-understood thermodynamic and geometric relationships that underlie the conventional design methods of the earlier chapters. Its fatal weakness in the nanofluid context is that the relationships between the many variables governing conductivity, viscosity and convective enhancement are not known well enough to be articulated as rules, so that the knowledge required to program a symbolic system simply does not exist in usable form. The data-driven approach inverts the logic entirely, requiring not that the relationships be known in advance but only that examples of the input and output be available, from which the system infers the relationship itself; where the physics is obscure but the data are plentiful, this inversion turns an intractable knowledge-elicitation problem into a tractable learning problem [73]. The migration of thermal engineering toward data-driven methods is thus not a fashion but a rational response to a domain in which the knowledge needed for the older paradigm is unavailable while the data needed for the newer one are increasingly abundant.

The mid-twentieth-century origins of the field also bequeathed a recurring pattern of expectation and disappointment whose lessons remain pertinent. Periods of intense optimism, in which the imminent achievement of general machine intelligence was confidently predicted, gave way to periods of retrenchment when the predictions went unfulfilled and funding withdrew, episodes remembered as the winters of the field, and the alternation was driven in each case by a mismatch between ambition and the capabilities that the available data, computation and algorithms could actually support [73]. The contemporary resurgence rests on a firmer footing than earlier enthusiasms, because it is grounded in demonstrable performance on well-defined tasks rather than in speculation about general intelligence, but the historical pattern counsels a measured assessment of any new claim, and it is directly relevant to thermal engineering, where the sober evaluation of what data-driven methods can and cannot deliver, pursued in the final section of this chapter, is a safeguard against a local recurrence of the same cycle of overpromise and disillusionment.

The relationship among the terms artificial intelligence, machine learning and deep learning, which are frequently used loosely and interchangeably, is best understood as a nested hierarchy, and clarity about this hierarchy is a prerequisite for the sound use of the methods. Artificial intelligence is the broadest term, encompassing any technique by which a machine performs a task requiring intelligence, whether by explicit programming or by learning. Machine learning is the subset of artificial intelligence in which the system learns from data rather than being explicitly programmed, and it encompasses the regression, classification and clustering methods that constitute the bulk of thermal-engineering applications. Deep learning is a further subset of machine learning in which the learning is performed by artificial neural networks with many layers, capable of learning hierarchical representations from raw data and responsible for many of the most striking recent advances, in which each successive layer transforms the representation produced by the layer before it, building from simple to increasingly abstract features in a manner loosely analogous to the layered processing of the biological visual system [74]. The distinction between machine learning in general and deep learning in particular carries practical consequences that the thermal engineer must weigh, for it is not merely a matter of taxonomy. Deep learning, with its many-layered networks capable of discovering their own features from raw data, achieves its most spectacular results in domains characterised by vast datasets and by inputs, such as images, speech and text, whose relevant features are numerous, hierarchical and difficult for a human to specify. The tabular datasets of nanofluid thermal engineering, by contrast, are typically modest in size, numbering hundreds or a few thousands of examples rather than the millions on which deep networks thrive, and their inputs are a handful of physically meaningful variables whose relevance is already understood, so that the feature-discovery capability that is deep learning's great strength has little to work upon [74]. In consequence the shallower methods of classical machine learning, which learn efficiently from limited data and which do not require the analyst to abdicate control over the features, frequently outperform deep networks on thermal-engineering problems, and the reflexive equation of machine learning with deep learning, common in the popular understanding, is a misconception that can lead the practitioner to reach for an unnecessarily data-hungry tool.

Beyond this three-level hierarchy, the field is often described in terms of a further distinction between narrow and general intelligence that clarifies the realistic scope of thermal-engineering applications. Every method discussed in this book, and every application to nanofluids and heat exchangers, belongs to narrow artificial intelligence, in which a system is trained to perform a single, circumscribed task, such as predicting a thermal conductivity or optimising a geometry, and possesses no capability or understanding beyond that task. The general intelligence of popular imagination, a system with the broad, transferable competence of a human, remains a research aspiration with no bearing on the engineering practice developed here [74]. Keeping this distinction in view guards against both the inflated expectation that a data-driven model somehow understands the physics it predicts and the corresponding disappointment when it fails outside the narrow task for which it was trained, and it reinforces the theme, developed throughout the chapter, that these methods are powerful instruments for specific purposes rather than substitutes for engineering understanding.

The evolution from symbolic to statistical artificial intelligence was driven by a confluence of factors whose relevance to thermal engineering is direct. The accumulation of large datasets, from experimental campaigns, from computational studies and from the digitisation of the historical literature, provided the raw material from which data-driven methods learn. The growth of computational power, and in particular the advent of specialised hardware for the parallel arithmetic that neural-network training demands, made the training of large models feasible. The development of new algorithms and architectures, and their dissemination through open-source software libraries, lowered the barrier to entry so that thermal engineers without a background in computer science could apply sophisticated methods to their problems [75]. These same factors, operating within thermal engineering, explain the rapid growth of data-driven nanofluid and heat-exchanger modelling over the past decade, and they suggest that the trend will continue as datasets grow, computation cheapens and methods mature.

The role of open-source software in this democratisation deserves particular acknowledgement, because it has altered the practical accessibility of the methods as profoundly as any algorithmic advance. Where the application of a sophisticated learning method once demanded that the practitioner implement it from a mathematical description, a task requiring specialised expertise and considerable time, the mature libraries now freely available supply tested, efficient and well-documented implementations that a thermal engineer can apply with a few lines of instruction, so that the intellectual effort shifts from implementation to the more appropriate concerns of problem formulation, data preparation and the critical assessment of results [75]. This accessibility is a double-edged asset, for the same ease that empowers the careful analyst also permits the careless one to apply powerful methods without understanding them, producing results that are superficially impressive and fundamentally unsound; the ready availability of the tools thus raises, rather than lowers, the premium on the conceptual understanding that this book seeks to impart, for the tools will execute a flawed analysis as obligingly as a sound one and offer no warning of the difference.

A historical observation of direct relevance to thermal engineering is that the adoption of data-driven methods within the discipline has lagged their development in computer science by a decade or more, and that this lag, while diminishing, has shaped the character of the field's literature. Many early applications transplanted methods from other domains without adapting them to the peculiarities of thermal data, applying data-hungry techniques to small datasets or neglecting the physical constraints that thermal problems afford, and the resulting literature is uneven in quality, containing both exemplary studies and cautionary ones [73]. The maturing of the field entails not merely the adoption of ever more sophisticated methods but the development of a discipline-specific practice that respects the modest size, the physical structure and the experimental uncertainty of thermal data, and it is toward such a considered practice, rather than toward the uncritical importation of whatever is fashionable in the wider machine-learning world, that the guidance of this and the following chapter is directed.

The vocabulary of machine learning, which the thermal engineer must master to read and contribute to the literature, rests on a small number of central concepts. A feature is an input variable, such as the nanoparticle concentration or the Reynolds number, from which the model makes its prediction, and the collection of features constitutes the input space, whose dimensionality, being the number of features, exerts a decisive influence on how much data a model requires, since the volume of the input space grows so rapidly with dimension that the data needed to populate it adequately grows correspondingly, a difficulty known as the curse of dimensionality that makes the parsimonious choice of features, developed in the next chapter, a matter of real consequence rather than mere tidiness. A target, or label, is the output quantity the model predicts, such as the thermal conductivity or the Nusselt number, and the availability of correct targets for the training examples is what distinguishes the supervised learning that dominates thermal applications from the unsupervised learning that proceeds without them, a distinction whose full development the following chapter undertakes. A model is a parameterised function that maps features to targets, and training is the process of adjusting the model's parameters so that its predictions match the targets in a training dataset, a process that is guided by an explicit objective, the minimisation of a measure of prediction error, and that is carried out automatically by a numerical procedure rather than by hand. Generalisation is the ability of a trained model to make accurate predictions on data it has not seen, and it is the true measure of a model's worth, because a model that merely memorises its training data is of no predictive value, since the whole purpose of building a predictive model is to make predictions for conditions not already measured, and a model that performs well only on the conditions it has already seen serves no purpose that a lookup table would not serve as well [76]. These concepts, elaborated in Chapter 7, structure every application of machine learning to thermal engineering, and their careful definition here forestalls the confusion that a loose use of terminology invites.

The central tension embodied in the concept of generalisation, between fitting the training data and predicting the unseen, deserves emphasis at this early stage because it governs every subsequent judgement about a model's worth. A model with sufficient flexibility can be made to pass through every point of its training data exactly, achieving an apparently perfect fit, and yet such a model may predict disastrously on new inputs because it has memorised the idiosyncrasies and noise of the particular training set rather than learning the underlying relationship. The art of machine learning consists very largely in navigating this tension, in endowing a model with enough flexibility to capture the genuine structure of the data but not so much that it captures the noise, and the whole apparatus of validation, regularisation and complexity control that the following chapter develops exists to manage precisely this balance [76]. For the thermal engineer accustomed to correlations whose few coefficients are fitted once and reported, the shift to models with many adjustable parameters and a corresponding capacity for self-deception is a conceptual adjustment of the first importance, and the recognition that a good fit is not the same as a good model is among the most valuable lessons the discipline imparts.

A further concept that the vocabulary must accommodate is the distinction between the parameters that a model learns from the data during training and the hyperparameters that the analyst sets before training and that govern the learning process itself. The weights of a neural network, adjusted automatically to fit the data, are parameters; the number of layers, the width of each and the strength of the regularisation are hyperparameters, chosen by the analyst and not adjusted by the training. This distinction, elaborated in the next chapter, matters here because the performance of a data-driven model depends as much on the judicious setting of its hyperparameters as on the training data themselves, so that the construction of a good model is an iterative human activity of configuration and assessment rather than a single automatic procedure [76]. The appreciation that meaningful human judgement is exercised throughout the modelling process, in the choice of method, the engineering of features and the setting of hyperparameters, dispels the misconception that data-driven modelling is a mechanical substitute for expertise, and it foreshadows the integrated practice, developed in the chapter's conclusion, in which physical understanding guides every such judgement.

## 6.2 AI Techniques for Heat Transfer and Fluid Flow Problems

The artificial-intelligence techniques applied to heat-transfer and fluid-flow problems form a diverse toolkit, and the sound practitioner selects among them according to the nature of the problem, the quantity and quality of the available data, and the requirements of the application for accuracy, interpretability and computational cost. It bears emphasising at the outset that there exists no single technique that is best for all problems, a principle sometimes expressed as the observation that superior performance on one class of problems is necessarily paid for by inferior performance on another, so that the search for a universally optimal method is misguided and the effort is better spent in matching the method to the problem at hand. In practice the prudent analyst does not commit to a single method in advance but trials several representative techniques on the problem, compares their performance by the disciplined evaluation procedures of the following chapter, and selects on the evidence, a comparative approach that guards against both the misplaced loyalty to a favourite method and the uncritical adoption of whatever is currently fashionable, and that is entirely feasible given the ready availability of tested implementations of all the principal methods. The artificial neural network, the most widely used technique in nanofluid and heat-exchanger modelling, is a flexible function approximator composed of layers of interconnected processing units, each of which computes a weighted sum of its inputs and passes the result through a nonlinear activation function. By adjusting the weights during training, a neural network with even a single hidden layer of sufficient width can approximate any continuous function to arbitrary accuracy, a property that makes it well suited to learning the complex, nonlinear relationships between the many variables that govern nanofluid behaviour and the properties or performance measures of interest [77].

This universal-approximation property, frequently invoked to justify the use of neural networks, is often misunderstood in a way that warrants correction. The property guarantees only that a network of sufficient size exists which can approximate a given function; it says nothing about how large that network must be, whether the finite and noisy data available suffice to determine its weights, or whether the training procedure will succeed in finding them. A capability that exists in principle may be unattainable in practice when the data are too few to pin down the many weights that a capable network contains, and the naive appeal to universal approximation, as though it assured success, overlooks precisely the difficulties of data scarcity and generalisation that dominate real applications [77]. The property is therefore best regarded as a licence to attempt the fitting of complex relationships rather than a promise that the attempt will succeed, and the success depends, as always, on the sufficiency and quality of the data far more than on the theoretical expressiveness of the architecture. The neural network's flexibility is both its strength and its weakness, for the same capacity that allows it to fit complex relationships also allows it to overfit limited or noisy data, so that its successful application depends on careful regularisation and validation.

The manner in which a neural network learns repays a verbal description, because an intuitive grasp of the training process illuminates both its power and its failure modes. Training presents the network with examples whose correct outputs are known, compares the network's predictions against those known outputs through a loss function that measures the discrepancy, and adjusts the weights so as to reduce that discrepancy, repeating the cycle many times over the dataset. The adjustment is guided by the gradient of the loss with respect to each weight, computed efficiently by propagating the error backward through the layers, and each weight is nudged in the direction that most reduces the loss, by an amount governed by a learning rate whose setting is itself a delicate matter, for too large a rate causes the adjustment to overshoot and diverge while too small a rate causes learning to crawl [77]. This gradient-guided descent through a landscape of enormous dimensionality is remarkably effective in practice, but it offers no guarantee of finding the best possible set of weights, only a good one, and the dependence of the outcome on the starting point, the learning rate and the order in which examples are presented means that two ostensibly identical training runs may yield perceptibly different models, a variability that the practitioner must anticipate and manage.

The architecture of a network, meaning the number of layers and the number of units in each, determines its capacity to represent complex relationships, and its selection is among the consequential judgements in applying the method. A network with too few units lacks the capacity to capture the relationship and underfits, predicting poorly even on its training data; a network with too many units has capacity to spare and, on the limited datasets of nanofluid research, tends to expend that surplus capacity in fitting noise, overfitting and generalising badly. The guidance that emerges from experience is to prefer the smallest network that captures the relationship adequately, to be especially conservative with depth and width when data are scarce, and to lean on regularisation techniques, which penalise complexity or randomly disable units during training, to restrain a network's tendency to overfit [77]. The activation function, the nonlinearity through which each unit passes its weighted input, is a further architectural choice whose modern default admits efficient training of deep networks, but the deeper architectures that such functions enable are, as noted earlier, rarely warranted for the modest tabular datasets of thermal engineering, where a single hidden layer of modest width frequently suffices.

Support-vector methods constitute a second important family, founded on the principle of finding the decision boundary or regression function that maximises a margin of separation or that fits the data within a specified tolerance while penalising complexity. Support-vector regression, the variant used for the continuous prediction problems typical of thermal engineering, is valued for its solid theoretical foundation, its resistance to overfitting when properly configured, and its effective performance on the modestly sized datasets that characterise much of the nanofluid literature, where the number of experimental points is often too small for the reliable training of large neural networks [78]. The performance of support-vector methods depends on the choice of a kernel function, which implicitly maps the data into a higher-dimensional space in which a linear relationship is sought, and on the tuning of regularisation parameters, and this dependence on configuration, shared with the neural network, is a recurring theme in the application of machine learning.

The kernel device that lends support-vector methods their power deserves a fuller intuitive account, because it is at first encounter mysterious. A relationship that is hopelessly nonlinear in the original variables may become simple, even linear, when the data are viewed in a space of higher dimension constructed from nonlinear combinations of the original features, and the kernel function is a mathematical shortcut that computes the effect of working in that higher-dimensional space without ever constructing it explicitly, evaluating instead a measure of similarity between pairs of data points. By selecting a kernel, the analyst in effect selects the family of nonlinear relationships the method can represent, a smooth and gently curving family for one common kernel and a more locally flexible family for another, and the match between the chosen kernel and the true character of the relationship largely determines the method's success [78]. This displacement of the modelling choice from the form of the function to the form of the kernel is elegant, but it does not eliminate the need for judgement; it relocates it, and the tuning of the kernel and its associated parameters by disciplined validation remains essential.

The special suitability of support-vector regression to small datasets, which recommends it strongly for much nanofluid work, arises from its founding principle of controlling complexity explicitly. Rather than seeking merely to fit the training points, the method seeks the flattest, simplest function that fits them within a tolerance, trading a small amount of training accuracy for a large gain in robustness, and this deliberate preference for simplicity is exactly the discipline that guards against overfitting when data are too few to reveal complex structure reliably [78]. A neural network confronted with a few hundred scattered measurements is prone to contrive an elaborate relationship that the data cannot justify, whereas support-vector regression, constrained by its complexity penalty, resists such contrivance, and this difference in temperament, as much as any difference in raw capability, explains why the two methods suit different data regimes and why the choice between them should follow from the size and quality of the dataset rather than from the reputation of the method.

Tree-based methods, and in particular the ensemble methods that combine many trees, form a third family that has grown rapidly in popularity for thermal-engineering applications. A decision tree partitions the input space into regions by a sequence of binary splits and predicts a constant within each region, a simple and interpretable structure that, taken alone, tends to overfit. The manner in which a tree chooses its splits is itself illuminating, for at each step it examines the available features and the possible thresholds and selects the split that most reduces the impurity or the prediction error of the resulting regions, a greedy procedure that builds the tree from the top down without reconsidering earlier choices. This greediness makes the tree fast to construct and easy to follow, since the path from root to leaf reads as a sequence of intelligible conditions on the features, but it also renders the single tree brittle, because an early split chosen on the basis of a slightly unrepresentative sample can commit the whole tree to a suboptimal structure, and it is this brittleness that the ensembles are designed to overcome [79].

A particular virtue of tree-based methods in the thermal-engineering context, beyond their accuracy, is their indifference to the scaling and distribution of the features, which spares the analyst the normalisation and transformation that other methods demand and which makes them robust to the mixed and irregularly distributed variables that thermal datasets contain. A tree splits on the rank order of a feature rather than its magnitude, so that a feature measured in inconvenient units, or one whose values span several orders of magnitude, is handled as gracefully as a well-behaved one, and features on wildly different scales, such as a particle diameter in nanometres and a temperature in kelvin, coexist without the rescaling that a neural network or a support-vector machine would require [79]. This robustness, combined with the tolerance of tree ensembles for the occasional missing or anomalous value, contributes materially to their reputation as the dependable first choice for tabular thermal data, the method that most often yields a solid result with the least fuss. The ensemble methods overcome this tendency by combining many trees: the random forest averages the predictions of many trees grown on random subsets of the data and features, reducing variance, while the gradient-boosting methods build trees sequentially, each correcting the errors of its predecessors, achieving high accuracy that has made them dominant in many predictive-modelling competitions and increasingly prevalent in nanofluid property prediction [79]. The ensemble methods offer a favourable combination of accuracy, robustness to the scaling and distribution of the features, and a measure of interpretability through the feature-importance scores they produce, and they are frequently the strongest performers on tabular data of the kind that thermal engineering generates.

The distinction between the two principal ensemble strategies, the parallel averaging of the random forest and the sequential correction of gradient boosting, illuminates a general principle about how ensembles improve upon their constituent trees. The random forest attacks the instability of a single tree, its tendency to change markedly with small changes in the data, by growing many trees on randomly perturbed versions of the data and averaging their predictions, so that the idiosyncratic errors of the individual trees, being uncorrelated, largely cancel while their shared signal reinforces; the result is a prediction far more stable than any single tree could provide, achieved by the reduction of variance [79]. Gradient boosting attacks a different deficiency, the limited accuracy of a shallow tree, by building trees in sequence, each one trained to predict the errors that its predecessors left behind, so that the ensemble progressively refines its prediction and reduces bias. This sequential refinement can achieve remarkable accuracy, but it carries a corresponding risk, for a boosting ensemble trained too long will eventually begin to fit the noise in the training data, and its complexity must therefore be restrained by limiting the number and depth of the trees and by moderating the rate at which successive trees are allowed to contribute [79].

The feature-importance scores that tree ensembles produce are a distinctive asset in the thermal-engineering context, where the interpretability of a model is prized both for the trust it engenders and for the physical insight it may yield. By recording how much each feature contributes to the reduction of prediction error across the many splits of the ensemble, these methods produce a ranking of the variables by their predictive influence, and when that ranking accords with physical expectation, assigning high importance to variables known to govern the phenomenon, it lends confidence that the model has learned genuine relationships rather than spurious correlations; when it conflicts with expectation, it prompts a valuable scrutiny that may reveal either a flaw in the data or an unanticipated physical effect [79]. This capacity to interrogate what a model has learned, limited though it is, sets the tree ensembles apart from the opaque neural network and contributes materially to their popularity in a field where the reconciliation of data-driven predictions with physical understanding is a constant preoccupation. Beyond these predictive techniques, the toolkit includes methods for optimisation and for the discovery of structure. Evolutionary and swarm-based optimisation algorithms, including genetic algorithms and particle-swarm optimisation, search a design space by maintaining and iteratively improving a population of candidate solutions according to rules inspired by biological evolution or collective behaviour, and they are well suited to the multi-objective, constraint-bounded optimisation of heat-exchanger designs, particularly when combined with a machine-learning surrogate that supplies the cheap evaluations the search requires [80]. Gaussian-process regression, which predicts not only a value but a calibrated uncertainty, is valued both as a surrogate model and as the engine of Bayesian optimisation, in which the uncertainty guides the search toward the most informative next evaluation. Unsupervised methods, including clustering and dimensionality reduction, discover structure in unlabelled data and are used to explore and organise datasets, to identify regimes and to reduce the dimensionality of the input space before predictive modelling [81]. The breadth of this toolkit, and the specialisation of its members to different tasks, means that the selection of technique is itself a skill, and the following chapters develop the criteria by which the selection is made.

The evolutionary and swarm-based optimisers merit a closer conceptual description, because their coupling with predictive surrogates is a recurring motif of data-driven design. A genetic algorithm maintains a population of candidate designs, each encoded as a set of decision variables, and improves the population across successive generations by imitating the mechanisms of natural selection: the better-performing candidates are preferentially selected to contribute to the next generation, their variables are recombined to produce offspring that blend the characteristics of their parents, and occasional random mutations introduce novelty that prevents the search from stagnating. Particle-swarm optimisation, drawing instead on the metaphor of a flock, moves each candidate through the design space under the combined influence of its own best-found position and the best found by the swarm as a whole, so that the population converges collectively toward promising regions [80]. Both methods share the virtues of requiring no gradient information, of tolerating the discontinuous and multi-modal objective landscapes that heat-exchanger design presents, and of naturally handling multiple competing objectives, and both share the vice of requiring very many evaluations of the objective, a vice that is fatal when each evaluation is an expensive computation but harmless when each is a query to a cheap machine-learning surrogate, which is precisely why the pairing of the two is so productive.

Gaussian-process regression warrants particular notice for a capability that few other methods possess, namely the provision of a calibrated estimate of its own uncertainty alongside each prediction. Rather than committing to a single function, the method maintains a distribution over the functions consistent with the data, so that where the data are dense its predictions are confident and where they are sparse its predictions are attended by a frankly reported wide uncertainty, a behaviour that mirrors the honest epistemic state of a model asked to extrapolate [81]. This self-knowledge is what makes the method the natural engine of Bayesian optimisation, in which the search for an optimum is guided not merely toward regions predicted to perform well but toward regions where the prediction is most uncertain and hence most informative, balancing the exploitation of known-good designs against the exploration of the unknown. The unsupervised methods of clustering and dimensionality reduction, finally, though they make no predictions, earn their place in the toolkit by preparing and illuminating the data, revealing the grouping of measurements by regime or by source and compressing correlated features into a smaller set, services whose value in organising the heterogeneous nanofluid database the following chapter develops in detail [81].

## 6.3 Advantages, Limitations, and Opportunities of AI-Based Thermal Modelling

The advantages of artificial-intelligence-based thermal modelling follow directly from the difficulties that motivate its adoption, and they are substantial. The foremost advantage is the ability to learn complex, high-dimensional and nonlinear relationships directly from data without requiring a closed-form mechanistic theory, which is precisely the capability that the intractable physics of nanofluid conductivity, viscosity and convective enhancement demands. A well-trained data-driven model can predict a property or a performance measure across the joint variation of many input variables with an accuracy that empirical correlations, limited to a few fitted constants and a fixed functional form, cannot match, and it can do so at negligible computational cost once trained, enabling the many-query optimisation that design demands [82]. The contrast with the conventional correlation is instructive: a correlation imposes a functional form chosen in advance, often a power-law product of dimensionless groups, and can represent only the relationships that form admits, so that a genuine dependence departing from the assumed shape is simply invisible to it, whereas a flexible data-driven model imposes no such prior form and adapts its shape to whatever the data reveal, capturing curvature, saturation and interaction that the rigid correlation cannot. This freedom from an imposed functional form is the deep source of the accuracy advantage, and it is most valuable precisely in the nanofluid domain, where the true relationships are complex, poorly understood and unlikely to conform to any simple analytical shape [82]. A second advantage is the capacity to integrate heterogeneous data from experiment, computation and the literature into a single model, extracting more value from the scattered database than any single source could provide, and it is worth noting that this capacity operates across the joint variation of variables in a way that the human mind, adept at reasoning about one or two variables at a time, cannot readily match; a data-driven model considers all the features simultaneously and captures the interactions among them, the ways in which the effect of one variable depends on the values of others, which are exactly the relationships that defeat the intuition and that the fixed functional forms of conventional correlations struggle to represent. A third is the ability to serve as a fast surrogate for expensive computation, reproducing the input–output behaviour of a validated computational model at a tiny fraction of its cost and thereby making feasible the exploration and optimisation that direct computation would render prohibitive.

The surrogate advantage deserves elaboration, because it reframes the relationship between computation and design in a way that is transformative in practice. A high-fidelity computation of a single heat-exchanger configuration may consume hours or days of processor time, a cost that permits the evaluation of a handful of candidate designs but forecloses the systematic search over hundreds or thousands of configurations that genuine optimisation requires. A surrogate trained on a designed set of such computations reproduces their input–output relationship in milliseconds, so that once the expense of generating the training data has been borne, the design space can be explored with a freedom that direct computation could never afford, and the optimisation methods described earlier can be unleashed upon a cheap surrogate objective rather than a ruinously expensive one [82]. The economic logic is that of a fixed investment in data generation followed by an essentially free stream of subsequent evaluations, and it is this logic, rather than any claim that the surrogate is more accurate than the computation it imitates, that justifies the surrogate paradigm and explains its growing prevalence in heat-exchanger design.

A further advantage, less often remarked but of considerable practical value, is the capacity of data-driven models to reconcile and synthesise the discordant results of a fragmented literature. The experimental record of nanofluid behaviour is scattered across many studies that differ in their materials, methods and conditions and that frequently disagree, and no single correlation can accommodate this diversity gracefully. A data-driven model trained on the pooled record can, if the sources of variation are represented among its features, learn to account for the differences between studies rather than being confounded by them, extracting from the collective database a coherent predictive relationship that no individual study could supply [82]. This synthesising capacity turns the very heterogeneity that frustrates conventional correlation into a resource, provided that the metadata describing how each measurement was obtained are preserved and supplied to the model, a proviso whose importance the following chapter's treatment of data preparation makes plain.

The limitations of the data-driven approach are equally real and must be understood if the methods are to be applied soundly rather than credulously. The foremost limitation is the dependence of a model's accuracy on the quantity and quality of its training data, encapsulated in the maxim that a model can be no better than the data from which it learns; a model trained on a scattered, biased or limited dataset will inherit those deficiencies, and no algorithmic sophistication can compensate for inadequate data [83]. A second limitation is the poor extrapolation of most data-driven models beyond the range of their training data, a consequence of their fitting the data rather than embodying the physics, which means that a model trained on one range of concentrations, temperatures or geometries cannot be trusted outside that range, a restriction of particular importance in a field where the design space is large and the data sparse. A third limitation is the opacity of many high-performing models, especially neural networks, whose learned relationships are difficult to interpret and to reconcile with physical understanding, raising concerns about trust and about the detection of physically absurd predictions.

The extrapolation limitation is so consequential for thermal-engineering practice that it warrants a fuller and more cautionary treatment. A data-driven model, having learned its relationship by fitting the training data, has no knowledge of the physics beyond what those data implicitly encode, and when it is queried outside the region the data cover it does not so much predict as guess, extending its fitted function into territory where nothing constrains it to remain physically sensible. The danger is aggravated by the fact that the model issues its extrapolated guess with the same outward confidence as its well-founded interpolations, offering no intrinsic warning that it has left the region of validity, so that an unwary user may accept a grossly wrong prediction as authoritative [83]. In a field where the design space is vast and the data sparse, the boundary of the training region is easily and unknowingly crossed, and the discipline of recording that boundary explicitly, of checking each query against it, and of preferring methods such as Gaussian processes that signal their own uncertainty is not a refinement but a necessity for responsible practice. The physical understanding cultivated in the earlier chapters provides the indispensable external check, allowing the engineer to recognise an extrapolated prediction that violates a known limit or trend even when the model itself gives no sign of distress.

The opacity of the more powerful models raises concerns that extend beyond mere inconvenience to the heart of engineering responsibility, for a design decision that cannot be explained is difficult to defend, to audit or to trust in a safety-relevant context. The field of interpretable and explainable machine learning has developed a range of techniques to peer into otherwise opaque models, from the feature-importance measures native to tree ensembles to post-hoc methods that probe how a trained model responds to systematic variation of its inputs, and these techniques can recover a partial account of what a model has learned and whether that account accords with physical expectation [83]. Yet such explanations are approximate and can themselves mislead, and the more fundamental safeguard is to prefer, wherever the loss of accuracy is tolerable, models whose structure is inherently interpretable, accepting a modest sacrifice of predictive performance in exchange for the transparency that engineering accountability demands. The choice between an opaque model of slightly higher accuracy and a transparent one of slightly lower accuracy is a genuine engineering trade-off, to be resolved by the requirements of the application rather than by an uncritical preference for the highest headline accuracy.

A fourth limitation, subtler than the others but no less real, concerns the treatment of uncertainty and the seductive precision of a point prediction. A model that returns a single number for a thermal conductivity or a Nusselt number conveys an impression of exactitude that the underlying data, scattered and uncertain as they are, cannot support, and a design founded on such point predictions inherits an unacknowledged risk. The remedy lies in methods that quantify and propagate uncertainty, reporting a range within which the true value is likely to lie rather than a single deceptive figure, so that the design process can account explicitly for what is not known [83]. The neglect of predictive uncertainty is among the more common shortcomings of the applied literature, and its correction, through the uncertainty-aware methods that the opportunities discussed next emphasise, is a mark of maturing practice. The opportunities that lie at the intersection of the advantages and limitations define the most promising directions for the field, and several deserve emphasis because they recur in the applications of the following chapters. The integration of physical knowledge into data-driven models, through physics-informed architectures that embed conservation laws or known limiting behaviours, through the use of dimensionless features that encode physical similarity, or through constraints that enforce physical plausibility, offers a route to models that combine the flexibility of data-driven learning with the reliability and extrapolation of physics, mitigating the principal limitations of the pure data-driven approach [84]. The quantification of predictive uncertainty, through Gaussian processes, ensembles or Bayesian neural networks, offers a route to models that report not only a prediction but a confidence, enabling the sound use of predictions in design and guiding the acquisition of new data toward the regions where the model is least certain. The coupling of surrogate models with optimisation, already noted, offers a route to the efficient multi-objective design that the conventional framework of Chapter 4 defines but cannot cheaply execute [85].

The integration of physical knowledge into data-driven models, which is perhaps the most fertile of these opportunities, can be pursued at several levels of intimacy, and it is worth distinguishing them because they demand differing degrees of effort and confer differing benefits. At the least invasive level, physical knowledge enters through the features, when the raw variables are combined into the dimensionless groups that thermal similarity prescribes, so that the model learns in terms of quantities that already embody the physics and that generalise across scales and fluids in the way the physics guarantees. At a deeper level, physical knowledge enters through constraints imposed upon the model, requiring its predictions to respect known bounds, monotonic trends or limiting behaviours, so that a prediction which would violate a physical law is forbidden by construction. At the deepest level, the governing equations themselves are woven into the training, the model being penalised not only for departing from the data but for violating the differential equations that the physics imposes, so that the learned function is simultaneously a fit to the data and an approximate solution of the physics [84]. Each level trades increased implementation effort for increased physical fidelity and improved extrapolation, and the selection among them is itself an engineering judgement, but the common principle, that the reliability of a data-driven model can be enhanced by informing it with what is already known, is one of the most important correctives to the limitations catalogued above.

The quantification of predictive uncertainty, the second major opportunity, transforms a model from an oracle issuing unqualified pronouncements into an advisor that reports both its estimate and its confidence, and this transformation has consequences that ramify through the whole design process. A prediction accompanied by a calibrated uncertainty can be used soundly in a design that must respect safety margins, for the margin can be set in relation to the reported uncertainty rather than guessed; it can guide the acquisition of new data toward the regions where the model is least certain, so that a limited budget for experiment or computation is spent where it most improves the model, a strategy known as active learning; and it can inform a robust optimisation that seeks designs which perform well not merely at the predicted operating point but across the range of uncertainty, hedging against the model's own fallibility [85]. The methods that furnish such uncertainty, whether the Gaussian processes noted earlier, the ensembles whose spread of predictions estimates their uncertainty, or the Bayesian treatments of neural networks, thus open design strategies that a bare point prediction forecloses, and their adoption is among the surest signs that a data-driven practice has matured beyond the pursuit of headline accuracy toward the responsible support of engineering decisions.

A third opportunity, distinct from these but increasingly prominent, lies in the transfer of knowledge between related problems, which addresses directly the chronic scarcity of data in any single nanofluid application. A model trained on the abundant data of one well-studied system can serve as a starting point that is then refined with the sparse data of a related but less-studied system, transferring the general relationships learned from the first to accelerate and stabilise the learning of the second, a strategy that can extract useful predictions from datasets far too small to support training from scratch [85]. The applicability of such transfer rests on a genuine physical kinship between the source and target problems, and its misuse where no such kinship exists can transfer misleading relationships as readily as helpful ones, so that here again physical judgement governs the sound exercise of a powerful technique. Taken together, the integration of physics, the quantification of uncertainty and the transfer of knowledge constitute a research frontier at which the limitations of the pure data-driven approach are progressively overcome by reuniting it with the physical understanding it initially set aside.

The proper place of artificial intelligence within thermal engineering is, finally, as a complement to physical understanding and mechanistic modelling rather than a replacement for them, and the recognition of this complementarity is the mark of sound practice. The physical understanding developed in the earlier chapters guides the formulation of the data-driven problem, informing the selection of features, the choice of dimensionless groups, the assessment of whether a prediction is physically reasonable, and the interpretation of what a model has learned. The mechanistic models of correlation and computation provide the training data, define the benchmarks against which data-driven models are judged, and cover the regimes in which data are absent and extrapolation is required. This division of labour is not a temporary expedient to be abandoned as data-driven methods mature but a durable feature of sound practice, for the physical laws that the mechanistic models encode will always extrapolate more reliably than any relationship inferred from finite data, and the data-driven models will always capture complexities that resist closed-form expression; neither can wholly supplant the other, and the recognition of their permanent complementarity is the conceptual foundation on which the integrated workflows of the following chapters are built [86]. The data-driven models, in turn, extend the reach of the mechanistic ones, learning relationships too complex for closed-form expression and providing the fast evaluations that optimisation demands [86]. It is this integrated practice, in which physical, computational and data-driven methods reinforce one another, that the remaining chapters develop and exemplify, first by establishing the fundamentals of machine learning in Chapter 7 and then by applying them to the prediction of nanofluid properties and heat-exchanger performance in Chapters 8 and 9, always with the understanding, established here, that the power of the data-driven approach is real but conditional, and that its sound exploitation requires the physical judgement that only a grounding in the fundamentals of heat transfer and nanofluids can supply.

The complementarity between data-driven and mechanistic approaches can be stated more concretely by considering how each compensates for the weaknesses of the other. The mechanistic models excel where the physics is understood and fail where it is obscure; the data-driven models excel where data are plentiful and fail where they are absent; and the two failure modes are, fortunately, largely disjoint, so that a mechanistic model can supply the physical structure and the extrapolative reach that a data-driven model lacks, while a data-driven model can supply the flexibility to capture relationships that no mechanistic model can express in closed form [86]. A workflow that exploits this complementarity might use a validated computation to generate training data across a designed range, a data-driven surrogate to learn the computation's behaviour cheaply, physical constraints to keep the surrogate honest where data are thin, and an uncertainty estimate to flag the queries that stray beyond the trustworthy region, each element covering a gap that the others would otherwise leave open. The construction of such integrated workflows, rather than the isolated application of any single method, is the practice toward which the field is converging and which the applications of the following chapters illustrate.

It is fitting to close this chapter with a caution against two opposite errors that beset the adoption of artificial intelligence in thermal engineering, for the avoidance of both is the essence of sound practice. The first error is an uncritical enthusiasm that treats data-driven methods as a panacea, applying them indiscriminately, accepting their predictions without scrutiny, and neglecting the physical understanding and experimental discipline on which their reliability ultimately depends; this error produces the plausible-looking but unsound results that discredit the methods and squander their promise. The second and opposite error is a reflexive scepticism that dismisses data-driven methods as mere curve-fitting devoid of physical content, and that thereby forgoes the genuine advantages they offer in precisely the high-dimensional, physically obscure problems where mechanistic modelling falters [86]. The mature position, which this book advocates throughout, steers between these errors by regarding artificial intelligence as a powerful but conditional instrument, whose power is realised only when it is wielded with an understanding of both its capabilities and its limits and in concert with the physical and computational methods that the earlier chapters established. It is with this balanced understanding in place that the reader is now equipped to study, in the following chapter, the fundamentals of machine learning in the detail that their sound application to heat-exchanger design requires.
