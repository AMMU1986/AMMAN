# Chapter 2. Nanofluids: Fundamentals and Thermophysical Properties

## Abstract

The performance of any convective heat exchanger is ultimately limited by the thermophysical properties of the working fluid, and among these properties thermal conductivity is frequently the most restrictive, because conventional heat transfer liquids such as water, ethylene glycol and mineral oils possess conductivities that are one to three orders of magnitude smaller than those of the metals and metal oxides from which heat-exchanger surfaces are made. Nanofluids, engineered suspensions of nanometre-scale solid particles in a base liquid, were conceived as a route to circumvent this limitation by dispersing a small volume fraction of a highly conducting solid throughout the fluid so as to raise its effective conductivity and, with it, its capacity for convective heat transport. This chapter provides the physical and materials foundation required to understand why nanofluids behave as they do and how their behaviour is measured and modelled. It surveys the classes of nanoparticle and base fluid in common use, describes the one-step and two-step synthesis routes and the compromises each entails, and explains the central importance of colloidal stability to any practical application. It then examines the four thermophysical properties that govern convective performance, namely thermal conductivity, dynamic viscosity, density and specific heat capacity, presenting the classical mixing rules and the more elaborate models that attempt to account for the anomalous behaviour that early experiments reported. The chapter gives particular attention to the mechanisms that have been proposed to explain conductivity enhancement, to the measurement techniques that underpin the experimental literature, and to the factors that determine whether a nanofluid remains a stable, well-dispersed suspension or degrades into an agglomerated and sedimenting slurry. The material prepares the reader to interpret the heat-transfer results of Chapter 3 and to appreciate why the data-driven property models of Chapter 8 have become an active field of research.

## 2.1 Classification and Preparation of Nanofluids

A nanofluid is defined as a colloidal suspension in which the dispersed phase consists of particles with at least one dimension smaller than approximately one hundred nanometres, suspended in a continuous liquid phase that serves as the heat transfer medium. The definition is deliberately broad, encompassing spherical particles, rods, tubes, platelets and more complex morphologies, as well as suspensions containing a single species of particle and hybrid suspensions containing two or more distinct particle types. The essential idea in every case is that the addition of a small volume fraction of a solid with favourable thermophysical properties, most importantly a high thermal conductivity, will confer on the resulting suspension properties intermediate between those of the pure liquid and those of the solid, but weighted so heavily toward the liquid, on account of the small solid fraction, that the suspension remains fluid and pumpable [15]. What distinguishes nanofluids from the earlier and largely unsuccessful attempts to suspend millimetre- and micrometre-scale particles in liquids is the dramatic reduction in particle size, which suppresses the rapid sedimentation, severe abrasion and channel clogging that plagued the coarser suspensions and which also, according to a substantial body of experimental evidence, produces conductivity enhancements larger than classical effective-medium theory would predict [16].

The historical trajectory that led to this definition is worth recalling, because it explains both the enthusiasm and the caution with which the field has developed. The suspension of solid particles in liquids to augment heat transfer is an old idea, pursued intermittently for well over a century, but every early attempt foundered on the same practical obstacles: particles in the millimetre and micrometre range settled under gravity within minutes to hours, they scoured the walls of pipes and the internals of pumps, and when they accumulated in bends and low-velocity regions they blocked the very passages they were meant to serve. The advent of reliable nanoparticle synthesis in the closing decades of the twentieth century changed the calculus decisively, because a particle two orders of magnitude smaller experiences a gravitational settling velocity smaller by four orders of magnitude, while the Brownian agitation that opposes settling becomes correspondingly more effective at keeping the particle aloft. The term nanofluid was coined to mark this qualitative shift, and the early reports of conductivity enhancements far exceeding the modest predictions of classical theory generated an intense wave of interest, some of which later proved to rest on measurements that were difficult to reproduce. The maturation of the field has consequently been as much a matter of methodological discipline, in the careful characterisation and reporting of what was actually suspended, as of new physics, and the sober modern consensus is that genuine enhancements exist and are useful but are frequently smaller and more conditional than the most optimistic early claims suggested [15].

It is useful, before turning to materials, to fix the physical scales that make the nanometre regime special. A particle one hundred nanometres in diameter contains on the order of tens of millions of atoms, of which a small but non-negligible fraction reside at the surface; as the diameter falls toward ten nanometres, the surface fraction rises steeply, so that surface energy, surface chemistry and interfacial phenomena come to dominate the behaviour of the particle in ways that have no counterpart in bulk matter. This large specific surface area is simultaneously the source of the favourable interfacial transport that nanofluids exploit and the driving force for the aggregation that threatens their stability, and much of the art of nanofluid engineering consists of harnessing the former while suppressing the latter. The same smallness that renders the particles effectively buoyant against gravity also renders them susceptible to the attractive van der Waals forces that act only over short ranges, so that two particles that approach closely tend strongly to adhere. The nanofluid designer therefore operates in a regime where the very properties that confer the desired benefits are inseparable from the properties that pose the central practical difficulty, a tension that recurs throughout this chapter [16].

The dispersed phase is drawn from several broad materials classes, each with characteristic advantages. Metallic nanoparticles such as copper, silver, gold and aluminium offer the highest intrinsic thermal conductivities and therefore the greatest potential for enhancement, but they are prone to oxidation, are comparatively expensive, and raise the density of the suspension appreciably. Metal-oxide nanoparticles, including aluminium oxide, copper oxide, titanium dioxide, silicon dioxide, iron oxide and zinc oxide, are the most widely studied because they are chemically stable, relatively inexpensive, readily available in controlled sizes and reasonably easy to disperse, even though their conductivities are lower than those of the parent metals. Carbon-based materials, notably carbon nanotubes in single- and multi-walled forms, graphene and its derivatives, and diamond nanoparticles, possess exceptionally high conductivities, especially along the axis of a nanotube or the plane of a graphene sheet, and can produce large enhancements at very low loadings, although their anisotropy, their tendency to entangle and their hydrophobicity present dispersion challenges [17]. A further and increasingly studied class comprises ceramic and semiconducting compounds beyond the common oxides, such as silicon carbide, aluminium nitride, boron nitride and various nitrides and carbides prized for their combination of high conductivity, chemical inertness and, in the case of the nitrides, electrical insulation that suits dielectric coolants; these materials occupy a middle ground between the metals and the oxides and are chosen when a specific balance of conductivity, stability and compatibility is sought. The selection of a dispersed phase is thus never a matter of maximising a single figure of merit but of reconciling several: the intrinsic conductivity that sets the ceiling on enhancement, the density that governs both settling and the parasitic mass the pump must move, the specific heat that determines how much thermal energy the particles themselves store, the chemical stability that decides whether the particles survive the service environment without oxidising or dissolving, and the cost, which for the noble metals can be prohibitive at the loadings required for a useful effect. The oxides have come to dominate the applied literature not because they are the best on any one of these axes but because they are acceptable on all of them, a pattern that recurs whenever a technology moves from laboratory demonstration toward practical deployment [18].

The intrinsic conductivity of the solid phase, though it sets the theoretical ceiling on enhancement, is a far weaker lever in practice than a naive reading of the mixing rules would suggest, and appreciating why is important for realistic expectations. Because the volume fractions of practical interest are small, typically well below five per cent and often below one, the effective conductivity of the suspension is dominated by the continuous liquid phase, and the marginal benefit of replacing a solid of four hundred watts per metre-kelvin with one of four thousand is far smaller than the tenfold ratio of their conductivities implies. The dilute effective-medium relations show that once the solid conductivity exceeds the liquid conductivity by a factor of roughly twenty, further increases yield rapidly diminishing returns, because the limiting resistance to heat flow lies in the liquid that surrounds each particle rather than in the particle itself. This is why oxides of comparatively modest conductivity can perform nearly as well as metals of far higher conductivity at equal loading, and why the interfacial and dynamic mechanisms discussed in Section 2.2, rather than the bulk conductivity of the solid, are frequently the decisive factors in the enhancement actually observed [17]. The base fluid is selected according to the operating temperature range and the application. Water is the preferred base fluid wherever its freezing and boiling points are compatible with the service, because it combines a high specific heat and a comparatively high conductivity with low cost, low toxicity and low viscosity. Ethylene glycol and propylene glycol, alone or in aqueous mixtures, extend the usable range to sub-zero temperatures and are standard in automotive and building-services coolants, at the cost of higher viscosity and lower conductivity than water. Engine oils, transformer oils and other mineral and synthetic oils serve high-temperature and dielectric applications, and refrigerants form the base for two-phase nanofluid studies. Hybrid nanofluids, which combine two nanoparticle species such as an oxide with a carbon material, or a metal with an oxide, aim to exploit complementary attributes, for example combining the high conductivity of one component with the stability or low cost of another, and have attracted growing interest as a means of tailoring properties to a specific duty [18].

The choice of base fluid carries consequences that extend well beyond the operating temperature range, because the base fluid establishes the physical and chemical environment in which the particles must remain dispersed and through which the enhancement mechanisms operate. A base fluid of high polarity, such as water, supports electrostatic stabilisation because its high dielectric constant screens the surface charges of the particles and sustains the diffuse ionic double layer on which electrostatic repulsion depends; a non-polar oil, by contrast, cannot support significant surface charge and must rely almost entirely on steric stabilisation by adsorbed molecules. The viscosity of the base fluid sets the baseline against which the particle-induced viscosity increase is measured and thus governs the hydraulic penalty, while its own conductivity sets the baseline against which enhancement is judged, so that the same absolute increase in conductivity represents a larger relative gain in a low-conductivity oil than in water. These considerations mean that a particle-liquid pairing that performs admirably in one base fluid may perform poorly in another, and that reports of enhancement must always be read together with the identity of the base fluid rather than attributed to the particle alone [19]. The glycols and their aqueous mixtures occupy an especially important practical niche because so much of the world's installed heat-transfer equipment, from automotive radiators to building chilled-water loops, operates with them, and a nanofluid additive that could be introduced into an existing glycol-based system without redesign would have a large and immediate market, which is one reason the glycol nanofluids have received disproportionate applied attention.

Hybrid nanofluids merit closer examination because they represent the frontier at which the tailoring of properties becomes most deliberate and most complex. The rationale for combining two particle species is that the attributes desired of a nanofluid, high conductivity, low viscosity increase, robust stability and low cost, are seldom all present in a single material, so that a judiciously chosen pair may supply collectively what neither supplies alone. A common strategy pairs a small quantity of a very high conductivity carbon material, which provides percolating conductive pathways, with a larger quantity of an inexpensive and easily stabilised oxide, which provides bulk and assists dispersion; another pairs a metal with its own oxide to combine high conductivity with the stability conferred by the oxide surface. The difficulty is that the property behaviour of a hybrid is not in general the simple sum or average of its constituents, because the two particle species interact with one another as well as with the fluid, sometimes stabilising one another and sometimes accelerating mutual aggregation, so that the composition space of hybrids is both large and non-additive. This combinatorial richness makes hybrids a natural proving ground for the data-driven methods introduced later in the book, since the number of candidate compositions vastly exceeds what can be examined experimentally [18].

Two synthesis routes dominate the preparation of nanofluids, and the choice between them has a decisive influence on the quality and stability of the product. The two-step method, illustrated on the left of Figure 2.1, first produces dry nanoparticles by an independent process such as inert-gas condensation, chemical vapour deposition or mechanical milling, and then disperses these pre-formed particles into the base fluid with the aid of intensive agitation, ultrasonication and, frequently, chemical surfactants or pH adjustment to stabilise the suspension. The two-step method is economical and scalable because the nanoparticle production and the dispersion are decoupled and the former can exploit the economies of commercial powder manufacture, but it suffers from a strong tendency for the dry particles to agglomerate before and during dispersion, so that the suspensions it yields are often less stable and contain larger effective particle clusters than their one-step counterparts [19].

The mechanics of the two-step dispersion process reward closer attention, because the quality of the final suspension is determined largely by how effectively the agglomerates present in the dry powder are broken apart and prevented from re-forming. Commercial nanopowders, however carefully manufactured, arrive as loose agglomerates and harder aggregates in which the primary particles are held together by van der Waals forces and, in the case of aggregates, by sintered necks formed during synthesis; simple stirring disperses the powder into the liquid but leaves these clusters largely intact, so that the effective particle size in a stirred-only suspension may be an order of magnitude larger than the nominal primary size quoted by the supplier. Ultrasonication supplies the concentrated mechanical energy needed to fracture the softer agglomerates, through the violent collapse of cavitation bubbles that generates local shear and shock, and its effect is progressive: the mean cluster size falls with sonication time toward an asymptote set by the strength of the aggregates and the intensity of the field. There is, however, an optimum beyond which further sonication is counterproductive, both because the energy input heats the fluid and can degrade surfactants and because, once the driving field is removed, the freshly exposed and highly energetic particle surfaces may re-aggregate more rapidly than before. The two-step route is therefore not a single procedure but a sequence of coupled choices, of surfactant, of pH, of sonication power and duration, whose joint optimisation for a given powder and fluid is itself a small research undertaking [20].

The one-step method, shown on the right of Figure 2.1, synthesises the nanoparticles directly within the base fluid, so that the particles never exist in a dry, agglomeration-prone state; representative techniques include the direct condensation of a metal vapour into a flowing low-vapour-pressure liquid and various wet-chemical reductions carried out in the base fluid itself. Because the particles are formed in situ and immediately stabilised by the surrounding liquid, one-step suspensions are typically more stable and better dispersed than two-step suspensions, which makes the one-step route attractive for fundamental studies where dispersion quality must be controlled. Its disadvantages are the difficulty of removing reaction by-products, the restriction to low-vapour-pressure base fluids in the vapour-condensation variant, and the poor scalability and high cost that at present confine it largely to the laboratory [20]. The practical consequence of these trade-offs is that the great majority of applied heat-transfer studies use two-step suspensions and devote considerable effort to stabilisation, whereas one-step suspensions are reserved for investigations in which dispersion quality is itself the object of study.

The distinction between the two routes has an epistemic dimension that is easily overlooked but that bears directly on the interpretation of the literature. Because one-step suspensions are typically better dispersed and contain smaller and more uniform particles, measurements made on them tend to reflect the intrinsic behaviour of well-separated nanoparticles, whereas measurements on two-step suspensions reflect the behaviour of a population that includes clusters of ill-defined size and morphology. When a two-step suspension exhibits an enhancement larger than a one-step suspension of nominally the same composition, the difference is frequently attributable not to any superiority of the two-step material but to the clustering that the two-step route promotes, clustering that can raise conductivity through the percolation mechanism discussed later while simultaneously undermining stability. The apparent paradox that a less stable suspension may show a larger conductivity enhancement is thus resolved by recognising that the two are manifestations of the same underlying aggregation, and it serves as a warning against optimising conductivity in isolation from stability. A responsible characterisation programme records not merely the nominal composition but the actual dispersion state, so that an enhancement can be attributed to its true cause rather than to the nominal recipe [21].

Beyond the two dominant routes, a number of intermediate and specialised techniques have been developed to address particular difficulties. Microfluidic and continuous-flow reactors offer finer control over particle nucleation and growth than batch synthesis and can produce narrow size distributions in a scalable manner, narrowing the historic gap between the quality of one-step and the throughput of two-step methods. Surface functionalisation performed during or immediately after synthesis, rather than as a separate downstream step, can stabilise particles before they have any opportunity to aggregate, which is especially valuable for carbon nanomaterials whose native surfaces resist wetting by water. The persistence of a plurality of methods, none of which is optimal on every axis of cost, quality and scale, is characteristic of a field still maturing toward industrial practice, and it reinforces the theme that the properties of a nanofluid are inseparable from the history of its preparation [20].

The characterisation of a prepared nanofluid is as important as its synthesis, because the effective properties depend not only on the nominal composition but on the actual state of dispersion. Particle size and its distribution are measured by dynamic light scattering, which infers a hydrodynamic diameter from the Brownian motion of the particles, and by transmission and scanning electron microscopy, which image the particles directly but require dilution and drying that may not represent the suspended state. The concentration is expressed either as a volume fraction or as a mass fraction, and the conversion between them requires the densities of both phases; careless reporting of concentration is a recurrent source of confusion in the literature. Zeta potential, discussed in Section 2.3, quantifies the electrostatic stabilisation of the suspension, and spectroscopic and gravimetric sedimentation tests assess stability over time [21]. Only when these characterisation data accompany a property or heat-transfer measurement can the result be interpreted with confidence, and the absence of adequate characterisation is one reason for the scatter that pervades the experimental database and that motivates the statistical and machine-learning treatments introduced later.

## 2.2 Thermophysical Properties and Their Measurement

Four thermophysical properties govern the convective performance of a nanofluid, and each must be known as a function of concentration and temperature before the heat-transfer behaviour can be predicted. Thermal conductivity determines the conductive contribution to convection and appears in the Nusselt number and the overall coefficient; dynamic viscosity governs the pressure drop and the pumping power and enters the Reynolds and Prandtl numbers; density affects the mass flow rate, the momentum and the buoyancy; and specific heat capacity determines the thermal energy that a given mass of fluid can transport for a given temperature change. Because these properties enter the governing dimensionless groups in different combinations, an enhancement in one may be partly or wholly offset by an adverse change in another, and a proper assessment of a nanofluid requires all four to be considered together rather than conductivity in isolation [22].

The manner in which the four properties combine into the dimensionless groups that govern convection deserves to be made explicit, because it reveals why an enhancement in one property does not translate directly into an enhancement in performance. The Prandtl number, which measures the relative diffusion of momentum and heat, rises when viscosity rises and falls when conductivity rises, so that the two dominant nanofluid property changes act in opposition within it; the Reynolds number, which measures the ratio of inertial to viscous forces, falls as viscosity rises at fixed mass flow, tending to move the flow toward the laminar regime where heat transfer is weaker. The Peclet number, the product of Reynolds and Prandtl numbers, measures the relative importance of advective and diffusive transport of the particles themselves and governs the strength of the particle-migration effects discussed in the following chapter. Because a single physical change, the addition of particles, alters several of these groups simultaneously and in partly opposing directions, the net effect on heat transfer can only be established by carrying all four property changes through the governing relations together, and the intuition that a more conductive fluid must transfer heat better is unreliable precisely because it ignores the accompanying penalties in viscosity and, through it, in the Reynolds and Prandtl numbers [23].

It is also important to recognise that the four properties do not vary independently as composition and temperature change, because they share common microscopic origins in the state of dispersion. A rise in temperature, for instance, simultaneously lowers the viscosity, intensifies the Brownian motion that contributes to conductivity, and may accelerate the aggregation that alters both, so that the temperature dependences of the properties are coupled rather than separable. Aggregation raises the effective conductivity through percolation while raising the viscosity through the increased effective volume fraction of the clusters and their entrained liquid, coupling the two properties most consequential for performance. This interdependence is one reason that attempts to model each property with an independent correlation, and then to combine the results, can accumulate error and violate the physical constraints that link the properties, and it is an argument, developed in later chapters, for models that treat the properties jointly and respect their common dependence on the underlying dispersion state [22].

Thermal conductivity is the property that first attracted attention to nanofluids and remains the most intensively studied. The classical baseline is the effective-medium theory of Maxwell, derived for dilute suspensions of non-interacting spheres, which predicts an enhancement that depends only on the conductivities of the two phases and the volume fraction and which, for the small fractions typical of nanofluids, is modest and approximately linear in concentration. Hamilton and Crosser extended Maxwell's result to non-spherical particles by introducing a shape factor, capturing the empirical observation that elongated and platelet particles enhance conductivity more effectively than spheres at equal loading [23]. The experimental literature repeatedly reports conductivity enhancements that exceed the predictions of classical effective-medium theory, sometimes substantially, and the explanation of this anomalous enhancement has generated a large body of mechanistic work. Four mechanisms are most often invoked. The first is Brownian motion of the nanoparticles, which is thought to contribute both directly, through the micro-convection that the moving particles induce in the surrounding liquid, and indirectly, by promoting particle collisions and clustering. The second is the formation of an ordered nanolayer of liquid molecules at the particle surface, a region of higher local conductivity than the bulk liquid that effectively enlarges the particle and increases the volume fraction of the conducting phase. The third is the clustering or aggregation of particles into fractal structures that provide percolating paths of high conductivity through the suspension, a mechanism that can enhance conductivity at the expense of stability. The fourth is ballistic and near-field phonon transport within and between closely spaced particles [24].

Each of these mechanisms carries testable signatures, and the effort to confirm or refute them by their predicted dependences has structured much of the mechanistic debate. The Brownian-motion hypothesis predicts a strong dependence of enhancement on temperature and an inverse dependence on particle size, because smaller and warmer particles diffuse more rapidly; experiments do commonly show that enhancement grows with temperature and with decreasing size, lending the hypothesis support, yet quantitative estimates of the micro-convective contribution frequently fall short of the observed enhancement by a wide margin, suggesting that Brownian motion alone cannot be the whole story. The nanolayer hypothesis predicts an enhancement that grows as the ratio of layer thickness to particle radius grows, and therefore as particle size falls, a dependence broadly consistent with observation, but the thickness and conductivity of the postulated ordered layer are difficult to measure independently and are usually inferred by fitting, which robs the hypothesis of predictive force. The aggregation hypothesis predicts that enhancement should correlate with the degree of clustering and hence, paradoxically, with declining stability, and it is supported by the frequent observation that freshly sonicated, well-dispersed samples show smaller enhancements than the same samples after partial aggregation. The phonon-transport hypothesis, drawn from the physics of solid nanocomposites, is most relevant at high loadings and small interparticle spacings that lie outside the dilute regime of most nanofluids. The picture that emerges is not of one correct mechanism and three incorrect ones but of several mechanisms operating together, with their relative weights shifting according to material, size, temperature and dispersion state, which is precisely why a single closed-form model has proved so elusive [24].

The practical upshot of this mechanistic complexity is a humbling one for the correlation-builder. A model calibrated on one material system, at one range of sizes and temperatures and one dispersion state, embeds the particular blend of mechanisms operative in that system, and there is no assurance that the same blend, and hence the same functional dependences, will govern a different system. This is the deep reason that the many empirical conductivity correlations in the literature agree tolerably with the data on which they were fitted yet diverge sharply when extrapolated, and it distinguishes the conductivity problem, where the underlying physics is contested, from the density problem, where a single conservation-based rule suffices universally. The recognition that the conductivity enhancement is a superposition of mechanisms with system-dependent weights is the physical justification for turning to models that learn those weights implicitly from data rather than positing them in advance [23]. The relative importance of these mechanisms remains debated, and no single model incorporating them has achieved universal predictive success, a state of affairs that directly motivates the data-driven conductivity models developed in Chapter 8. Dynamic viscosity is the property that most directly penalises the use of nanofluids, because the addition of particles invariably raises the viscosity and hence the pumping power, and the increase is often proportionally larger than the accompanying conductivity gain. The classical baseline is the Einstein relation for dilute suspensions of rigid spheres, which predicts a viscosity increase linear in volume fraction with a coefficient of two and one half; the Brinkman and Batchelor extensions add higher-order terms to account for hydrodynamic interactions between particles at larger fractions. Measured nanofluid viscosities, however, frequently exceed even these extended predictions, particularly at higher concentrations and lower temperatures and for non-spherical particles, and the viscosity generally depends on temperature, shear rate, particle size and the degree of aggregation in ways that no single closed-form model captures reliably [25]. Figure 2.3 shows the characteristic decrease of relative viscosity with temperature and its increase with concentration, a behaviour that has direct consequences for the pumping penalty at the low temperatures encountered during cold start-up.

The failure of the classical viscosity relations to predict measured nanofluid viscosities has origins that parallel, but are not identical to, those of the conductivity problem. The Einstein relation and its extensions treat the particles as rigid, isolated, spherical and neutrally interacting, and they account for the viscosity increase solely through the perturbation that each particle imposes on the surrounding flow field. Real nanoparticles violate every one of these assumptions to some degree: they are frequently non-spherical, so that their rotation in shear dissipates additional energy; they are charged, so that the deformation of their ionic double layers under shear adds an electroviscous contribution absent from the neutral theory; and above all they aggregate, so that the entity that perturbs the flow is not the primary particle but a cluster whose effective volume, swollen by the liquid immobilised within its porous structure, greatly exceeds the volume of solid it contains. This last effect is the dominant reason that measured viscosities exceed the predictions of the rigid-sphere theories, because the effective volume fraction that governs viscosity is the fraction occupied by clusters and their entrained liquid rather than the nominal fraction of solid, and it grows disproportionately as aggregation proceeds [25]. The temperature dependence of viscosity, which follows the strongly decreasing trend of the base liquid modulated by the temperature dependence of aggregation, compounds the difficulty, and the occasional appearance of shear-thinning or yield-stress behaviour at higher loadings places some nanofluids outside the Newtonian framework altogether.

The consequences of the viscosity increase for the energy economy of a nanofluid system are severe enough to warrant emphasis here, in advance of the fuller treatment in later chapters. Because the pumping power scales with the product of pressure drop and flow rate, and the pressure drop rises with viscosity, a viscosity increase translates more or less directly into an increase in the parasitic power that the pump must supply, and this power is drawn at the low thermodynamic quality of shaft work rather than the potentially low quality of the heat being managed. A nanofluid that raises conductivity by a tenth while raising viscosity by a third may therefore consume more additional pumping energy than it saves in heat-transfer surface, and whether it represents a net gain depends on the detailed balance developed in Chapters 3 and 4. The asymmetry between the typically modest conductivity gain and the frequently larger viscosity penalty is the single most important reason that the enthusiasm of the early conductivity-focused literature has given way to a more measured contemporary assessment, and it is why viscosity, though less glamorous than conductivity, is arguably the more decisive property in determining whether a nanofluid earns its place in a real system [26].

Density and specific heat capacity are, by contrast, well described by simple mixing rules that follow from mass and energy conservation, and they exhibit far less anomalous behaviour than conductivity and viscosity. The density of a nanofluid is the volume-fraction-weighted average of the densities of the two phases, a relation confirmed by measurement to within experimental uncertainty across the range of concentrations of practical interest. The specific heat capacity, when expressed on a mass basis, follows from an energy balance that weights the two phases by their mass fractions, although some authors employ a simpler volume-weighted expression that is thermodynamically less consistent and can introduce discernible error at higher loadings. Because nanoparticles generally have a lower specific heat than water, the addition of particles reduces the specific heat of the suspension, which slightly diminishes its capacity to transport thermal energy per unit mass and must be weighed against the conductivity gain [26]. The reliability of these mixing rules for density and specific heat, in contrast to the difficulty of predicting conductivity and viscosity, is itself instructive: it shows that the anomalies of nanofluid behaviour are concentrated in the two properties most sensitive to the microscopic state of dispersion, and it explains why data-driven models are most needed precisely for conductivity and viscosity.

The measurement of nanofluid properties demands care because the small enhancements involved are easily obscured by experimental artefacts. Thermal conductivity is most commonly measured by the transient hot-wire method, in which a thin wire immersed in the fluid is heated by a known power and its temperature rise, inferred from its resistance, is analysed to extract the conductivity; the method is fast, which limits the influence of natural convection and sedimentation, but it requires that the wire be electrically insulated when used with conducting fluids and that the measurement window be short enough to precede significant particle settling. Steady-state methods and the transient plane-source technique are also used. Viscosity is measured with rotational and capillary viscometers and, increasingly, with rheometers that resolve any shear-rate dependence, which is important because some nanofluids exhibit non-Newtonian behaviour at higher concentrations. Density is obtained by pycnometry and specific heat by differential scanning calorimetry [27]. The transient hot-wire method deserves particular scrutiny because it dominates the conductivity literature and because its very virtues can become vices in careless hands. Its short measurement window, typically a fraction of a second to a few seconds, is a deliberate defence against natural convection, which would otherwise inflate the apparent conductivity by superimposing an advective transport on the conductive transport the method is meant to isolate; the same brevity guards against the sedimentation that would change the sample during a longer measurement. Yet the analysis assumes an ideal line source in an infinite medium, and departures from that ideal, through the finite length and heat capacity of the wire, through the insulating coating required when the fluid conducts electricity, and through any residual convection at longer times, all bias the result, so that the reliability of a hot-wire measurement rests on the restriction of the analysis to the time interval over which the ideal model holds. When different laboratories analyse different time windows, or fail to suppress convection, or use samples in different states of aggregation, their results on nominally identical fluids can diverge by margins that dwarf the enhancement being sought, and this methodological variance is a leading contributor to the notorious scatter of the conductivity database [24].

The measurement of specific heat illustrates a subtler source of error, namely the propagation of an inconsistent mixing rule into the reduction of the raw data. Some investigators, having measured a heat-capacity signal, report a specific heat computed from a volume-weighted rule rather than the thermodynamically consistent mass-weighted rule, and because the two rules diverge increasingly at higher loadings, the reported values embed a systematic and avoidable error whose sign and magnitude depend on a choice of formula rather than on any physical difference. Similar inconsistencies attend the reporting of concentration, which may be quoted as a volume or a mass fraction without clear indication of which, and the conversion between them, requiring the densities of both phases, is a recurrent site of arithmetic error. These are not exotic difficulties but mundane ones, and their prevalence underscores a point that recurs throughout the book: the quality of a property database is limited at least as much by the discipline of measurement and reporting as by the subtlety of the underlying physics, and any model, mechanistic or data-driven, is ultimately hostage to the fidelity of the numbers it is given [27]. The scatter among reported property values for nominally identical nanofluids, which can reach tens of per cent, arises from differences in preparation, dispersion state, characterisation and measurement technique, and it is this scatter, as much as the complexity of the underlying physics, that has made accurate property prediction such a persistent challenge and has drawn researchers toward the machine-learning methods that later chapters describe.

## 2.3 Stability, Agglomeration, and Factors Affecting Nanofluid Performance

The single most important practical determinant of whether a nanofluid can be used in a real heat exchanger is its colloidal stability, that is, its ability to remain a uniformly dispersed suspension over the time scales and under the conditions of service. An unstable nanofluid, in which the particles aggregate and settle, loses its property enhancement as the conducting phase migrates out of suspension, and the settled deposits can foul surfaces, block passages and abrade pumps and valves, negating the very benefits that motivated the use of the nanofluid in the first place. Stability is therefore not a secondary concern to be addressed after the thermophysical properties have been optimised but a primary constraint that shapes the choice of particle, base fluid, concentration and stabilisation strategy from the outset [28]. The distinction between the pristine, freshly prepared state in which most laboratory property measurements are made and the aged, partially aggregated state in which a fluid actually operates is one of the most consequential gaps between the research literature and engineering practice.

The time scales over which stability must be maintained differ sharply between the laboratory and the field, and this difference is a principal reason that promising laboratory results so often disappoint in service. A property measurement or a short heat-transfer test may be completed within minutes or hours of preparation, an interval over which even a marginally stable suspension retains most of its dispersion; a heat exchanger in continuous industrial service, by contrast, may be expected to operate for years, subjecting the fluid to thousands of thermal cycles, prolonged exposure to elevated temperature, repeated passage through pumps and valves, and the slow chemical evolution of surfaces and additives. Aggregation that is negligible over an afternoon may be decisive over a year, and a stabilisation strategy adequate for a test may prove inadequate for a service life, so that the demonstration of enhancement in the laboratory establishes only that a benefit is achievable in principle, not that it is durable in practice. The assessment of long-term stability under realistic thermal and hydraulic cycling is accordingly among the most important and least frequently reported aspects of nanofluid research, and its neglect is one of the reasons that the translation of nanofluids from laboratory to industry has been slower than the early enthusiasm anticipated [28].

The physics of colloidal stability is governed by the balance between the attractive and repulsive forces acting between suspended particles, a balance formalised in the Derjaguin–Landau–Verwey–Overbeek theory. The attractive force is the van der Waals interaction, which is always present and which drives particles together and, once they are in contact, holds them in aggregates. Opposing it are repulsive forces, of which two are commonly exploited. Electrostatic repulsion arises when the particle surfaces carry like electrical charges, surrounded by diffuse clouds of counter-ions in the liquid, and its strength is characterised by the zeta potential, the electrical potential at the plane of shear around a particle. Steric repulsion arises when adsorbed polymer or surfactant molecules on adjacent particles overlap and resist compression. A suspension is stable when the repulsive forces dominate at the relevant interparticle separations, so that the thermal energy of Brownian motion is insufficient to drive particles over the energy barrier into the primary attractive minimum where aggregation occurs [29].

The shape of the interaction-energy curve that the Derjaguin–Landau–Verwey–Overbeek theory predicts repays interpretation, because its features map directly onto the observed behaviour of real suspensions. As two particles approach from a distance, the summed potential first passes through a shallow secondary minimum, at which particles may associate loosely and reversibly into flocs that can be redispersed by mild agitation; nearer still it rises to a maximum, the energy barrier whose height, relative to the thermal energy available from Brownian collisions, governs the rate at which particles reach the deep primary minimum of irreversible aggregation. A high barrier renders the suspension kinetically stable even though the aggregated state is thermodynamically favoured, which is the ordinary condition of a well-formulated nanofluid: it is not that aggregation is impossible but that it is slow on the time scale of use. Raising the barrier, by increasing surface charge or thickening the adsorbed steric layer, therefore extends the useful life of the suspension without altering the fact that aggregation is the ultimate destination. This kinetic conception of stability, in which the practical question is the rate of aggregation rather than its thermodynamic possibility, is essential to understanding why stability is always a matter of degree and of time scale rather than an absolute property [29].

The theory also clarifies the dramatic sensitivity of aqueous suspensions to dissolved salts, a sensitivity of considerable practical importance because real coolant water is rarely pure. Dissolved ions compress the diffuse double layer that carries the electrostatic repulsion, shrinking its range so that the repulsive barrier falls; beyond a critical ionic concentration the barrier vanishes and rapid aggregation ensues. A nanofluid stabilised electrostatically in deionised water may therefore aggregate promptly when introduced into a system carrying the dissolved solids of ordinary industrial water, a failure mode that laboratory tests in pure water do not reveal. This vulnerability is one of the strongest practical arguments for steric or electrosteric stabilisation, which depends on the physical bulk of adsorbed molecules rather than on a charge cloud that salts can collapse, and it exemplifies the general lesson that a stabilisation strategy must be matched not merely to the particle and the base fluid but to the full chemistry of the service environment [30].

The zeta potential provides the most widely used quantitative index of electrostatic stability, and its dependence on pH is central to the control of aqueous nanofluids. At the isoelectric point, the pH at which the net surface charge and hence the zeta potential is zero, electrostatic repulsion vanishes and the suspension is least stable, aggregating and settling rapidly. As the pH is moved away from the isoelectric point in either direction, the magnitude of the zeta potential grows and stability improves; a common rule of thumb regards a magnitude in excess of roughly thirty millivolts as indicative of a moderately stable suspension. Figure 2.4 shows the characteristic dependence of zeta potential on pH, with the isoelectric point marking the minimum-stability condition and the stable regimes lying at more acidic and more alkaline pH. Adjusting the pH away from the isoelectric point is consequently one of the simplest and most effective stabilisation strategies for oxide nanofluids [30].

The zeta potential, for all its usefulness, must be interpreted with an awareness of its limitations, lest it be mistaken for a complete measure of stability. It quantifies only the electrostatic contribution and is silent about steric stabilisation, so that a sterically stabilised suspension may be robustly stable at a zeta potential near zero, contradicting the naive rule of thumb. It is measured on dilute samples and at a particular ionic strength and temperature, and its value may differ in the concentrated, salt-bearing, elevated-temperature conditions of service. And it characterises the individual particle rather than the population, giving no direct indication of the cluster-size distribution that ultimately determines both the property enhancement and the settling behaviour. A responsible stability assessment therefore combines the zeta potential with direct observation of the cluster size, by dynamic light scattering, and with the sedimentation and spectroscopic tests that follow the suspension over the relevant time scale, treating the zeta potential as one informative indicator among several rather than as a sufficient criterion in itself [31]. The adjustment of pH away from the isoelectric point, though simple and effective for laboratory oxide suspensions, also has practical limits, because a system may impose its own constraints on pH through corrosion considerations or the chemistry of other fluids it contacts, so that the freedom to tune pH for stability is often smaller in an engineered system than in a beaker.

Three broad strategies are employed to enhance and maintain stability, and they are frequently combined. The first is physical dispersion, in which mechanical energy is supplied to break up aggregates, most commonly through ultrasonication with a probe or bath; the intensity and duration of sonication influence the resulting particle-size distribution, with insufficient sonication leaving large aggregates and excessive sonication risking particle damage or re-aggregation once the energy input ceases. The second is the addition of surfactants, amphiphilic molecules that adsorb on the particle surfaces and provide steric or electrosteric repulsion; the choice of surfactant must match the surface chemistry of the particle and the polarity of the base fluid, and an excess of surfactant can generate foam, raise viscosity and even degrade thermal performance, so an optimum dosage exists. The third is surface functionalisation, in which the particle surface is chemically modified to render it more compatible with the base fluid, a route especially important for carbon nanomaterials whose native hydrophobicity otherwise prevents their dispersion in water [31]. Each strategy has costs as well as benefits, and the design of a stable nanofluid is an exercise in balancing dispersion quality against viscosity penalty, cost and the risk of interfering with the heat-transfer mechanism.

The use of surfactants illustrates with particular clarity the conflicting demands that stabilisation places on the designer. A surfactant that adsorbs strongly enough to confer durable steric repulsion also coats the particle in a layer of organic material whose own thermal conductivity is low, interposing an additional interfacial resistance between the conducting particle and the liquid and thereby diminishing, at the microscopic scale, the very enhancement the particle was added to provide. An excess of surfactant, beyond the amount needed to coat the particles, remains free in solution, where it raises the viscosity, may generate troublesome foam under the agitation of a pump, and can even reduce the surface tension in ways that alter boiling behaviour in two-phase systems. There exists, therefore, an optimum surfactant dosage that secures adequate stability with the least penalty to the thermal and hydraulic performance, and locating that optimum is a delicate exercise specific to each particle-fluid-surfactant combination. The thermal degradation of surfactants at elevated temperature adds a further constraint, because a surfactant that stabilises a fluid at room temperature may decompose in service, releasing its stabilising grip precisely when the fluid is hottest and aggregation most rapid, which is one reason that high-temperature nanofluids often rely on surface functionalisation, more robust than physisorbed surfactant, or on inherently stable particle chemistries [31].

It is worth drawing together the several factors into an integrated picture of how a stable, high-performing nanofluid is actually formulated, because the individual influences interact in ways that a factor-by-factor account can obscure. The designer begins by selecting a particle material for its conductivity, cost and chemical compatibility, and a base fluid for its temperature range and its ability to support the intended stabilisation mechanism; chooses a particle size small enough to resist settling and to engage the interfacial enhancement mechanisms yet not so small that aggregation becomes uncontrollable; selects a concentration high enough to secure a useful enhancement yet below the loading at which viscosity and aggregation penalties overwhelm the benefit; and then commits to a stabilisation strategy, or a combination of strategies, matched to the surface chemistry of the particle, the polarity and ionic strength of the fluid, and the temperature and duration of service. Each of these choices constrains the others, so that the formulation is not a sequence of independent optimisations but a single coupled problem, and the space of feasible formulations is a bounded and irregular region within a high-dimensional space of composition and process variables. The size and complexity of that space, together with the scatter of the data that populate it, is the recurring motivation for the data-driven methods that the book develops, and it is the concrete sense in which the physical understanding of this chapter defines the problem that those methods are designed to solve [32].

Beyond stability, a number of factors modulate the heat-transfer performance of a nanofluid, and understanding them is essential to interpreting the sometimes contradictory results of the experimental literature. Particle concentration is the most obvious: increasing it raises the conductivity and hence, in principle, the heat transfer, but it also raises the viscosity and the pumping penalty, and beyond a certain loading the viscosity rises so steeply, and the risk of aggregation grows so large, that the net benefit declines and may become negative. Particle size influences both the conductivity, through the surface-area-to-volume ratio and the nanolayer and Brownian-motion mechanisms, and the stability, since smaller particles resist sedimentation more effectively but aggregate more readily owing to their higher surface energy. Particle shape, as noted in connection with the Hamilton–Crosser model, affects conductivity through the shape factor, with high-aspect-ratio particles enhancing conductivity more than spheres at equal loading but also raising viscosity more steeply [32]. The influence of shape thus embodies in miniature the central trade-off of the entire subject, for the very geometric feature that lengthens the conductive pathways through the suspension also increases the hydrodynamic resistance the particle presents to the flow, so that the morphology which maximises conductivity is seldom the morphology which maximises net performance once the pumping penalty is weighed. Temperature affects every property and generally improves the relative performance of nanofluids because it intensifies Brownian motion and reduces viscosity, though it may also accelerate aggregation. The dual role of temperature is a good example of the coupling that pervades the subject: the same rise in temperature that lowers viscosity and thereby eases the pumping penalty also lowers the height of the electrostatic energy barrier, through its effect on the double layer, and increases the frequency of the Brownian collisions that carry particles over that barrier, so that the thermal condition most favourable to instantaneous performance may be least favourable to long-term stability. A design that exploits the improved relative performance of nanofluids at elevated temperature must therefore reckon with the accelerated aggregation that the same elevation promotes, and the resolution of this tension is one of the practical judgements that distinguish a durable formulation from a merely demonstrable one [33].

The interplay of these factors means that the optimisation of a nanofluid for a given duty is a genuinely multivariable problem in which conductivity, viscosity, stability, cost and compatibility must be traded against one another, and in which the optimum for one application may be far from the optimum for another. This complexity, combined with the scatter in the experimental database and the absence of a first-principles model that captures all the relevant mechanisms, is precisely the setting in which data-driven and machine-learning methods offer the greatest promise, because such methods can learn the joint influence of many variables directly from data without requiring a closed-form mechanistic theory [33]. At the same time, the physical understanding developed in this chapter remains indispensable, both for the selection of the input variables, or features, that a data-driven model should receive and for the critical evaluation of whether a model's predictions are physically reasonable. The chapters that follow build directly on this foundation, first by examining the convective heat-transfer behaviour that these properties produce and then by developing the computational and data-driven tools with which that behaviour can be predicted and optimised. A recurring theme, established here and reinforced throughout, is that the promise of nanofluids is real but conditional, realised only when property enhancement, colloidal stability and hydraulic penalty are managed together rather than pursued in isolation [34].
