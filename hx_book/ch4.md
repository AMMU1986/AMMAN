# Chapter 4. Conventional Design of Nanofluid Heat Exchangers

## Abstract

The design of a heat exchanger that uses a nanofluid as its working fluid rests on the same conservation principles and the same sizing frameworks as the design of a conventional exchanger, but it introduces additional considerations that arise from the concentration-dependent and often uncertain properties of the working fluid and from the hydraulic penalty that those properties entail. This chapter develops the conventional, that is to say the pre-computational and pre-data-driven, methodology for designing nanofluid heat exchangers, and it situates that methodology within a thermodynamic framework broad enough to expose the trade-offs that determine whether a nanofluid design is genuinely superior to its base-fluid counterpart. It begins with the established design methodologies, the logarithmic mean temperature difference and effectiveness formulations introduced in Chapter 1, and shows how they are adapted to accommodate property variation and the coupled thermal-hydraulic behaviour of nanofluids. It then develops the energy, exergy and entropy-based methods of performance assessment, which together provide a far more penetrating evaluation than a first-law energy balance alone, because they account for the quality as well as the quantity of energy and locate the irreversibilities that a design should seek to minimise. Finally, it examines the constraints, trade-offs and performance indicators that govern practical design, including the pumping-power penalty, the concentration optimum, and the several figures of merit by which competing designs are compared. The chapter thereby completes the conventional foundation on which the computational and machine-learning methods of the subsequent chapters build, and it makes explicit the multi-objective character of the design problem that those methods are ultimately intended to solve.

## 4.1 Heat Exchanger Design Methodologies

The design of a heat exchanger is conventionally divided into two related but distinct tasks, sizing and rating. In a sizing problem the required duty and the inlet and outlet conditions are specified, and the object is to determine the transfer area and hence the physical dimensions of an exchanger that will accomplish the duty. In a rating problem the exchanger is already specified, and the object is to determine the outlet conditions and the duty that it will achieve under given inlet conditions. The two frameworks introduced in Chapter 1, the logarithmic mean temperature difference method and the effectiveness–number-of-transfer-units method, are suited respectively to the sizing and rating tasks, and both must be adapted when the working fluid is a nanofluid, principally because the thermophysical properties that enter them are functions of the nanoparticle concentration and of temperature [47]. The logarithmic mean temperature difference method suits the sizing task because, when the terminal temperatures of both streams are known, it yields directly the mean driving temperature difference from which, given the duty and the overall coefficient, the required area follows; a correction factor accommodates the departure of real multi-pass and cross-flow arrangements from the ideal counterflow on which the logarithmic mean is based. The effectiveness–number-of-transfer-units method suits the rating task because it expresses the effectiveness, the ratio of the actual duty to the maximum thermodynamically possible, as a function of the number of transfer units and the ratio of the heat-capacity rates, functions that are tabulated for each flow arrangement and that permit the outlet conditions to be found without the iterative solution that the logarithmic mean method would require when the outlets are unknown. Both methods rest on the assumption that the properties are constant, evaluated at a representative mean condition, and it is this assumption that the concentration and temperature dependence of nanofluid properties strains, for the more strongly the properties vary the less faithfully a single mean value represents them across the exchanger [48]. Figure 4.1 sets out the iterative design workflow that underlies both tasks, in which an initial estimate of the overall coefficient leads to a first sizing, which is then checked against the pressure-drop constraint and revised until a self-consistent and constraint-satisfying design is obtained.

The iterative character of the workflow shown in Figure 4.1 is not an incidental inconvenience but an intrinsic feature of the design problem, and it arises because the quantities that must be known to begin the calculation are themselves outcomes of the calculation. The overall heat transfer coefficient depends on the film coefficients, which depend on the flow velocities, which depend on the flow areas, which are among the dimensions the design is meant to determine; the mean temperature at which properties are evaluated depends on the outlet temperatures, which depend on the duty achieved, which depends on the coefficient being sought. The designer breaks this circularity by assuming initial values, computing the consequences, and revising the assumptions in the light of the results, repeating until successive iterations agree to within an acceptable tolerance. For a conventional single-phase fluid with weakly varying properties the iteration converges rapidly and can be carried out by hand, but for a nanofluid, whose properties depend on both temperature and concentration and whose viscosity may vary strongly across the operating range, the iteration is more delicate and the property evaluations more numerous, so that the manual procedure becomes tedious and error-prone and the case for computational implementation correspondingly stronger [47]. The workflow thus foreshadows, within the conventional framework itself, the computational assistance that the later chapters develop more fully.

It is worth emphasising that the sizing and rating tasks, though computationally distinct, address the same physical device from complementary directions, and that a mature design practice moves fluently between them. A preliminary sizing establishes the approximate dimensions required for a duty; a rating of that preliminary design under off-design conditions reveals how it will behave across the range of loads it will actually encounter; and the insights of the rating feed back into a revised sizing that better accommodates the full operating envelope. For a nanofluid heat exchanger this iteration between sizing and rating acquires an additional dimension, because the concentration of the nanofluid is itself a design variable that may be chosen, so that the designer is free to vary not only the geometry but the working fluid in search of a satisfactory design. This enlargement of the design space is the source of both the opportunity and the difficulty that nanofluids present: the opportunity to tune the fluid to the duty, and the difficulty of searching a space enlarged by the concentration and the several particle characteristics that define the fluid [48].

The adaptation of the sizing method to nanofluids proceeds by inserting the nanofluid properties, evaluated at the appropriate mean temperature and concentration, into the correlations for the film coefficients and into the expression for the overall coefficient. Because the nanofluid conductivity is higher than that of the base fluid, the film coefficient on the nanofluid side is raised, and because the nanofluid viscosity is also higher, the Reynolds number at a given mass flow rate is reduced, so that the two effects partly oppose one another in their influence on the coefficient. The net change in the overall coefficient, and hence in the required area, depends on which film controls the total resistance and on the balance between the conductivity and viscosity changes, and it can only be determined by carrying the property changes through the full calculation rather than by reasoning about conductivity alone [48]. This is the design-level expression of the principle, emphasised in earlier chapters, that a nanofluid benefits an exchanger only to the extent that the film it flows against is a significant contributor to the total thermal resistance. The overall coefficient, being the reciprocal of the sum of the series resistances, cannot be raised beyond the limit set by the resistances that the nanofluid does not touch, and no enhancement of the nanofluid film can overcome a wall or an opposing film that dominates the sum; the arithmetic of series resistances thus imposes a ceiling on the benefit obtainable from any working-fluid improvement, and recognising where that ceiling lies is the first step in judging whether a nanofluid is worth considering at all for a given exchanger.

This principle, the controlling-resistance principle, is worth developing because it disciplines expectations and directs effort where it will be rewarded. The overall resistance to heat transfer in an exchanger is the sum of the resistances in series: the film resistance on the hot side, the resistance of the separating wall, any fouling resistances, and the film resistance on the cold side. Because these resistances add, the largest of them dominates the sum, and an improvement in a small resistance yields a correspondingly small improvement in the total, however large that improvement may be in proportional terms. If the nanofluid flows on the side whose film resistance is already small compared with the resistance on the other side or in the wall, then enhancing the nanofluid film, even substantially, moves the total resistance scarcely at all, and the effort and expense of the nanofluid are largely wasted. If, on the contrary, the nanofluid flows on the side that controls the total resistance, then its enhancement translates almost fully into an enhancement of the overall coefficient. The implication for design is direct: a nanofluid should be deployed on the controlling side, and the assessment of any nanofluid proposal must begin by identifying which resistance controls, for a nanofluid on the wrong side is a solution to the wrong problem [48]. This consideration also explains why nanofluids are most attractive in applications where a liquid faces a high-resistance situation, such as laminar flow in compact passages, and least attractive where the liquid film is already a minor resistance.

The controlling-resistance principle interacts with the opposing effects of conductivity and viscosity in a way that further constrains the benefit. Raising the nanofluid conductivity raises the film coefficient and so lowers the film resistance, but raising the viscosity lowers the Reynolds number at fixed mass flow, which also lowers the film coefficient and so raises the resistance, and the two effects partly cancel. Whether the net effect on the controlling film is favourable depends on the relative magnitudes of the conductivity and viscosity changes and on how strongly the film coefficient depends on the Reynolds number in the prevailing regime, a dependence that is stronger in turbulent flow than in laminar. The upshot is that even on the controlling side a nanofluid does not guarantee an improvement, and that the improvement, where it occurs, must be established by carrying both property changes through the coefficient correlation rather than assumed from the conductivity gain alone. This is the design-level counterpart of the thermal-hydraulic trade-off that pervaded the preceding chapter, and it shows that the trade-off cannot be evaded by moving from the fluid to the device: it reappears, in transformed guise, at every level of the analysis [49].

The property variation of a nanofluid along the length of an exchanger complicates the constant-property assumptions on which the classical correlations rest. As the nanofluid is heated or cooled, its viscosity and, to a lesser degree, its conductivity change, altering the local film coefficient and the local friction factor, so that a design based on properties evaluated at a single mean temperature incurs an error whose magnitude depends on the temperature range and the strength of the property variation. For fluids and duties in which this variation is significant, the exchanger is divided into segments, the properties and coefficients are evaluated locally in each segment, and the segmental results are combined, a procedure that is straightforward in principle but laborious by hand and that consequently benefits from computational implementation [49]. The need to evaluate concentration- and temperature-dependent properties repeatedly, and to do so accurately, is one of the points at which the property-prediction models of Chapter 8 connect directly to conventional design practice, because a design method is only as accurate as the property values it is fed.

The segmental treatment of property variation deserves fuller description because it is the conventional method's principal device for coping with the strong temperature dependence of nanofluid properties, and because it exposes clearly the computational burden that motivates the more efficient methods of later chapters. In the segmental approach the exchanger is divided along its length into a number of zones small enough that the properties may be regarded as constant within each, the local mean temperature and concentration are used to evaluate the properties and coefficients for that zone, the local duty and temperature change are computed, and the results are accumulated from zone to zone along the exchanger. The accuracy of the method improves as the zones are made smaller and more numerous, at the cost of proportionally more property evaluations and more arithmetic, so that a fine segmentation of a nanofluid exchanger, whose properties must be evaluated from concentration- and temperature-dependent models at every zone, entails a large number of property calculations. When those property calculations are themselves the output of an elaborate model, whether a mechanistic correlation or a data-driven predictor, the cost of the segmental design is dominated by the cost of the property evaluations, and the efficiency of the property model becomes a determinant of the practicality of the design procedure. This is a concrete instance of the general truth that the conventional design framework and the property-modelling methods of later chapters are not separate concerns but tightly interlocked, the one consuming what the other produces [49].

A further complication specific to nanofluids, and absent from conventional single-phase design, is the possibility that the concentration itself varies along the exchanger or over time, whether through the migration effects discussed in the previous chapter, through local aggregation and settling in low-velocity zones, or through the gradual degradation of the suspension over the service life. A design that assumes a uniform and constant concentration may therefore mispredict the performance of a real exchanger in which the concentration is neither uniform nor constant, and a conservative design practice makes allowance for the degradation of properties as the suspension ages, sizing the exchanger to meet its duty not only when the fluid is fresh but when it has aged to the least favourable state anticipated in service. This requirement, that the design be robust to the evolution of the working fluid, has no counterpart in conventional design and is one of the ways in which the uncertain and time-dependent nature of nanofluid properties complicates the conventional framework beyond the mere substitution of enhanced property values [50]. ## 4.2 Energy, Exergy, and Entropy-Based Performance Assessment

A design that satisfies the thermal duty and respects the pressure-drop constraint is not necessarily a good design, because the first-law energy balance on which conventional sizing rests accounts only for the quantity of energy transferred and is silent about its quality and about the irreversibilities incurred in transferring it. The second-law methods of exergy and entropy analysis remedy this deficiency and provide a far more discriminating basis for the assessment and optimisation of nanofluid heat exchangers, in which the pumping penalty and the finite-temperature-difference heat transfer are both sources of irreversibility that a first-law analysis cannot properly weigh [50]. Energy analysis begins with the first law and quantifies the energy effectiveness of the exchanger, the fraction of the available energy that is actually transferred, and it is the basis of the effectiveness measure already introduced, which expresses in a single dimensionless number how nearly the exchanger approaches the greatest duty that the second law would permit given the inlet conditions and the heat-capacity rates of the two streams. Energy analysis is indispensable but incomplete, because it treats a joule of high-temperature energy and a joule of low-temperature energy as equivalent, whereas their capacity to perform useful work differs. A joule of thermal energy at a temperature far above the surroundings can be made to perform a large amount of useful work, through a heat engine that rejects heat to the environment, whereas a joule at a temperature only slightly above the surroundings can perform almost none; the two joules are equal in quantity but unequal in quality, and any accounting that treats them as interchangeable, as the first law does, is blind to a distinction that is central to the rational use of energy. It is exactly this blindness that the exergy and entropy analyses remove, and their removal of it transforms the assessment of a nanofluid design from a question of how much heat is moved into the far more searching question of how well the quality of the energy is preserved and how little work is squandered in moving it.

The inadequacy of a purely first-law assessment for nanofluid design can be made vivid by considering what it fails to see. A first-law energy balance is satisfied whenever the heat given up by the hot stream equals the heat received by the cold stream, less any losses to the surroundings, and it is entirely indifferent to the temperature difference across which that heat is transferred and to the pumping work expended in moving the fluids. Two exchangers that transfer the same duty are, to the first law, equivalent, even if one accomplishes it across a small temperature difference with little pumping and the other across a large temperature difference with heavy pumping. Yet the two are far from equivalent in any economic or thermodynamic sense: the first preserves the quality of the energy and consumes little work, while the second degrades the energy and consumes much. The pumping penalty that dominates the nanofluid trade-off is precisely a consumption of high-quality mechanical work, and the finite-temperature-difference heat transfer that a nanofluid seeks to reduce is precisely a degradation of energy quality, so that the two central concerns of nanofluid design are the two irreversibilities that the first law cannot weigh and the second law is designed to expose. This is why the second-law methods are not a refinement to be applied after the first-law design is complete but a necessary component of any assessment that aspires to identify the genuinely best design rather than merely a feasible one [50].

The reference environment, or dead state, against which exergy is measured deserves a word, because its choice, though it does not affect the location of the optima, affects the numerical values of the exergy quantities and must be stated for the results to be interpretable. The dead state is the condition of temperature, pressure and composition to which a stream could be brought by reversible processes exchanging heat and work only with the surroundings, and it is conventionally taken as the ambient condition; exergy is the maximum work obtainable as a stream is brought reversibly to this state. Because the exergy of a heat interaction depends on its temperature relative to the dead state, through the Carnot factor, exergy analysis automatically weights high-temperature heat more heavily than low-temperature heat, capturing the quality distinction that the first law misses. For a heat exchanger operating near ambient temperature the exergetic value of the heat transferred is modest, and the frictional destruction of mechanical exergy looms correspondingly large in the balance, which is one reason that the pumping penalty weighs so heavily in near-ambient applications and that the exergy optimum in such applications falls at a low concentration [51].

Exergy analysis, founded on the second law, quantifies the maximum useful work obtainable from a stream relative to a reference environment and thereby measures the quality of energy as well as its quantity. Applied to a heat exchanger, exergy analysis identifies the exergy destroyed by the two principal irreversibilities of the device, the transfer of heat across a finite temperature difference, which is the price paid for driving the heat at a finite rate, and the frictional dissipation of mechanical energy in overcoming the pressure drop, which is the price paid for moving the fluid through the passages. The exergy efficiency, the ratio of the exergy recovered to the exergy supplied, provides a figure of merit that penalises both a large temperature difference, which wastes the quality of the energy, and a large pressure drop, which destroys mechanical exergy, and it therefore captures the central thermal-hydraulic trade-off of nanofluid design in a single number [51]. The power of the exergy efficiency as a single figure of merit lies precisely in this capacity to weigh two dissimilar irreversibilities, the thermal and the frictional, on a common scale, for the two are otherwise incommensurable: a temperature difference and a pressure drop cannot be compared directly, but the exergy each destroys can, because exergy is a single currency into which both are converted. By expressing the thermal loss and the frictional loss in the common currency of destroyed work-potential, the exergy analysis makes it possible to add them, to compare them, and to find the design that minimises their sum, which no analysis working in the separate and incommensurable units of temperature and pressure could do. This is the deep reason that the second-law analysis succeeds where the first law fails: not merely that it accounts for quality, but that it provides a common measure in which the competing losses can be balanced against one another and an optimum meaningfully defined [52]. Figure 4.2 contrasts the energy and exergy efficiencies of a representative nanofluid exchanger as functions of concentration, illustrating that the energy efficiency rises steadily with loading while the exergy efficiency passes through a maximum, because at higher loadings the growing pumping irreversibility begins to outweigh the improving heat-transfer irreversibility.

The contrast between the two curves in Figure 4.2 is among the most pedagogically valuable results in the whole treatment of nanofluid design, because it shows two legitimate figures of merit disagreeing, and it forces the recognition that the choice of figure of merit is itself a design decision with consequences. A designer guided solely by the energy efficiency, which rises monotonically with concentration, would be led toward ever higher loadings, concluding that more nanoparticles are always better; a designer guided by the exergy efficiency, which peaks and then declines, would be led to a finite optimum loading and would reject the high concentrations that the energy criterion favours. The two designers, looking at the same exchanger and the same data, would reach opposite conclusions about the direction of improvement, and the disagreement is resolved not by more accurate calculation but by recognising that the exergy efficiency is the sounder criterion, because it accounts for the pumping work that the energy efficiency ignores. The lesson generalises well beyond nanofluids: whenever a first-law criterion and a second-law criterion disagree about the direction of improvement, the second-law criterion is to be trusted, because it alone accounts for the quality of energy and the work consumed, and a design optimised on a first-law criterion alone may be systematically driven toward thermodynamically inferior configurations [52].

The monotonic rise of the energy efficiency itself merits explanation, lest it be thought paradoxical that a genuine figure of merit should mislead. The energy efficiency rises with concentration because the enhanced conductivity raises the heat transfer coefficient and hence the duty achievable for a given surface and temperature difference, and the first law records this straightforwardly as an improvement. What the first law does not record is that the same increase in concentration raises the viscosity and hence the pumping work, and that beyond a certain loading the additional pumping work exceeds, in exergetic terms, the value of the additional heat transferred. The energy efficiency is therefore not wrong within its own terms; it is incomplete, measuring a real benefit while omitting a real cost. The exergy efficiency completes the accounting by placing benefit and cost on the common footing of work-equivalent value, and its maximum marks the loading at which the marginal exergetic benefit of further enhancement equals its marginal exergetic cost. That this maximum is finite and generally modest is the thermodynamic vindication of the empirical observation, made repeatedly in the previous chapter, that the best concentration is a moderate one [52].

The existence of a maximum in the exergy efficiency, evident in Figure 4.2, is one of the most important results of second-law analysis for nanofluid design, because it establishes that the optimum concentration from a thermodynamic standpoint is finite and generally modest, in contrast to the first-law intuition that more conductivity is always better. The optimum arises from the competition between two opposing trends: as concentration increases, the heat-transfer irreversibility associated with the finite temperature difference falls, because the enhanced coefficient permits the same duty at a smaller temperature difference, but the frictional irreversibility associated with the pressure drop rises, because the higher viscosity and friction factor increase the pumping dissipation. The concentration at which the sum of these irreversibilities is minimised, or equivalently at which the exergy efficiency is maximised, is the thermodynamically optimal loading, and it provides a principled target for design that the coupled property and hydraulic models of later chapters can help to locate [52]. The existence and the modesty of this optimum together constitute perhaps the most important single message of second-law analysis for the practitioner, for they replace the open-ended and misleading injunction to maximise conductivity with the bounded and correct injunction to seek a particular, calculable, and generally low concentration. A great deal of the disappointment that has attended attempts to deploy nanofluids can be traced to designs that pursued the first-law intuition past the second-law optimum, loading the fluid so heavily in pursuit of conductivity that the pumping penalty destroyed the advantage, and the exergy analysis provides both the diagnosis of that error and the remedy for it.

Entropy-generation analysis provides an equivalent and often more convenient formulation of the same second-law reasoning, expressed in terms of the rate at which entropy is generated by the irreversibilities of the exchanger. The total entropy-generation rate is the sum of a thermal contribution, arising from heat transfer across the finite temperature difference, and a frictional contribution, arising from the pressure drop, and the two contributions depend oppositely on many design variables, so that their sum passes through a minimum. The method of entropy-generation minimisation, which seeks the design that minimises the total entropy-generation rate, thereby provides a systematic route to the thermodynamically optimal design and has been applied extensively to nanofluid exchangers [53].

The equivalence between the entropy-generation and exergy formulations, which the Gouy–Stodola relation makes precise by proportioning the destroyed exergy to the generated entropy through the dead-state temperature, means that the two analyses must agree on the location of the optimum, and that the choice between them is one of convenience rather than of substance. The entropy-generation formulation is often preferred for design because its two contributions, the thermal and the frictional, can be written directly in terms of the heat duty, the temperature difference, the mass flow and the pressure drop, quantities that emerge naturally from the sizing calculation, so that the entropy-generation rate can be evaluated as a by-product of the thermal-hydraulic design with little additional labour. The exergy formulation is often preferred for communication, because an efficiency between zero and unity is more readily grasped than a rate of entropy generation, and because it connects the analysis to the broader language of thermodynamic performance. That the two agree is not a coincidence but a consequence of their common foundation in the second law, and a designer may move between them according to the demands of the moment, secure in the knowledge that they point to the same optimal design [53].

The opposing dependence of the two entropy contributions on the design variables is the mathematical engine that produces the optimum, and understanding it guides the direction of design changes. The thermal contribution, arising from heat transfer across a finite temperature difference, is reduced by anything that lowers that temperature difference, which for a fixed duty means anything that raises the heat transfer coefficient or the surface area; increasing the flow rate or the nanoparticle loading, by raising the coefficient, therefore reduces the thermal entropy generation. The frictional contribution, arising from the pressure drop, is increased by anything that raises the pressure drop, which increasing the flow rate or the loading also does. The two contributions thus respond oppositely to the flow rate and to the loading, the one falling and the other rising, and their sum, falling at first as the rapidly decreasing thermal contribution dominates and rising later as the accelerating frictional contribution takes over, passes through a minimum at the operating point where their marginal changes balance. That this minimum exists and is unique under ordinary conditions is what makes entropy-generation minimisation a well-posed design method, and its location is the operating point a rational design should seek [54]. Figure 4.3 shows the thermal, frictional and total entropy-generation rates as functions of the Reynolds number, exhibiting the characteristic minimum in the total that defines the optimal operating point and that shifts as the fluid and geometry are changed. The shifting of the minimum with the fluid and the geometry is itself informative, for it shows that the optimal operating point is not a universal constant but a property of the particular combination of fluid and device, to be located afresh for each case; introducing a nanofluid, by altering the conductivity and the viscosity, moves both the thermal and the frictional curves and hence relocates their summed minimum, generally toward a lower Reynolds number than the base fluid would favour, because the enhanced conductivity reduces the flow rate needed to suppress the thermal irreversibility while the elevated viscosity aggravates the frictional penalty at any given flow rate. The direction and magnitude of the shift depend on the balance between the conductivity gain and the viscosity penalty of the particular nanofluid, and reading it from the entropy-generation curves is among the most direct ways of judging whether a given nanofluid improves upon its base fluid at the optimal operating point rather than merely at some arbitrarily chosen one [54].

The entropy-generation minimisation framework brings a valuable discipline to nanofluid design because it forces the pumping penalty and the heat-transfer benefit onto a common thermodynamic footing and identifies the operating point at which their combined irreversibility is least. The Bejan number, defined as the ratio of the thermal entropy generation to the total, expresses which of the two irreversibilities dominates at a given operating point, and it provides a diagnostic that guides the direction of design changes: when the Bejan number is near unity the thermal irreversibility dominates and enhancement is worthwhile, whereas when it is small the frictional irreversibility dominates and further enhancement is counterproductive [54]. These second-law tools, taken together, transform the assessment of a nanofluid from a one-dimensional question about conductivity into a multidimensional optimisation that weighs quality, quantity and irreversibility, and they define objective functions that the machine-learning-assisted optimisation of later chapters can pursue.

The Bejan number deserves a closer look as a design diagnostic, because it converts the abstract balance of irreversibilities into a concrete indication of what to do next. When the Bejan number is close to unity, the thermal irreversibility dominates the total, which means that the exchanger is losing most of its available work to heat transfer across too large a temperature difference; the appropriate response is to enhance the heat transfer, whether by raising the flow rate, increasing the surface, or introducing or increasing the nanoparticle loading, because such enhancement attacks the dominant loss. When the Bejan number is small, the frictional irreversibility dominates, which means the exchanger is losing most of its available work to pumping; the appropriate response is the opposite, to reduce the flow rate or the loading, because further enhancement would only aggravate the dominant loss. The optimum lies where the two losses are comparable and the Bejan number takes an intermediate value, and the direction in which a design should move to approach the optimum is read off from whether its Bejan number is too large or too small. This diagnostic use of the Bejan number gives the designer not merely a figure of merit to be maximised or minimised but a signpost indicating the direction of improvement, and it is especially valuable in the early stages of design when the location of the optimum is not yet known [54].

A caution must accompany the enthusiasm for second-law methods, however, lest they be applied mechanically to the exclusion of judgement. The entropy-generation and exergy analyses locate a thermodynamic optimum, but the thermodynamic optimum is not necessarily the design that should be built, because it takes no account of capital cost, of the practical constraints on stability and erosion discussed in the next section, or of the reliability and maintainability of the equipment. A design at the thermodynamic optimum might require a surface area so large, or a nanofluid so concentrated, that its capital cost or its instability rendered it uneconomic or impractical, and the true design optimum, weighing thermodynamic performance against cost and constraint, generally differs from the thermodynamic optimum considered alone. The second-law analysis is therefore best understood as furnishing one of several objectives in a multi-objective problem, an objective that captures the thermodynamic quality of the design with a rigour that the first law cannot match, but that must be balanced against the economic and practical objectives that the following section brings into view [55].

## 4.3 Design Constraints, Trade-Offs, and Performance Indicators

The energy, exergy and entropy analyses of the preceding section identify the thermodynamically optimal design, but thermodynamics is not the whole of engineering, and a design must satisfy a host of practical requirements that lie outside the thermodynamic account altogether. The present section turns from the thermodynamic optimum to the practical constraints that bound the space within which any optimum, thermodynamic or economic, must be sought, and to the indicators by which candidate designs are compared once the constraints are respected. It is in the interplay of these constraints, indicators and objectives that the design problem attains its full complexity, and that the case for the systematic computational methods of the later chapters becomes conclusive.

Every practical design is bounded by constraints, and the constraints that bound a nanofluid heat exchanger are more numerous and more tightly coupled than those of a conventional exchanger. The pressure-drop constraint, which limits the pumping power that may be expended, is the most immediate, because the higher viscosity of a nanofluid raises the pressure drop at a given flow rate and can cause a design that meets the thermal duty to violate the hydraulic limit. The stability constraint, discussed in Chapter 2, limits the concentration and the operating temperature to values at which the suspension remains dispersed over the service life, and it may be more restrictive than the thermodynamic optimum. The erosion constraint, in its turn, limits the velocity to those values at which the particles do not unacceptably abrade the wetted surfaces, and the cost constraint limits the concentration and the choice of particle material to values that the application can economically bear [55]. These constraints interact, so that the feasible design space is a bounded region within the multidimensional space of concentration, flow rate, geometry and material, and the optimal design lies within or on the boundary of that region.

The interaction of the constraints deserves emphasis because it is the feature that most distinguishes nanofluid design from conventional design and that most complicates the search for an optimum. In a conventional exchanger the constraints are relatively few and largely independent: a pressure-drop limit bounds the flow rate, a velocity limit bounds erosion, and the two can be treated more or less separately. In a nanofluid exchanger the constraints are coupled through the shared dependence on concentration, so that tightening one may relax or tighten another in ways that are not obvious. Raising the concentration to improve heat transfer raises the viscosity and hence tightens the pressure-drop constraint; it also raises the propensity to aggregate and hence tightens the stability constraint; and it raises the cost of the fluid and hence tightens the economic constraint. Lowering the velocity to respect the erosion constraint lowers the heat transfer and may push the design toward higher concentration to compensate, re-tightening the stability and cost constraints. The constraints thus form a coupled system, and a change made to satisfy one may violate another, so that the feasible region is not a simple box but an irregular volume whose boundaries bend and interact, and finding the true optimum within it is a genuine constrained optimisation rather than a sequence of independent adjustments [55]. This coupling is one of the clearest respects in which nanofluid design exceeds the reach of hand calculation and rewards the systematic optimisation that computational methods afford.

The stability constraint merits particular attention among the constraints because it is the one most often neglected in academic studies and most often decisive in practice. A design that is optimal by every thermal, hydraulic and thermodynamic criterion is worthless if the working fluid aggregates and settles within weeks of commissioning, and the concentration and temperature at which a nanofluid remains stable over its service life may be well below the values that the thermodynamic optimisation would select. The stability constraint therefore frequently binds before the thermodynamic optimum is reached, so that the best achievable design is not the thermodynamic optimum but the most thermodynamically favourable design that remains stable, a design on the boundary of the stability constraint rather than at the interior thermodynamic optimum. This is a sobering conclusion, because it means that the elaborate thermodynamic optimisation may in the end be overridden by a colloidal-chemical limit, and it underscores the theme, developed throughout the book, that the stability of the suspension is not a secondary concern but a primary determinant of what designs are achievable. The proper treatment of the stability constraint requires the long-term stability data that, as noted in Chapter 2, are among the least frequently reported quantities in the literature, and the scarcity of such data is a real impediment to sound nanofluid design [56].

The erosion constraint, though less often binding than the stability constraint, introduces a consideration absent from single-phase design and deserves mention. The solid particles in a nanofluid, however small, carry momentum, and where the flow impinges on a surface, at bends, at tube inlets and around obstructions, the particles can abrade the surface over time, thinning walls and eventually causing failure. The rate of erosion increases steeply with velocity, so that the erosion constraint takes the form of an upper limit on the flow velocity, and this limit interacts with the heat-transfer and pressure-drop considerations that also depend on velocity. The very hardness that makes some particle materials attractive for their conductivity, such as the oxides and carbides, may make them more erosive, adding yet another dimension to the multi-attribute material selection discussed in earlier chapters. Although the extremely small size of nanoparticles renders their individual erosive effect far smaller than that of the coarse particles that plagued early slurry systems, the effect is not zero, especially at the aggregated clusters that form in an ageing suspension, and a conservative design respects a velocity limit that keeps erosion within acceptable bounds over the service life [57].

The pumping-power penalty deserves particular emphasis because it is the single consideration most likely to negate the apparent benefit of a nanofluid, and because it is frequently understated in studies that compare fluids at equal flow rate or equal Reynolds number rather than at equal pumping power. As established in Chapter 3, a fair comparison holds the pumping power constant and asks whether the nanofluid transfers more heat under that constraint, and the thermal-hydraulic performance factor, exceeding unity for a net benefit, encapsulates the answer. The performance factor depends on the concentration, the flow regime and the geometry in a coupled manner, and it typically exceeds unity at low to moderate concentrations in appropriate regimes and falls below unity at high concentrations, so that its maximisation defines an optimum that coincides broadly with the thermodynamic optimum identified by the exergy and entropy analyses [56]. The frequent understatement of the pumping penalty in the literature is worth dwelling on because it has distorted the perceived value of nanofluids and because avoiding it is within the designer's control. A study that compares a nanofluid with its base fluid at equal Reynolds number, or at equal flow velocity, credits the nanofluid with a heat-transfer enhancement while charging it nothing for the extra pumping work that its higher viscosity demands to reach that Reynolds number or velocity, and the enhancement so reported is therefore illusory in part, an artefact of an unfair comparison rather than a genuine benefit available to an operator. The same fluid compared honestly at equal pumping power, the resource actually constrained in service, frequently shows a far smaller enhancement, and sometimes a penalty. Because the equal-Reynolds-number comparison is easier to arrange experimentally and yields more flattering numbers, it has been widely used, and a large part of the optimistic literature on nanofluids rests upon it; the more demanding equal-pumping-power comparison, though harder and less flattering, is the only one that reflects the operating economics, and a designer who adopts it as the standard will avoid the disappointment that has attended designs based on the easier comparison. The discipline of always comparing at equal pumping power is thus among the most important habits a nanofluid designer can cultivate, and it costs nothing but the willingness to report honest rather than flattering numbers [56].

The consistency of these several optima, arrived at from the first-law performance factor, the exergy efficiency and the entropy-generation minimisation, provides reassurance that a modest, well-chosen concentration is the robustly correct design target across a range of criteria.

The convergence of the several optima, arrived at independently from the first-law performance factor, the exergy efficiency and the entropy-generation minimisation, is a result of some depth that repays reflection, because it is not logically guaranteed and its occurrence tells us something about the structure of the problem. The criteria measure different things: the performance factor measures heat transferred per unit pumping power, the exergy efficiency measures the fraction of supplied work-equivalent energy that is usefully recovered, and the entropy-generation rate measures total irreversibility. That their optima coincide, at least approximately, reflects the fact that all three are governed by the same underlying competition between the heat-transfer benefit and the pumping penalty, and that this single competition dominates the behaviour of all three criteria over the range of practical interest. Where the competition is more even, or where secondary effects intrude, the optima may separate somewhat, and the designer must then decide which criterion best represents the objectives of the particular application. But the broad convergence provides a valuable robustness: a design placed near the common optimum is nearly optimal by several independent measures, and is therefore unlikely to be badly wrong under any reasonable weighting of the objectives, a reassurance that is worth a great deal in the face of the uncertainty that attends nanofluid properties and performance [56].

It should not be inferred from this convergence, however, that the design problem reduces to the selection of a single optimal concentration, for the concentration is only one of the variables the designer controls, and the others, the geometry, the flow rate, the particle material, size and shape, and the base fluid, remain to be chosen, and their optima do not in general coincide so obligingly. The convergence of the concentration optima simplifies one dimension of the problem but leaves the others, and the full design problem remains a genuine multivariable, multi-objective optimisation over a constrained space. The value of the second-law analysis and the performance factor is that they collapse the thermal-hydraulic trade-off, the most important single trade-off, into criteria whose optima largely agree, thereby reducing the effective dimensionality of the problem and providing a firm anchor from which the remaining variables can be explored. This partial simplification is welcome, but it does not eliminate the need for the systematic search over the remaining variables that the computational and data-driven methods of later chapters are designed to perform [57].

The performance indicators by which competing nanofluid designs are compared may be organised into first-law, second-law and economic categories, and a comprehensive assessment employs indicators from all three. The first-law indicators include the heat transfer coefficient, the Nusselt number, the effectiveness and the thermal-hydraulic performance factor, which measure the heat-transfer benefit and, in the case of the performance factor, weigh it against the hydraulic cost. The second-law indicators include the exergy efficiency, the entropy-generation rate and the Bejan number, which measure the thermodynamic quality of the design and locate its irreversibilities. The economic indicators include the capital cost, which scales with the transfer area, the operating cost, which is dominated by the pumping power and hence by the hydraulic penalty, and the cost of the nanofluid itself, which scales with the concentration and the particle material [57]. Table 4.2 organises these indicators and identifies the aspect of performance that each captures, making explicit the multi-objective character of the design problem.

The organisation of the indicators into first-law, second-law and economic categories is more than a taxonomic convenience, for it reflects a hierarchy of concerns that a mature design process addresses in sequence. The first-law indicators answer the primary question of whether the design accomplishes its duty and how much heat it transfers for a given hydraulic cost; they are the indicators of feasibility and of gross performance, and a design that fails them is rejected outright. The second-law indicators answer the deeper question of how well the design uses the quality of the energy it handles and where its irreversibilities lie; they discriminate among designs that all satisfy the first-law requirements, identifying the thermodynamically superior among them and guiding their improvement. The economic indicators answer the ultimate question of whether the design is worth building and operating, weighing the capital cost of the surface against the operating cost of the pumping and the recurring cost of the nanofluid itself; they are the indicators by which the thermodynamically attractive design is finally judged fit or unfit for the application. A comprehensive assessment moves through these categories in order, using the first law to establish feasibility, the second law to optimise thermodynamic quality, and economics to confirm viability, and a design that excels in one category but fails in another is not a good design however impressive its selective merits [57].

The economic indicators deserve particular emphasis in the nanofluid context because they capture a cost that conventional design does not face, namely the cost of the working fluid itself. In a conventional water or glycol system the working fluid is cheap and its cost negligible against the capital and pumping costs, but a nanofluid, especially one containing precious-metal or high-quality carbon particles, may be expensive, and its cost scales with the concentration and with the volume of the system. This fluid cost introduces an economic pressure toward lower concentrations that reinforces the thermodynamic pressure already identified, and it can shift the economic optimum below even the thermodynamic optimum, particularly for expensive particle materials in large systems. The economic assessment must also reckon with the cost of maintaining stability, the cost of replacing degraded fluid over the service life, and the potential cost of the more elaborate equipment that an erosive or unstable fluid may demand, so that the true lifetime economics of a nanofluid system can be considerably less favourable than a comparison of thermal performance alone would suggest. It is a recurring finding of careful techno-economic studies that the thermal advantages of nanofluids, real though they are, translate into economic advantages only under particular and often demanding conditions, and that the economic case for a nanofluid is frequently more marginal than its thermal case, a sobering conclusion that any honest assessment must confront [58].

The multi-objective character of nanofluid heat-exchanger design, evident from the diversity of constraints and indicators, means that there is in general no single design that is best by every measure, but rather a set of designs, each of which is optimal in the sense that no other design improves one objective without worsening another. This set, known as the Pareto front, is the proper object of a rigorous design study, and its determination requires the repeated evaluation of many candidate designs across the feasible space, an undertaking that is expensive when each evaluation involves a full thermal-hydraulic calculation with accurate property models. It is precisely here that the computational and data-driven methods of the following chapters demonstrate their value: computational fluid dynamics can evaluate candidate designs with a fidelity that correlations cannot match, while machine-learning surrogates can approximate the expensive evaluations cheaply enough to permit the many-query optimisation that Pareto-front determination demands [58]. The conventional design framework developed in this chapter thus defines the objectives, the constraints and the indicators of the problem, while the methods of the subsequent chapters provide the computational means to solve it efficiently, and the two are complementary rather than competing.

The notion of the Pareto front deserves elaboration because it reframes the design problem in a way that is both more honest and more useful than the search for a single best design. When several objectives conflict, as the thermal, hydraulic, thermodynamic and economic objectives of a nanofluid exchanger do, there is in general no design that is best on all of them at once, and the demand for such a design is misconceived. What exists instead is a set of designs each of which is efficient, in the sense that any change that improves one objective necessarily worsens another; these are the non-dominated designs, and they form the Pareto front. A design not on the front is dominated, meaning some other design is at least as good on every objective and strictly better on one, and such a design can be improved unambiguously and should be rejected. A design on the front cannot be improved on one objective without sacrifice on another, and the choice among the designs on the front is not a matter of optimisation but of preference, of how the decision-maker weighs the competing objectives against one another. The proper output of a rigorous design study is therefore not a single design but the Pareto front, presented to the decision-maker so that the trade-offs are explicit and the final choice is made with full knowledge of what each objective costs in terms of the others [58].

The determination of the Pareto front is computationally demanding, for it requires the evaluation of many candidate designs distributed across the feasible space, and the retention of the non-dominated ones, and when each evaluation entails a full thermal-hydraulic calculation with accurate concentration- and temperature-dependent property models, the total cost of mapping the front can be prohibitive by conventional means. This is the precise point at which the machine-learning surrogates of the later chapters earn their place, for a surrogate trained on a limited set of expensive evaluations can approximate the objectives across the whole space at negligible cost per evaluation, permitting the many thousands of evaluations that a thorough exploration of the Pareto front requires. The surrogate does not replace the accurate but expensive model; it interpolates it, standing in for it during the many-query optimisation and being validated against it at the designs of interest. The conventional design framework of this chapter defines the objectives and constraints, the accurate models of the intervening chapters supply the expensive evaluations, and the machine-learning surrogates make the many-query optimisation tractable, the three combining into a design methodology far more powerful than any one of them alone [58]. The engineer who understands the thermodynamic structure of the design problem is best placed to formulate the machine-learning problem that will accelerate its solution, and to judge whether the solution that emerges is physically and economically sound.
