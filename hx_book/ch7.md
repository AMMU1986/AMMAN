# Chapter 7. Machine Learning Fundamentals for Heat Exchanger Design

## Abstract

The sound application of machine learning to heat-exchanger design requires more than familiarity with a software library; it requires an understanding of the principles that govern how models learn from data, how their performance is measured, and how the many decisions taken in the course of building a model affect the reliability of its predictions. This chapter develops those fundamentals at the level of detail a thermal engineer needs to build, evaluate and critically assess data-driven models of nanofluid properties and heat-exchanger performance. It begins with the three principal paradigms of machine learning, supervised, unsupervised and reinforcement learning, characterising the kind of problem each addresses and the role each plays in thermal-engineering applications. It then treats the practical craft of building a model, encompassing the preparation and cleaning of data, the engineering and selection of features, the splitting of data for training and testing, and the training and tuning of the model itself, emphasising the decisions that most strongly influence the outcome. Finally, it addresses the evaluation of models, the cross-validation procedures that guard against over-optimistic assessment, the performance metrics that quantify predictive accuracy for the regression problems typical of thermal engineering, and the diagnosis of the underfitting and overfitting that lie at the heart of the modelling craft. Throughout, the treatment is oriented toward the particular circumstances of nanofluid and heat-exchanger data, which are typically tabular, modest in quantity, and subject to the scatter and measurement uncertainty documented in the earlier chapters, and it prepares the reader for the specific applications developed in Chapters 8 and 9.

## 7.1 Supervised, Unsupervised, and Reinforcement Learning

Machine learning is conventionally divided into three paradigms according to the nature of the data available and the kind of feedback the learning process receives, and the great majority of thermal-engineering applications fall within the first of them, supervised learning. In supervised learning the training data consist of examples in which both the input features and the correct output target are known, and the object is to learn a mapping from features to targets that generalises to new, unseen inputs. When the target is a continuous quantity, such as a thermal conductivity, a Nusselt number or a pressure drop, the problem is one of regression, and regression is the dominant task in nanofluid and heat-exchanger modelling because the quantities of engineering interest are almost all continuous. When the target is a discrete category, such as a flow regime or a stability class, the problem is one of classification [87].

The predominance of regression over classification in thermal-engineering applications is a consequence of the nature of the questions the engineer asks, which are overwhelmingly quantitative, and it shapes the whole apparatus of methods and metrics that this chapter develops. The engineer wishes to know not merely whether a nanofluid will conduct heat well but how well, expressed as a numerical conductivity; not merely whether a heat exchanger will perform adequately but what its effectiveness and pressure drop will be, expressed as numbers that feed directly into design calculations. Classification does arise, as when a suspension must be judged stable or unstable, or a flow laminar, transitional or turbulent, but these categorical questions are the exception, and even they are frequently better addressed by predicting an underlying continuous quantity and comparing it against a threshold than by training a classifier directly [87]. The reader should therefore understand the emphasis on regression throughout this chapter not as an arbitrary choice but as a faithful reflection of the quantitative character of thermal-engineering practice, in which the objects of prediction are almost always numbers rather than labels.

A conceptual point that underlies the whole of supervised learning, and that the thermal engineer accustomed to deterministic physical laws must assimilate, is that the relationship the model learns is statistical rather than exact. The measured targets in a training set are corrupted by experimental error and by the influence of variables not among the features, so that even the true underlying relationship would not pass exactly through the measured points, and a model that tried to do so would be fitting the noise. Supervised learning therefore aims not to reproduce the training targets exactly but to recover the systematic relationship that underlies them, treating the scatter about that relationship as noise to be averaged out rather than signal to be captured [88]. This statistical conception of the learning task, in which the goal is the expected value of the target given the features rather than any individual measured value, is the conceptual root of the distinction between fitting and generalising that governs the entire discipline, and its acceptance marks the transition from a deterministic to a statistical habit of mind that the sound practice of machine learning requires. The supervised-learning workflow, which structures nearly every predictive application in this book, proceeds from a dataset of labelled examples through the training of a model to the prediction of targets for new inputs. The model is a parameterised function, and training adjusts its parameters to minimise a loss function that measures the discrepancy between the model's predictions and the known targets on the training data. The choice of model determines the form of the function and hence the kinds of relationship it can represent, while the choice of loss function determines what constitutes a good fit, and both choices influence the outcome. The essential challenge of supervised learning, to which much of the chapter is devoted, is that minimising the loss on the training data is not the true objective; the true objective is to minimise the loss on unseen data, and a model that fits the training data too closely may generalise poorly, a failure mode that the evaluation procedures of Section 7.3 are designed to detect and prevent [88].

The choice of loss function, which specifies precisely what the training seeks to minimise, is a modelling decision of some consequence that is easily overlooked because a default is so often accepted without reflection. The most common loss for regression penalises the square of the prediction error, which has the convenient mathematical property of a smooth gradient and the statistical interpretation of seeking the mean of the target, but which weights large errors very heavily and is therefore sensitive to the outliers that a scattered experimental dataset contains, so that a few erroneous measurements can distort the fitted model appreciably. An alternative loss that penalises the absolute error weights all errors in proportion to their magnitude and is correspondingly more robust to outliers, seeking the median rather than the mean of the target, and a compromise loss that behaves quadratically for small errors and linearly for large ones combines robustness with a smooth gradient [88]. The alignment of the loss function with the character of the data and the priorities of the application, rather than the unreflective acceptance of the squared-error default, is a mark of considered practice, and it is especially pertinent in the nanofluid context where outlying measurements are common and their undue influence is a genuine hazard.

The relationship between model capacity and the twin risks of underfitting and overfitting, which Section 7.3 treats in detail, can usefully be previewed here in its conceptual essence, because it motivates much of what follows. Every model possesses a capacity, a richness of the relationships it can represent, and the total error of its predictions on unseen data can be understood as arising from two opposing sources: a bias that reflects the model's inability to represent the true relationship when its capacity is too low, and a variance that reflects the model's sensitivity to the particular training sample when its capacity is too high. Increasing capacity reduces bias but increases variance, and the art of modelling lies in finding the capacity that minimises their sum, a balance that depends on the amount of data available, for more data permit higher capacity to be exploited without incurring excessive variance [88]. This decomposition of error into bias and variance is among the most illuminating ideas in the discipline, and it explains why the modest datasets of nanofluid research favour models of restrained capacity and why the acquisition of more data, when feasible, is so often the most effective route to improved prediction.

Unsupervised learning addresses data in which no target is provided, and its object is to discover structure within the input data themselves rather than to predict a known output. Clustering, the most common unsupervised task, partitions the data into groups of similar examples, and in thermal engineering it is used to identify regimes, to organise heterogeneous datasets and to detect outliers that may indicate erroneous measurements. Dimensionality reduction, a second unsupervised task, seeks a lower-dimensional representation of the data that preserves its essential structure, and it is used to visualise high-dimensional datasets, to remove redundancy among correlated features and to compress the input before predictive modelling; the detection of anomalies, a third unsupervised task closely allied to clustering, identifies the examples that conform poorly to the structure the bulk of the data exhibit, and in the preparation of a nanofluid dataset it provides an objective complement to the physically informed judgement by which suspect measurements are flagged for scrutiny [89]. Although unsupervised methods are less prominent than supervised ones in the nanofluid literature, they play a valuable supporting role in the exploration and preparation of data, and they can reveal structure, such as the clustering of measurements by preparation method or by laboratory, that alerts the analyst to biases in the dataset that would otherwise distort a supervised model.

The mechanics of clustering warrant a brief verbal account, because the intuition it provides carries over to the diagnostic use that thermal engineers make of it. A clustering algorithm groups examples by their similarity, gathering into a common cluster those examples that lie close together in the feature space and separating those that lie far apart, and the most familiar such algorithm proceeds by positing a number of cluster centres, assigning each example to its nearest centre, relocating each centre to the mean of the examples assigned to it, and repeating until the assignment stabilises. The result is a partition of the data into groups whose members resemble one another, and in the exploratory analysis of a nanofluid dataset such a partition may correspond to physically or procedurally meaningful categories, grouping together, for instance, the measurements made on a particular class of particle or by a particular experimental technique [89]. The value of this to the supervised modeller is diagnostic: if the clusters align with the source of the data rather than with its physics, the alignment betrays a systematic difference between sources that, unless accounted for, will confound a supervised model, and the clustering thus surfaces a hidden structure that the raw inspection of the data would not reveal.

Dimensionality reduction serves a complementary exploratory purpose, and its logic is that of finding a compact representation that preserves the essential variation in the data while discarding redundancy. The most established such method identifies the directions in the feature space along which the data vary most, and re-expresses each example in terms of its coordinates along a few of these directions, so that a dataset described originally by many correlated features may be summarised with little loss by a handful of derived quantities [89]. For the thermal engineer this offers both a means of visualising a high-dimensional dataset by projecting it onto two or three derived dimensions that can be plotted, revealing groupings and outliers that no single pairwise plot would show, and a means of reducing the dimensionality of the input before supervised modelling, mitigating the curse of dimensionality that afflicts learning from limited data. The interpretation of the derived dimensions in physical terms is not always straightforward, but where the original features are physically meaningful the directions of greatest variation often admit a physical reading that itself yields insight into which combinations of variables carry the information in the dataset.

Reinforcement learning, the third paradigm, addresses problems in which an agent learns to take actions in an environment so as to maximise a cumulative reward, learning from the consequences of its actions rather than from labelled examples. It differs fundamentally from the other two paradigms in that the data are generated by the agent's own actions and the feedback is a scalar reward rather than a correct answer, and it is suited to sequential decision and control problems. In thermal engineering its applications are at present less mature than those of supervised learning, but they include the real-time control of thermal systems, the adaptive operation of heat exchangers under varying conditions, and the sequential design of experiments or simulations to acquire the most informative data, and interest in these applications is growing as the methods mature [90]. The distinctive learning mechanism of reinforcement learning repays a fuller description, because it differs so markedly from the other paradigms that its applicability is easily misjudged. The reinforcement-learning agent is not shown correct answers but is instead placed in an environment in which it takes actions, observes the resulting change of state, and receives a reward that signals the desirability of the outcome, and over many such interactions it learns a policy, a mapping from states to actions, that maximises the cumulative reward it can expect to accumulate over time. The central difficulty, which distinguishes the paradigm from mere trial and error, is that the reward may be delayed, so that an action beneficial in the long run may appear costly in the short run, and the agent must learn to credit early actions for their eventual consequences, balancing the exploitation of actions known to yield reward against the exploration of untried actions that might yield more [90]. This framework is powerful for genuine sequential-control problems, but its data requirements and its need for a safe environment in which to experiment, whether real or simulated, are demanding, and the thermal engineer should recognise that many problems superficially resembling control, in which a single decision is made from fixed inputs, are in truth supervised-learning problems better addressed by the simpler paradigm.

The three paradigms are complementary rather than competing, and a sophisticated application may employ all three: unsupervised methods to explore and prepare the data, supervised methods to build predictive surrogates, and reinforcement learning to control or to optimise, and the thermal engineer benefits from a working familiarity with all of them even though supervised regression will dominate the applications of the following chapters. The judgement of which paradigm a given problem calls for is itself a foundational skill, and it turns on two questions that the analyst should pose at the outset: whether correct outputs are available for the training examples, which distinguishes supervised from unsupervised learning, and whether the problem involves a sequence of decisions with delayed consequences, which distinguishes reinforcement learning from both. A clear answer to these questions at the outset averts the not uncommon error of forcing a problem into an ill-suited paradigm, such as treating a straightforward prediction as a control problem or attempting to cluster data whose natural task is prediction, and it directs the subsequent effort of data preparation and model development along productive lines [90].

## 7.2 Data Preparation, Feature Engineering, and Model Development

The adage that a machine-learning model is only as good as the data from which it learns is nowhere more pertinent than in nanofluid and heat-exchanger modelling, where the data are drawn from a scattered and heterogeneous literature, and the preparation of the data is consequently the phase of model development that most repays careful attention while being the phase most often neglected. Data preparation begins with the assembly of a dataset from its sources, whether experimental measurements, computational results or values extracted from published tables and figures, and with the reconciliation of the units, conventions and definitions used by different sources, a step that is laborious but essential because inconsistencies in, for example, the definition of concentration or the reference temperature for a property can introduce systematic errors that no subsequent modelling can remove [91].

The reconciliation of conventions is more treacherous than it may first appear, because the discrepancies are often silent, buried in definitions that each source regards as too obvious to state. The concentration of a suspension may be reported as a volume fraction or a mass fraction, quantities that differ substantially for the dense particles typical of nanofluids, and a dataset that pools the two without conversion introduces a systematic distortion that no amount of subsequent modelling can detect or repair, since the corrupted feature carries no mark of its corruption. Temperatures may be referenced differently, properties may be measured by methods that yield subtly different values, and geometric quantities may be defined with respect to different characteristic dimensions, each a potential source of silent inconsistency [91]. The defence against these hazards is a meticulous and physically informed audit of every source, tracing each reported quantity back to its definition and converting all to a common convention before any modelling begins, a discipline that is tedious and unglamorous but that determines the integrity of everything built upon the data, and that the physically knowledgeable analyst is uniquely equipped to perform.

The extraction of data from published figures, a necessity when tabulated values are unavailable, introduces its own category of error that the analyst must weigh. Values read from a plotted curve or scatter of points, whether by hand or by digitisation software, carry a reading uncertainty that adds to the original measurement uncertainty, and this compounded uncertainty must be borne in mind when such extracted data are pooled with directly tabulated values of higher precision [91]. Where the provenance and reliability of the sources differ markedly, the analyst may reasonably weight them differently or record their provenance as metadata that a model can use to account for the varying quality, and in extreme cases may exclude sources whose reliability cannot be established, accepting a smaller but cleaner dataset in preference to a larger but corrupted one. These judgements about the admission and weighting of sources are among the most consequential in the whole modelling enterprise, and they rest on the physical and experimental understanding that the earlier chapters cultivate rather than on any purely statistical criterion. The cleaning of the assembled data addresses the errors, omissions and anomalies that any real dataset contains. Missing values, which arise when a source fails to report a variable that the model requires, must be handled either by discarding the incomplete examples, which wastes data, or by imputing the missing values from the others, which introduces assumptions that must be examined. Outliers, examples whose values depart markedly from the rest, may represent genuine but rare behaviour, in which case they carry valuable information, or erroneous measurements, in which case they distort the model, and distinguishing between the two requires physical judgement of the kind that the earlier chapters cultivate. Duplicate and near-duplicate examples, which arise when the same measurement is reported by multiple sources, must be identified and consolidated to prevent them from biasing the model toward the over-represented conditions, a hazard that is acute in a field whose most-studied nanofluids appear repeatedly across the literature while less fashionable systems are represented by a handful of points, so that an uncorrected dataset teaches the model far more about the popular systems than about the rest and generalises accordingly unevenly [92]. The cleaning phase is where physical understanding and data-analytic technique meet most directly, and the analyst who understands the physics of the measurements is far better placed to clean the data soundly than one who treats them as abstract numbers.

The handling of missing values illustrates this meeting of physics and technique with particular clarity, for the choice among the available strategies should be governed by why the values are missing, a question that only physical and procedural knowledge can answer. If a value is missing for reasons unrelated to its magnitude, as when a study simply did not report a variable, the incomplete examples may be discarded or the missing values imputed with relatively little risk of bias; but if a value is missing for reasons related to its magnitude, as when a measurement failed precisely because the quantity lay outside an instrument's range, then both discarding and naive imputation introduce a bias, systematically removing or misrepresenting the extreme cases [92]. The simplest imputation, replacing a missing value with the mean of the observed values, is convenient but distorts the distribution and weakens genuine relationships, whereas more sophisticated imputation that predicts the missing value from the other features preserves more of the structure at the cost of additional assumptions; the choice among them, and indeed the decision whether to impute at all, is a matter of judgement that the physics of the missingness should inform.

The identification of outliers is the aspect of cleaning in which physical judgement is most indispensable and most irreplaceable by mechanical rule. A purely statistical criterion, flagging as an outlier any value that departs from the others by more than some multiple of the scatter, cannot by itself distinguish a genuine but rare physical behaviour from an erroneous measurement, and its blind application will discard valuable information as readily as it removes error. The physically knowledgeable analyst brings to bear an understanding of what values are physically possible, what trends are expected, and what measurement failures are plausible, and with this understanding can adjudicate each flagged value on its merits, retaining the rare but real and removing the impossible or the manifestly mistaken [92]. This adjudication cannot be fully automated, and the attempt to automate it by statistical rule alone is among the more common causes of subtly corrupted datasets, in which either genuine physics has been discarded as noise or errors have been retained as signal, with corresponding damage to the model trained upon the result.

Feature engineering, the construction of the input variables from which the model predicts, is among the most consequential and most creative phases of model development, and in thermal engineering it offers a distinctive opportunity to inject physical knowledge into an otherwise data-driven process. The raw variables of a dataset, such as the particle diameter, the temperature and the flow velocity, can be combined into dimensionless groups, such as the Reynolds, Prandtl and Nusselt numbers, that encode the physical similarity relationships developed in the earlier chapters, and a model trained on such dimensionless features frequently generalises better than one trained on the raw variables, because the dimensionless features capture the underlying physics more directly and reduce the effective dimensionality of the problem [93]. The construction of dimensionless features is worth dwelling upon, because it is the point at which the accumulated physical understanding of the earlier chapters most directly enhances the data-driven enterprise, and its benefits are several and substantial. A model trained on dimensionless groups learns a relationship that is, by construction, independent of the system of units and valid across changes of scale, so that a relationship learned at one size may apply at another in the way that physical similarity guarantees, a transferability that raw dimensional features cannot provide. The grouping of several raw variables into a single dimensionless quantity also reduces the number of features the model must contend with, easing the curse of dimensionality and allowing the limited data to determine a simpler relationship more reliably. And because the dimensionless groups embody the physically meaningful combinations of variables, a model expressed in their terms is more interpretable and its learned relationships more readily checked against physical expectation [93]. The engineering of dimensionless features is thus not a mere convenience but a principled injection of physics into the model, and it exemplifies the integration of physical knowledge with data-driven learning that the previous chapter identified as the field's most fertile opportunity.

The scaling of features to comparable ranges, through normalisation or standardisation, is necessary for many algorithms whose behaviour depends on the relative magnitudes of the features, and the encoding of categorical variables, such as the particle material or the base fluid, into numerical form is required before they can be used. The necessity of scaling arises because several important methods measure distances or form weighted sums in the feature space, and a feature of large numerical magnitude will dominate such computations merely by virtue of its scale, regardless of its true predictive importance, unless the features are first brought to comparable ranges; the tree-based methods discussed earlier are a notable exception, being indifferent to scale, but the neural networks and support-vector methods are not, and for them the neglect of scaling is a common cause of poor performance [93]. The encoding of categorical variables demands its own care, for a naive assignment of consecutive integers to unordered categories imposes a spurious order and spurious distances upon them, implying for instance that one particle material lies numerically between two others, and the preferred encoding that represents each category by a separate indicator avoids this artefact at the cost of increasing the dimensionality, a trade-off that the number of categories governs. Feature selection, the complementary process of choosing which features to retain, guards against the inclusion of irrelevant or redundant variables that add noise and dimensionality without adding predictive value. A dataset may contain features that are physically irrelevant to the target, features that duplicate the information carried by others, and features whose apparent relevance is an artefact of the particular dataset, and the retention of such features degrades generalisation and obscures interpretation. Feature-selection methods range from simple filters that rank features by their statistical association with the target, through wrapper methods that evaluate subsets of features by the performance of the model they produce, to embedded methods that perform selection as part of model training, and the feature-importance scores produced by tree-based models provide a convenient and physically interpretable guide [94]. The discipline of selecting a parsimonious set of physically meaningful features, rather than indiscriminately including every available variable, produces models that generalise better, that are easier to interpret and that are less prone to the spurious correlations that plague high-dimensional modelling of limited data.

The three families of feature-selection method warrant a closer comparison, since each embodies a different balance of thoroughness against cost. The filter methods, which rank features by a statistical measure of their association with the target computed once and independently of any model, are fast and simple but blind to the interactions among features, so that they may discard a feature that is uninformative alone yet valuable in combination with another, or retain redundant features that are individually informative but jointly duplicative. The wrapper methods, which evaluate candidate subsets of features by actually training a model on each and comparing their performance, account for interactions and redundancy directly and so select more effectively, but at a computational cost that grows rapidly with the number of features and that risks overfitting the selection to the particular data. The embedded methods occupy a middle ground, performing selection as an intrinsic part of training, as when a penalty on model complexity drives the coefficients of unhelpful features toward zero, combining much of the effectiveness of the wrapper approach with a cost closer to that of training a single model [94]. The choice among these families depends on the number of features and the computational budget, and in the modest-dimensional problems typical of thermal engineering the more thorough wrapper and embedded methods are usually affordable and preferable.

A caution that pervades feature selection concerns the danger of selecting features using the whole dataset before setting aside the data on which the model will ultimately be evaluated, for such a procedure allows information from the evaluation data to influence the selection and thereby inflates the apparent performance, a subtle instance of the data leakage that Section 7.3 treats more fully. The correct practice performs the feature selection using only the training data, treating the choice of features as itself a part of the model-building process to be validated on genuinely held-out data, so that the reported performance reflects the whole procedure of selection and training as it would apply to new data [94]. This discipline is easily neglected, because the selection of features feels like a preliminary exploration rather than a part of the model, but its neglect is among the more insidious sources of over-optimistic assessment, and its observance is a mark of the rigour that trustworthy modelling demands.

Model development, the training and configuration of the model itself, proceeds once the data are prepared and the features are engineered and selected. The choice of model class, from among the neural networks, support-vector methods and tree-based ensembles surveyed in Chapter 6, is guided by the size and nature of the dataset, the required accuracy and interpretability, and the computational budget, with small tabular datasets often favouring support-vector methods or tree ensembles and larger datasets permitting neural networks. A prudent development strategy begins with a simple and robust model, such as a regularised linear fit or a modest tree ensemble, to establish a baseline against which the value of any greater complexity can be measured, for a sophisticated model that fails to improve upon a simple baseline is not worth its added complexity and opacity, and the establishment of such a baseline guards against the reflexive reach for the most elaborate method available. Only when the simpler model has been shown to leave predictive value unrealised is the progression to a more flexible model warranted, and each step of that progression should be justified by a demonstrated improvement on genuinely held-out data rather than by the mere availability of a more powerful technique [95]. Each model class exposes hyperparameters, configuration choices such as the number and width of a neural network's layers, the kernel and regularisation of a support-vector machine, or the number and depth of the trees in an ensemble, that are not learned from the data during training but must be set beforehand, and whose values strongly influence performance. The systematic tuning of these hyperparameters, through grid search, random search or more sophisticated Bayesian optimisation, guided by the cross-validation procedures of the next section, is an essential and often time-consuming part of model development, and the failure to tune hyperparameters, or the tuning of them against the test data, is a common source of disappointing or misleadingly optimistic results [95].

The three principal strategies for searching the space of hyperparameters differ in their efficiency in a manner worth understanding, since the tuning effort can consume the bulk of a modelling project. Grid search evaluates every combination of a set of candidate values specified for each hyperparameter, an approach that is exhaustive and simple but whose cost multiplies with each additional hyperparameter, so that the tuning of several hyperparameters together quickly becomes prohibitive, and whose fixed grid may miss good values that fall between the specified candidates. Random search, which samples combinations at random from specified ranges, is often more efficient for the same budget, because it explores a greater diversity of values for each individual hyperparameter and is not confined to a rigid lattice, and it is especially advantageous when only a few of the hyperparameters strongly affect performance, as is commonly the case [95]. Bayesian optimisation, the most sophisticated strategy, builds a probabilistic model of how performance depends on the hyperparameters and uses it to choose each successive combination to be maximally informative, converging on good values with fewer evaluations at the cost of greater complexity, and it is the method of choice when each evaluation is expensive.

The cardinal discipline of hyperparameter tuning, whose violation is perhaps the single most common methodological error in the applied literature, is that the tuning must not be guided by the data reserved for final evaluation, for to choose hyperparameters by their performance on the test data is to use the test data in the construction of the model and thereby to forfeit its role as an unbiased estimate of performance on genuinely unseen data. The correct practice, elaborated in the next section, isolates the tuning within the training data through a nested procedure, reserving the test data untouched until the model, hyperparameters and all, is finally fixed [95]. The temptation to peek at the test performance and adjust the model in response is strong and its indulgence easy to rationalise, but each such adjustment leaks information from the test set into the model and inflates the eventual estimate of performance, so that a model tuned in this way may perform far worse in deployment than its reported accuracy promised, a disappointment that has attended many an incautiously developed thermal model.

## 7.3 Model Evaluation, Cross-Validation, and Performance Metrics

The evaluation of a machine-learning model is the phase in which its true worth, its ability to predict accurately on data it has not seen, is measured, and the soundness of the evaluation determines whether the reported performance can be trusted. The cardinal principle of evaluation is that a model must be assessed on data that played no part in its training, because a model can achieve arbitrarily good performance on its training data simply by memorising them, and such performance is no indication of its ability to generalise. The simplest embodiment of this principle is the division of the available data into a training set, used to fit the model, and a separate test set, held back and used only once, at the end, to estimate the model's performance on unseen data [96]. The test set must be representative of the data on which the model will be used, and it must be genuinely held back, for any use of the test data to guide modelling decisions, however indirect, contaminates it and inflates the estimated performance, a subtle but pervasive error known as data leakage.

Data leakage merits fuller treatment because it takes forms subtler than the obvious one of tuning against the test set, and because its consequences are so damaging and so easily concealed. Leakage occurs whenever information that would not be available at the time of a genuine prediction finds its way into the training of the model, and it inflates the apparent performance in a manner that no amount of subsequent scrutiny of the model can detect, since the model performs genuinely well on the contaminated evaluation and fails only when deployed on truly independent data. A common and insidious form arises when the preparation of the data, such as the scaling of features or the imputation of missing values, is performed using statistics computed from the whole dataset before the split, so that the training data are subtly informed by the distribution of the test data; the correct practice computes all such preparation statistics from the training data alone and applies them to the test data as a fixed transformation [96]. Another form arises in datasets containing multiple measurements of closely related conditions, where the presence of near-duplicate examples on both sides of the split allows the model to succeed by recognising rather than by genuinely predicting, and its avoidance requires that related examples be kept together on one side of the split.

The representativeness of the test set is a requirement as important as its independence and, in the nanofluid context, considerably harder to satisfy. A test set drawn at random from the available data estimates the model's performance on data resembling the available data, but if the model is to be applied to conditions systematically different from those the data cover, as it often must be given the sparseness of the design space, the random test set will overstate the performance to be expected in application [96]. A more searching assessment deliberately constructs the test set to resemble the intended application, perhaps by holding out an entire range of conditions rather than a random scatter of points, so that the evaluation measures the model's ability to generalise to genuinely new regions rather than merely to interpolate among familiar ones. This distinction between interpolative and extrapolative evaluation is one that the honest reporting of a thermal model should address, for a model whose reported accuracy rests on interpolative testing may fail badly in the extrapolative use to which the paucity of data so often condemns it.

Cross-validation refines the simple train–test split to make more efficient use of limited data and to obtain a more reliable estimate of performance, and it is especially valuable for the modestly sized datasets typical of nanofluid research. In the most common form, the data are divided into a number of folds, and the model is trained and evaluated repeatedly, each time using a different fold as the test set and the remainder as the training set, so that every example serves once for testing and the performance is averaged over the folds. This procedure yields a more stable estimate of performance than a single split, because it averages over the variability introduced by the particular choice of test set, and it uses the data more efficiently, because every example contributes to both training and testing across the folds [97]. Cross-validation is also the appropriate framework within which to tune hyperparameters, with an inner cross-validation loop used to select the hyperparameters and an outer loop used to estimate the performance of the tuned model, a nested procedure that guards against the optimistic bias that arises when the same data are used both to tune and to evaluate.

The choice of the number of folds involves a trade-off that the practitioner should understand rather than settle by rote. A larger number of folds means that each training set is larger and closer in size to the full dataset, reducing the pessimistic bias that arises because the model is trained on less than all the data, but it also means that the training sets of successive folds overlap heavily and their results are correlated, so that the averaged estimate, while less biased, may be more variable, and the computational cost rises in proportion to the number of folds. The extreme case, in which each fold consists of a single example so that the model is trained as many times as there are examples, makes the fullest use of the data but is costly and yields a high-variance estimate, and it is generally reserved for the smallest datasets where the sacrifice of any example to testing is most keenly felt [97]. A moderate number of folds is the usual compromise, and the modest datasets of nanofluid research, for which the efficient use of data is paramount and the computational cost of many folds is trivial, generally favour the more thorough end of the practical range.

A refinement of cross-validation that is especially pertinent to the heterogeneous datasets of thermal engineering concerns the manner in which the folds are constructed, for a naive random partition may not respect the structure of the data. When the target values are distributed unevenly, a stratified partition that ensures each fold contains a representative range of target values yields a more reliable estimate than a purely random one, which might by chance concentrate the extreme values in a single fold. More importantly, when the data comprise groups of related measurements, such as several points from a single study or a single sample, a partition that keeps each group intact within a single fold prevents the leakage that would arise if related measurements were split across the training and testing of a fold, and it yields an estimate that more honestly reflects the model's ability to generalise to new groups [97]. The thoughtful construction of the folds, in accordance with the structure of the data and the intended use of the model, is a detail that distinguishes a rigorous validation from a merely conventional one.

The metrics by which the performance of a regression model is quantified must be chosen to reflect the aspects of accuracy that matter for the application, and several complementary metrics are conventionally reported together for the regression problems of thermal engineering. The coefficient of determination measures the fraction of the variance in the target that the model explains, ranging from unity for a perfect fit downward, and it provides an intuitive overall measure of fit that is independent of the scale of the target. The root-mean-square error measures the typical magnitude of the prediction error in the units of the target, weighting large errors more heavily than small ones, and it is sensitive to outliers. The mean absolute error also measures the typical error magnitude but weights all errors equally and is consequently more robust to outliers, while the mean absolute percentage error expresses the error as a fraction of the target and permits comparison across quantities of different scale [98].

The complementarity of these metrics, and the reason that several should be reported together rather than any one alone, deserves emphasis because the reliance on a single figure can mislead in characteristic ways. The coefficient of determination, being a relative measure, can appear flatteringly high when the target itself varies widely, since explaining a large variance is easier than explaining a small one, so that an impressive value may conceal a model of modest absolute accuracy applied to a broadly varying target; conversely it can appear poor for a genuinely accurate model applied to a target of small intrinsic variation. The root-mean-square error and the mean absolute error, being absolute measures in the units of the target, convey the practical magnitude of the error that the relative measure obscures, and the comparison of the two reveals the influence of outliers, for a root-mean-square error substantially larger than the mean absolute error signals a distribution of errors dominated by a few large ones [98]. The percentage error, finally, exposes whether the model's accuracy is uniform across the range of the target or concentrated where the target is large, since a fixed absolute error represents a large relative error for small targets, a pattern of particular concern where small values of the predicted quantity are of engineering importance.

The interpretation of any metric against the irreducible uncertainty of the data is a discipline whose neglect leads to both undue satisfaction and undue disappointment. A model cannot predict a quantity more accurately than that quantity is known in the data from which it learned and against which it is judged, so that when the prediction error approaches the measurement uncertainty of the underlying data, the model has extracted essentially all the signal the data contain, and further reduction of the error would represent not improvement but the fitting of noise [99]. The recognition of this floor prevents the futile pursuit of an accuracy the data cannot support and reframes the assessment of a model in terms of whether it performs as well as the data permit rather than whether it attains some absolute standard borrowed from a different context. In the nanofluid domain, where the scatter among independent measurements of the same nominal quantity is often substantial, this floor may be surprisingly high, and a model whose error matches it is performing admirably however modest its accuracy may appear by the standards of a cleaner domain [99]. The reporting of several metrics together, rather than a single figure, guards against the partial picture that any one metric provides, and the parity plot, in which the predicted values are plotted against the measured values with the line of perfect prediction superimposed, offers a graphical complement that reveals patterns, such as systematic bias or heteroscedastic error, that a single number conceals. The interpretation of the metrics must, moreover, be tempered by the quality of the data, for a model cannot be expected to predict its target more accurately than the target is measured, and an apparent error comparable to the measurement uncertainty of the data may indicate a model that is performing as well as the data permit rather than one that is deficient [99]. This connection between model error and data uncertainty, which the scatter of the nanofluid database makes especially salient, is one of the points at which the evaluation of a data-driven model requires the physical and experimental judgement that the earlier chapters develop.

The graphical examination of a model's errors complements the numerical metrics by revealing the structure that a summary number necessarily discards, and it should form a routine part of any careful evaluation. The parity plot, already mentioned, exposes at a glance whether the errors are distributed evenly about the line of perfect prediction or whether the model systematically over-predicts in one range and under-predicts in another, a systematic bias that a single error metric would average away and conceal. A plot of the residuals, the differences between prediction and measurement, against the predicted value or against each feature in turn reveals whether the error is uniform or whether it grows with the magnitude of the prediction or varies with a particular feature, patterns that diagnose specific deficiencies of the model and point toward their remedy [99]. A model whose residuals show a trend against a feature has failed to capture the full dependence on that feature, perhaps because its form is too simple or because an interaction has been missed, and the graphical diagnosis of such trends often suggests the engineering of a new feature or the adoption of a more flexible model in a way that the aggregate metrics never could.

The reporting of a model's performance should, in the interest of honesty and reproducibility, extend beyond a single headline metric to a fuller account that permits the reader to judge the model's suitability for a purpose that may differ from the developer's. Such an account states the metrics on genuinely held-out data, distinguishes interpolative from extrapolative testing, characterises the range of conditions over which the model was trained and may be trusted, and acknowledges the uncertainty in both the data and the resulting predictions [99]. This fuller reporting, still too rare in the applied literature, is what allows a model developed in one study to be soundly reused in another, and its practice is a mark of the maturing of data-driven thermal engineering from a collection of isolated demonstrations toward a cumulative and trustworthy body of engineering knowledge.

The twin failure modes of underfitting and overfitting, which lie at the heart of the modelling craft, are diagnosed through the comparison of a model's performance on its training data and on held-out data. Underfitting, in which the model is too simple to capture the underlying relationship, manifests as poor performance on both the training and the held-out data, and it is remedied by increasing the model's capacity, adding features or reducing regularisation. Overfitting, in which the model is too complex and fits the noise as well as the signal in the training data, manifests as good performance on the training data but poor performance on the held-out data, and it is remedied by reducing the model's capacity, adding regularisation, acquiring more data or simplifying the features [100].

The diagnostic signature that distinguishes the two failure modes, namely the gap between training and held-out performance, is worth stating explicitly because it directs the remedy unambiguously. A model that performs poorly on both the training and the held-out data, with little gap between them, is underfitting, and the appropriate response is to increase its capacity or enrich its features, for the model lacks the flexibility to represent the relationship the data contain. A model that performs well on the training data but markedly worse on the held-out data, with a large gap between them, is overfitting, and the appropriate response is the opposite, to restrain its capacity or acquire more data, for the model has flexibility to spare and is expending it on the noise [100]. The mistaking of one failure for the other, and the consequent application of exactly the wrong remedy, is a common and frustrating error, and the simple discipline of always comparing training against held-out performance, rather than examining either in isolation, is the surest guard against it.

Regularisation, the family of techniques that restrain a model's effective capacity without reducing its nominal size, deserves a concluding word because it is the principal tool for managing the balance between the two failure modes in a controlled and continuous way. Rather than choosing a model's capacity in the coarse discrete steps of adding or removing features or layers, regularisation adds to the training objective a penalty on the complexity of the fitted relationship, so that the model is discouraged from adopting the elaborate, wiggling forms characteristic of overfitting and is drawn toward smoother relationships unless the data strongly justify complexity [100]. The strength of this penalty is itself a hyperparameter, tuned by the cross-validation procedures described earlier, and its adjustment provides a smooth dial by which the model's effective flexibility can be matched to the amount of information the data contain, increasing the penalty when data are scarce and relaxing it as data accumulate. This continuous control over the bias–variance balance, exercised through regularisation and validated through cross-validation, is the mature expression of the modelling craft whose principles this chapter has developed, and it is the technique on which the successful application of flexible models to the limited data of nanofluid research most heavily relies.
