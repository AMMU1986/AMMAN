# Chapter 5. Computational Fluid Dynamics for Heat Exchanger Analysis

## Abstract

Computational fluid dynamics has become an indispensable tool in the analysis and design of heat exchangers because it resolves the velocity, pressure and temperature fields throughout a device with a spatial and temporal detail that empirical correlations cannot provide, and because it can incorporate the geometric complexity and the two-phase transport mechanisms that make nanofluid flows so difficult to predict by classical means. This chapter presents the foundations of computational fluid dynamics as applied to heat-exchanger and nanofluid analysis. It begins with the governing conservation equations of mass, momentum and energy and the closure models required to render the turbulent forms of those equations solvable, surveying the eddy-viscosity and Reynolds-stress families of turbulence model and the circumstances in which each is appropriate. It then addresses the modelling of nanofluid flow and heat transfer, contrasting the single-phase homogeneous approach, in which the suspension is treated as an effective fluid with concentration-dependent properties, with the several two-phase approaches that resolve, to varying degrees, the relative motion of the particles and the liquid. Finally, it examines the numerical practices on which the credibility of any computational result depends, namely mesh generation and the demonstration of mesh independence, the specification of physically appropriate boundary conditions, and the validation of the computational model against experimental or analytical benchmarks. The chapter emphasises throughout that a computational result is only as trustworthy as the modelling choices and numerical practices that produced it, and it prepares the reader to appreciate both the power of computational fluid dynamics as a source of high-fidelity data and its role as a generator of the datasets on which the machine-learning models of later chapters are trained.

## 5.1 Governing Equations and Computational Models

The motion of a fluid and the transport of thermal energy within it are governed by the conservation of mass, momentum and energy, expressed as a coupled set of partial differential equations that, for a Newtonian fluid, constitute the Navier–Stokes equations together with the energy equation. The continuity equation expresses the conservation of mass and, for an incompressible flow, reduces to the requirement that the velocity field be divergence-free. The momentum equations express Newton's second law for a fluid element and balance the local acceleration and the convective transport of momentum against the pressure gradient, the viscous stresses and any body forces such as gravity and buoyancy. The energy equation expresses the conservation of thermal energy and balances the local rate of change and the convective transport of energy against conduction, described by Fourier's law, and any volumetric sources [59]. These equations, closed by an equation of state and by constitutive relations for the fluid properties, in principle describe the flow and heat transfer in a heat exchanger completely, and their numerical solution is the object of computational fluid dynamics. Table 5.1 sets out the governing equations and the physical principle each expresses.

It is worth dwelling on the mathematical character of this system, because the numerical difficulties that attend its solution are direct consequences of that character. The convective terms in the momentum and energy equations are nonlinear, since the transported quantity is carried by the very velocity field that the momentum equations determine, and this nonlinearity is the mathematical seat of turbulence and the reason that closed-form solutions exist only for a handful of idealised laminar cases. The pressure, meanwhile, does not possess its own evolution equation in an incompressible flow; it appears in the momentum equations only through its gradient and functions as a Lagrange multiplier that enforces the divergence-free constraint on the velocity, so that the pressure field must be found indirectly by requiring that the velocity it produces satisfy continuity. This peculiar role of the pressure is what gives the incompressible equations their characteristic elliptic-parabolic structure and what motivates the pressure-correction algorithms, such as the family of segregated solvers that alternate between provisional velocity solutions and pressure corrections, that dominate incompressible heat-exchanger computation [59]. An appreciation of these structural features is not a mathematical nicety but a practical necessity, because the convergence behaviour, the stability limits and the sensitivity to boundary specification that a practitioner encounters in a real computation are all rooted in them.

The coupling between the momentum and energy equations deserves particular attention in heat-exchanger analysis, because it determines whether the flow and thermal fields must be solved simultaneously or may be solved in sequence. When the fluid properties are treated as constant and buoyancy is neglected, the velocity field is independent of the temperature field, and the momentum equations may be solved first and the energy equation solved afterwards on the converged flow, a one-way coupling that economises greatly on computation. When the properties vary appreciably with temperature, as they do for many nanofluids whose viscosity in particular is strongly temperature-dependent, or when buoyancy forces arising from density differences drive or modify the flow, the coupling becomes two-way and the fields must be solved together in an iterative loop, at substantially greater cost and with greater risk of convergence difficulty [60]. The judgement of when the simpler one-way treatment is admissible and when the fuller two-way coupling is required is one of the modelling decisions that most affects both the cost and the fidelity of a heat-exchanger computation, and it should be made deliberately rather than by default.

The direct numerical solution of these equations for the turbulent flows typical of industrial heat exchangers is, for the foreseeable future, computationally infeasible, because turbulence spans a range of length and time scales so wide that resolving all of them would require prohibitively fine meshes and short time steps. Practical computation therefore relies on averaging or filtering the governing equations to remove the smallest scales and on introducing closure models to represent their effect on the resolved scales. The Reynolds-averaged approach, which is by far the most common in heat-exchanger analysis, decomposes each quantity into a mean and a fluctuating part, averages the equations, and thereby introduces the Reynolds stresses, additional unknown correlations of the velocity fluctuations that must be modelled to close the system [60]. The modelling of the Reynolds stresses is the central problem of turbulence closure, and the choice of closure model is one of the most consequential decisions in a computational study, because different models can yield materially different predictions of the heat transfer and pressure drop, particularly in the complex geometries and adverse pressure gradients that heat exchangers present.

The conceptual difficulty at the heart of turbulence closure merits a fuller verbal explanation, because it is often obscured by the machinery of the equations. When the instantaneous equations are averaged, the average of the product of two fluctuating velocities does not vanish even though the average of each fluctuation individually does, and these surviving averaged products, the Reynolds stresses, represent the transport of mean momentum by the turbulent fluctuations. They are genuinely new unknowns for which the averaging procedure supplies no additional equations, so that the averaged system has more unknowns than equations, a deficiency known as the closure problem. Every turbulence model is, at bottom, a proposal for how to express these unknown correlations in terms of quantities that the averaged equations already contain, and because no such proposal can be exact for all flows, every turbulence model embodies a compromise calibrated against a particular class of flows. This is why a model that performs admirably for an attached boundary layer may fail for a separated or swirling flow, and why the reported success of a model in one study offers no guarantee of its success in another whose flow physics differ [61]. The practitioner who understands closure in these terms is guarded against the common error of regarding any turbulence model as a universal truth rather than as a fit-for-purpose approximation.

The eddy-viscosity hypothesis that underlies the most widely used models rests on an analogy that, while powerful, has definite limits that the practitioner must appreciate. The hypothesis supposes that the turbulent transport of momentum can be represented, like the molecular transport, as proportional to the mean rate of strain, with the constant of proportionality being a turbulent viscosity that, unlike the molecular viscosity, is a property of the flow rather than of the fluid. This analogy is serviceable where the turbulence is close to a local equilibrium and its structure is not strongly anisotropic, which describes much of the flow in a plain tube or a simple duct, but it breaks down where the turbulence is far from equilibrium or strongly anisotropic, as near a sharp bend, in a strongly swirling flow, or in the wake behind a tube or baffle. In these regions the assumption that a single scalar viscosity can represent the directional character of the turbulent stresses is simply false, and predictions of local heat transfer there should be regarded with corresponding caution [60]. The turbulent Prandtl number, which relates the turbulent transport of heat to the turbulent transport of momentum and is commonly assigned a constant value close to unity, embodies a further analogy of the same kind, and its constancy is likewise an approximation that fails where the thermal and momentum fields are dissimilar, an important caveat for heat-transfer prediction specifically.

The eddy-viscosity family of turbulence models, which represents the Reynolds stresses through an analogy with the molecular viscous stresses using a turbulent or eddy viscosity, is the most widely used because of its robustness and modest computational cost. Within this family, the two-equation models, which solve transport equations for two turbulence quantities from which the eddy viscosity is constructed, are the workhorses of engineering computation. The model based on the turbulent kinetic energy and its dissipation rate is well suited to fully turbulent flows away from walls and is widely used for the bulk of a heat-exchanger flow, but it requires wall functions or damping to represent the near-wall region correctly. The model based on the turbulent kinetic energy and the specific dissipation rate performs better in the near-wall region and in flows with adverse pressure gradients and separation, and blended formulations combine the near-wall accuracy of the latter with the free-stream robustness of the former [61]. The more elaborate Reynolds-stress models, which solve transport equations for the individual components of the Reynolds-stress tensor rather than assuming an isotropic eddy viscosity, capture the anisotropy of turbulence and the effects of streamline curvature and swirl that the eddy-viscosity models miss, at the cost of greater computational expense and reduced numerical robustness. Large-eddy simulation, which resolves the large energy-containing eddies directly and models only the smaller subgrid scales, offers still higher fidelity and is increasingly used for detailed studies of heat-exchanger components, but its computational cost remains too high for routine design [62].

The manner in which the continuous governing equations are converted into the algebraic system that a computer can solve is itself a source of both accuracy and error, and a verbal grasp of the principal discretisation schemes informs the interpretation of any computed result. The finite-volume method, which predominates in heat-exchanger computation, divides the domain into a mesh of contiguous control volumes and enforces the conservation of mass, momentum and energy over each one exactly, an approach whose intrinsic conservation is a decisive advantage where the accurate accounting of heat and mass fluxes is the very object of the study. The values of the transported quantities are stored at the centres of the control volumes, and their fluxes across the faces must be interpolated from these stored values by a numerical scheme whose choice trades accuracy against stability. A first-order upwind scheme, which takes the face value from the upstream cell, is robust and never produces unphysical oscillations but introduces a numerical diffusion that smears gradients and can seriously corrupt a heat-transfer prediction; a second-order scheme, which accounts for the variation of the quantity across the cell, greatly reduces this false diffusion at the cost of a susceptibility to oscillation that may require limiting [59]. The prevalence of first-order results in the older literature is one reason for caution in accepting reported enhancements uncritically, since numerical diffusion can masquerade as, or mask, a physical effect. The false diffusion is at its most insidious when the flow crosses the mesh lines obliquely, as it does in the swirling and recirculating passages of a real exchanger, for there the upwind scheme smears the transported temperature across streamlines and artificially flattens the very gradients that determine the heat transfer, so that a coarse first-order computation may report a plausible but spuriously uniform temperature field that owes more to the scheme than to the physics [59].

The choice between a Reynolds-stress model and large-eddy simulation, where fidelity beyond the reach of the two-equation models is required, turns on the nature of the information sought as much as on the available resources. The Reynolds-stress model, remaining within the averaged framework, delivers only the mean fields and the statistics of the turbulence, but it does so at a cost that, while several times that of a two-equation model, remains within reach of routine computation, and it repays that cost wherever the anisotropy of the turbulence or the effects of strong streamline curvature and swirl govern the heat transfer, as around tightly curved tubes and in cyclonic passages. Large-eddy simulation, by resolving the unsteady large-scale motions directly, yields not only accurate mean fields but the fluctuating detail from which unsteady loads, mixing and the spectral content of the flow can be assessed, information of genuine value for understanding flow-induced vibration or the intermittent thermal loading of a surface, but at a cost so much greater, and with a demand for temporal resolution and long averaging times so much more severe, that its use remains confined to research and to the detailed study of components rather than the design of complete exchangers [62]. The pragmatic middle path offered by hybrid formulations, which apply the averaged treatment in the near-wall region where resolving the small eddies would be ruinously expensive and switch to large-eddy resolution in the detached flow away from walls, is increasingly attractive for heat-exchanger components in which separated and recirculating regions dominate the thermal behaviour.

The iterative solution of the resulting algebraic system introduces a further category of numerical consideration, namely the convergence of the iteration and the criteria by which it is judged complete. Because the equations are coupled and nonlinear, they are solved not in one step but by repeated sweeps that progressively reduce the imbalance, or residual, in each equation, and the computation is deemed converged when these residuals fall below chosen thresholds. A common and serious error is to accept a solution whose residuals have merely stagnated at a high level, or to set the thresholds so loosely that the iteration halts before the solution has genuinely settled, with the consequence that the reported result reflects an unconverged intermediate state rather than the solution of the model [60]. The monitoring of an integrated quantity of physical interest, such as the outlet temperature or the total heat transfer, until it too has ceased to change provides a more meaningful convergence check than the residuals alone, and its use is a mark of careful practice. The selection among these approaches is a matter of balancing the required fidelity against the available computational resources, and it is informed by the nature of the flow: a simple developing pipe flow may be adequately captured by a two-equation model, whereas the swirling, separated flow around a baffle in a shell-and-tube exchanger may demand a Reynolds-stress model or large-eddy simulation for quantitative accuracy.

The two principal two-equation models deserve a more detailed comparison, because the choice between them is among the most frequent that the heat-exchanger analyst must make. The model built on the turbulent kinetic energy and its dissipation rate derives the length and velocity scales of the turbulence from those two transported quantities, and its dissipation equation is calibrated for high-Reynolds-number flow away from walls, where it behaves robustly and converges readily. Its weakness is that the dissipation-rate variable behaves badly as a wall is approached, requiring either the bridging of the near-wall region by empirical wall functions, which sacrifices local accuracy in exchange for economy, or the addition of damping functions that restore near-wall validity at the cost of numerical stiffness and a demand for very fine near-wall meshes. The model built on the specific dissipation rate, by contrast, is well behaved all the way to the wall and integrates naturally through the viscous sublayer, giving it a genuine advantage in the prediction of wall heat transfer and of the onset of separation under an adverse pressure gradient, but it is more sensitive to the value of the turbulence quantity specified at the inlet and in the free stream, where an ill-chosen value can contaminate the solution [61]. The blended formulation that has become the default choice for many heat-exchanger studies was devised precisely to secure the near-wall fidelity of the one model and the free-stream robustness of the other by switching smoothly between them according to the distance from the wall, and its popularity reflects a hard-won practical consensus rather than any theoretical superiority.

A further consideration that bears on the selection of a turbulence approach, and one often underappreciated, is the interaction between the turbulence model and the transitional character of many heat-exchanger flows. The Reynolds numbers encountered in compact and micro-scale exchangers frequently place the flow in the transitional regime between laminar and fully turbulent, where the standard turbulence models, calibrated for fully developed turbulence, systematically mispredict the heat transfer because they assume turbulence to be present and active throughout the domain. Transition-sensitive formulations, which augment the transport equations with additional quantities that track the intermittency of the turbulence and the onset of transition, have been developed to address this regime, and their use can markedly improve the prediction of heat transfer in exchangers operating near the transitional Reynolds numbers, though at the cost of additional equations and additional empirical calibration [62]. The nanofluid context sharpens this issue, because the altered viscosity of a suspension shifts the Reynolds number at which transition occurs, so that a computation which assumes the wrong flow regime may err substantially even before any nanofluid-specific modelling is considered. The lesson that pervades this section is that the selection of a computational model is not a matter of choosing the most elaborate option available but of matching the model to the physics of the particular flow, and that this matching requires the physical judgement that only an understanding of the underlying fluid mechanics can supply.

## 5.2 CFD Modelling of Nanofluid Flow and Heat Transfer

The computational modelling of nanofluid flow requires a decision about how to represent the two-phase nature of the suspension, and this decision divides the field into two broad approaches whose relative merits have been debated extensively. The single-phase homogeneous approach treats the nanofluid as a single effective fluid whose density, viscosity, conductivity and specific heat are the effective properties of the suspension, evaluated from mixing rules or measurements as functions of concentration and temperature, and whose flow and heat transfer are then computed exactly as for any single-phase fluid. This approach is computationally economical, because it solves only one set of conservation equations, and it is straightforward to implement within any standard computational framework, requiring only that the effective properties be supplied [63]. Its accuracy depends entirely on the accuracy of the effective properties, and where those properties are known reliably, the single-phase approach frequently reproduces experimental heat-transfer data well, lending support to the view that much of the observed nanofluid behaviour can be explained by property changes alone.

The practical appeal of the single-phase approach extends beyond its economy to the transparency of its logic, for it isolates the question of nanofluid heat transfer into two separable parts, namely the determination of the effective properties and the computation of the flow of a fluid possessing those properties. This separation allows the considerable body of knowledge and validated numerical practice accumulated for single-phase heat transfer to be brought to bear directly, and it means that an error in the prediction can, in principle, be traced either to the property models or to the flow computation rather than being entangled in a single opaque calculation. The approach does, however, carry an implicit assumption whose importance is easily overlooked, namely that the particles are distributed uniformly and move with the fluid, so that there is no local variation of concentration and no relative motion to transport heat. Where this assumption holds, as it often does in well-dispersed turbulent flows in which the vigorous mixing keeps the particles uniformly suspended, the single-phase model is not merely a convenient approximation but a physically defensible one; where it fails, as in laminar flows in which particles may migrate across streamlines under the influence of shear and temperature gradients, the single-phase model omits genuine physics and its agreement with experiment, if any, is fortuitous [63]. The discerning analyst therefore treats the choice of the single-phase approach as a hypothesis about the flow physics to be justified rather than as a default to be adopted unreflectively.

A recurring subtlety in the single-phase treatment concerns the temperature dependence of the effective properties, which for nanofluids is frequently stronger than for the base fluid and which couples the property evaluation to the very temperature field the computation seeks to determine. When the properties are evaluated at a single representative temperature, the computation is simplified but the local variation of, in particular, the viscosity across the thermal boundary layer is neglected, and this neglect can distort the predicted velocity profile and hence the heat transfer, especially in the near-wall region where the temperature gradient is steepest. A more faithful treatment evaluates the properties locally as functions of the computed temperature, restoring the two-way coupling between the thermal and momentum fields discussed earlier, at the cost of the additional iteration that such coupling entails [64]. The choice between these treatments is another instance of the pervasive trade-off between fidelity and cost, and its resolution depends on the magnitude of the temperature variation within the domain and on the sensitivity of the quantity of interest to the property variation.

The two-phase approaches, by contrast, recognise that the particles and the liquid may move relative to one another and that this relative motion may contribute to the heat transfer through mechanisms absent from the homogeneous model. The mixture model, the least elaborate of the two-phase approaches, solves the conservation equations for the mixture but permits a relative, or slip, velocity between the phases, computed from an algebraic relation that balances the forces on the particles; it captures the first-order effects of relative motion at modest additional cost. The Eulerian two-fluid model solves separate conservation equations for the liquid and particle phases, coupled through interphase transfer terms, and thereby represents the relative motion more completely, at the cost of greater complexity and the need for models of the interphase forces. The Eulerian–Lagrangian approach tracks individual particles or parcels of particles through the computed liquid flow, resolving the particle motion in detail but at a computational cost that limits it to dilute suspensions and small domains [64]. The choice among these approaches balances fidelity against cost, and the literature reports that two-phase models sometimes, though not always, improve agreement with experiment relative to the single-phase model, particularly in laminar flows where particle migration is significant.

The persistence of the debate between single-phase and two-phase advocates is itself instructive, for it reveals that the question of which approach is superior admits no context-free answer. Studies that report a decisive advantage for the two-phase treatment are frequently those conducted in the laminar regime at low flow rates, where the weak mixing permits the particle distribution to become genuinely non-uniform and where the relative motion consequently carries physical significance that the homogeneous model cannot represent. Studies that find the single-phase treatment fully adequate, by contrast, are frequently those conducted in vigorously turbulent flow, where the intense mixing overwhelms any tendency toward segregation and renders the suspension effectively homogeneous, so that the additional machinery of a two-phase model captures nothing the simpler model omits. Read in this light, the apparently conflicting conclusions of the literature are not contradictory but complementary, each valid within the regime it examined, and the resolution of the debate lies not in declaring one approach universally correct but in recognising the regime dependence of the answer [64]. This recognition is precisely the kind of nuanced, condition-dependent knowledge that resists compression into a simple rule and that motivates the data-driven synthesis of heterogeneous results pursued in later chapters.

The mixture model occupies an intermediate position that makes it attractive for many practical studies, and its logic repays a closer verbal examination. Rather than solving a full set of equations for each phase, it solves the conservation equations for the mixture as a whole and then reconstructs the distribution of the particle phase from a transport equation for the particle volume fraction, within which the crucial ingredient is the slip velocity that describes how fast the particles move relative to the mixture. This slip velocity is not itself computed from a momentum balance for the particles but is supplied by an algebraic closure that assumes the particle motion to be governed by a local balance between the drag exerted by the liquid and the driving forces of gravity, pressure gradient and particle inertia. The assumption is that the particles respond so quickly to changes in the flow that their relative velocity is always at its local equilibrium value, an assumption well justified for the very small and nearly neutrally buoyant particles of a nanofluid, whose response time is exceedingly short. For this reason the mixture model, despite its economy, often captures the leading effects of particle migration and non-uniform concentration adequately, and it is frequently the most sensible choice when the fuller two-fluid or particle-tracking approaches would be prohibitively expensive [64].

The Eulerian two-fluid and Eulerian–Lagrangian approaches differ in a manner that is best understood through the contrast between describing the particle phase as a continuous field and describing it as a collection of discrete entities. The two-fluid model treats the particles as a second interpenetrating continuum, defined everywhere by its own volume fraction, velocity and temperature fields, and coupled to the liquid through terms that exchange momentum and energy between the phases; this description is natural where the particles are numerous and their collective behaviour is of interest, but it requires closure models for the interphase drag, lift and heat transfer whose forms are uncertain for nanoscale particles. The Lagrangian approach instead follows the trajectories of individual particles or representative parcels through the computed liquid field, computing the force and heat exchange on each, which yields a detailed and physically direct picture of the particle behaviour but becomes computationally intractable when the number of particles is large, as it always is in a nanofluid, forcing the use of representative parcels and statistical arguments whose validity must be examined [65]. For the extreme particle numbers and vanishingly small particle sizes characteristic of nanofluids, the continuum descriptions are generally more tractable than particle tracking, and the choice among the continuum approaches turns on whether the relative motion of the phases is significant enough to warrant the expense of resolving it fully.

The modelling of the additional transport mechanisms specific to nanofluids, principally the Brownian motion of the particles and the thermophoretic migration driven by temperature gradients, can be incorporated into either the single-phase or the two-phase framework through additional terms in the governing equations or through concentration-dependent property models. Brownian motion is represented either through a Brownian contribution to the effective conductivity in the single-phase model or through a Brownian diffusion term in the particle-phase equation in the two-phase model, and thermophoresis is represented through a corresponding migration flux. The inclusion of these mechanisms improves the physical completeness of the model but introduces additional empirical coefficients whose values are uncertain, so that the gain in fidelity is not always accompanied by a gain in accuracy [65]. This tension, between the physical completeness of a model and the reliability of the empirical inputs it requires, recurs throughout nanofluid computation and is one of the reasons that data-driven models, which sidestep the need to specify uncertain mechanistic coefficients, have attracted interest as complements to physics-based computation.

The mechanisms of Brownian motion and thermophoresis warrant a fuller conceptual account, because their relative importance is frequently misjudged. Brownian motion is the incessant random agitation of the suspended particles by the thermal motion of the surrounding liquid molecules, and it produces a diffusive flux of particles from regions of high concentration toward regions of low concentration, tending to homogenise the suspension. Its direct contribution to heat transport, through the micro-convection that the darting particles induce in their immediate neighbourhood, is a matter of continuing debate, with some analyses finding it appreciable at small particle sizes and elevated temperatures and others finding it negligible compared with the enhancement attributable to the raised effective conductivity alone [65]. Thermophoresis, by contrast, is the migration of particles down a temperature gradient, driven by the imbalance of molecular impacts on the hotter and cooler faces of a particle, and it drives particles away from heated walls toward the cooler core, establishing a concentration distribution that is non-uniform even when the flow is steady. Because these two mechanisms act in concert, the former tending to homogenise and the latter to segregate, the equilibrium concentration profile in a heated channel reflects their balance, and a computation that includes one without the other, or that assigns implausible values to their coefficients, may produce a concentration field that is qualitatively wrong.

The critical assessment that this section invites is that no single modelling approach is uniformly best, and that the apparent sophistication of a two-phase or mechanism-resolved computation is no guarantee of superior accuracy when the empirical coefficients it requires are poorly known. A single-phase computation with well-characterised effective properties may well outperform a two-phase computation burdened with uncertain interphase and migration closures, and the reverse may hold where relative motion genuinely dominates. This state of affairs, in which the choice of model is contingent on the flow regime, the particle characteristics and the reliability of the available property and closure data, is precisely the kind of high-dimensional, judgement-laden problem for which data-driven surrogates offer a pragmatic alternative, learning the input–output relationship from data without committing to any particular mechanistic hypothesis [66]. It is for this reason that the generation of validated computational data and the training of data-driven models upon it, treated next, is not a competitor to physics-based computation but its natural extension.

The generation of high-fidelity data is one of the most valuable contributions that computational fluid dynamics makes to the broader project of this book, because a validated computational model can produce, at reasonable cost, the large and systematically varied datasets that machine-learning models require for training and that would be prohibitively expensive to obtain by experiment. A computational study can vary the concentration, the flow rate, the geometry and the boundary conditions across a designed range, recording the resulting heat transfer and pressure drop at each combination, and thereby populate a training set that spans the design space more densely and more uniformly than the scattered experimental literature ever could [66]. The combination of computational data generation with machine-learning surrogate modelling, in which a computational model produces the data and a machine-learning model learns to reproduce its input–output relationship at negligible query cost, is an increasingly important paradigm that the later chapters develop, and it depends critically on the fidelity and validation of the computational model that generates the data.

The design of a computational campaign intended to generate training data differs in important respects from the design of a study intended to answer a single engineering question, and these differences deserve explicit statement. Where a single-purpose study seeks the most accurate possible answer at one operating point, a data-generation campaign seeks a set of answers that spans the design space as informatively as possible within a fixed computational budget, and this shifts the emphasis from local accuracy to the coverage and balance of the sampled points. The principles of statistical design of experiments, which prescribe how to place a limited number of sample points so as to extract the maximum information about the response over a multidimensional space, apply as much to computational sampling as to physical experimentation, and a campaign that varies its inputs on a space-filling design will yield a training set from which a surrogate learns far more reliably than one that varies inputs one at a time or clusters them near familiar operating points [66]. The economy of computation relative to experiment makes it feasible to populate such designs densely, but the economy is not unlimited, and the disciplined allocation of the available runs to the most informative points is what distinguishes a productive campaign from a wasteful one.

A caution must temper the enthusiasm for computational data generation, and it follows directly from the arguments of the preceding sections. A surrogate model learns the behaviour of the computational model that generated its training data, not the behaviour of the physical system, so that any systematic error in the computation, whether from an inappropriate turbulence closure, an uncertain nanofluid property model or an unresolved near-wall region, is faithfully learned by the surrogate and propagated into every prediction it makes. The surrogate cannot correct the errors of its teacher; it can only reproduce them cheaply. For this reason the value of a computationally generated dataset is bounded above by the fidelity of the computation that produced it, and the validation of that computation against physical benchmarks, the subject of the section that follows, is not an optional refinement but the precondition on which the entire data-driven enterprise built upon it depends [66]. The practitioner who generates training data by computation therefore inherits, undiminished, the full burden of verification and validation, and any claim made for a surrogate trained on such data must ultimately rest on the demonstrated credibility of the underlying computational model.

## 5.3 Mesh Independence, Boundary Conditions, and Model Validation

The credibility of any computational result rests on three pillars of numerical practice, the demonstration that the result is independent of the computational mesh, the specification of physically appropriate boundary conditions, and the validation of the model against trusted benchmarks, and the neglect of any one of them can render an otherwise sophisticated computation worthless. The discretisation of the governing equations onto a finite mesh introduces a numerical error that diminishes as the mesh is refined, and the demonstration that a computed quantity of interest has ceased to change appreciably with further refinement, known as a mesh-independence or grid-independence study, is the minimum evidence required to establish that the result reflects the physics of the model rather than the artefacts of the discretisation. A mesh-independence study computes the quantity of interest, such as the Nusselt number or the pressure drop, on a sequence of progressively finer meshes and identifies the mesh beyond which further refinement produces a change smaller than a chosen tolerance, selecting that mesh as the basis for subsequent computation because it delivers mesh-independent results at the least cost [67].

The rigour of a mesh-independence study can be strengthened considerably beyond the mere observation that a quantity has ceased to change, and the more disciplined practice is worth describing because it distinguishes a defensible demonstration from a superficial one. When the meshes in the sequence are refined in a controlled ratio, the rate at which the computed quantity approaches its limiting value can be examined to confirm that the solution is converging at the order of accuracy that the numerical scheme is supposed to deliver, and a departure from the expected rate is a warning that something is amiss, whether an unresolved feature, an inconsistent boundary treatment or a scheme operating outside its asymptotic range. From such a controlled sequence one can also estimate, by extrapolation, the value that would be obtained on an infinitely fine mesh and hence the discretisation error remaining on the chosen mesh, converting the qualitative notion of mesh independence into a quantitative error bound [67]. This estimated discretisation uncertainty can then be reported alongside the result and, importantly, compared with the other uncertainties in the study, so that effort is not lavished on reducing a discretisation error that is already small compared with the uncertainty in the fluid properties or the experimental benchmark.

The quantity chosen to monitor in a mesh-independence study is itself a matter of judgement, because a mesh that is adequate for one quantity may be inadequate for another. An integral quantity such as the overall pressure drop or the mean Nusselt number converges more readily with mesh refinement than a local quantity such as the peak wall heat flux or the location of a separation point, because integration averages out local errors that a point value exposes. A study that demonstrates independence of an integral quantity while the local quantities of true interest remain mesh-dependent gives a false assurance, and the prudent practitioner therefore verifies independence of the specific quantities on which the engineering conclusions depend rather than of some convenient global measure alone [68]. In nanofluid heat-transfer computation, where the near-wall gradients are steep and the heat transfer is the quantity of central interest, this consideration argues for monitoring the wall heat flux or the local Nusselt number directly and for refining the mesh preferentially in the near-wall region where the gradients are concentrated. A subtlety that the plateau in such a study can conceal deserves mention, for the appearance of a plateau is a necessary but not a sufficient sign of true independence. A computed quantity may plateau over a range of intermediate meshes and then, on much finer meshes, resume changing as some feature previously unresolved by even the "converged" mesh finally becomes captured, a behaviour that a study terminating at the first apparent plateau would miss entirely. This pitfall is most dangerous in flows with subtle features, such as weak secondary flows or the incipient separation on a curved surface, whose influence on the monitored quantity is small until the mesh becomes fine enough to resolve them properly. The guard against it is to extend the refinement sequence well beyond the first sign of a plateau and to examine the flow field itself, not merely the integrated quantity, for features that remain mesh-dependent, so that independence is confirmed by the settling of the physics and not merely by the flattening of a single curve [67].

The near-wall region demands particular attention in the meshing of heat-transfer computations because the steep gradients of velocity and temperature there control both the wall shear stress and the heat flux, and because the treatment of this region interacts with the choice of turbulence model. Wall-function approaches, which bridge the near-wall region with algebraic profiles rather than resolving it, require that the first mesh point lie within a specified range of the dimensionless wall distance, whereas wall-resolving approaches require that the mesh be fine enough to place several points within the viscous sublayer, a far more stringent requirement that greatly increases the mesh count. The consistency between the near-wall meshing and the turbulence model is essential, because a mesh that is too coarse for a wall-resolving model or too fine for a wall-function model will yield erroneous heat-transfer predictions regardless of the quality of the rest of the computation [68]. This coupling of meshing and modelling is a frequent source of error in inexpert computation and underscores the need for the disciplined verification that a mesh-independence study provides.

The dimensionless wall distance that governs this coupling is a measure, evaluated at the first mesh point away from the wall, of how far that point lies into the structured layering of a turbulent boundary layer, expressed in the natural viscous units of the near-wall flow. A wall-function approach presumes that the first point lies in the logarithmic region where the mean velocity follows a known universal profile, and it uses that profile to supply the wall shear stress and heat flux without resolving the intervening viscous sublayer, which requires that the first point be neither so close to the wall that it falls below the logarithmic region nor so far that it lies beyond it. A wall-resolving approach, by contrast, requires the first point to lie deep within the viscous sublayer, with several further points distributed across the sublayer, so that the steep gradients there are captured directly [68]. The difficulty in practice is that the dimensionless wall distance depends on the very solution being computed, through the wall shear stress, so that the meshing cannot be verified until after a solution is obtained, and an initial mesh must be judged, computed and then refined or coarsened in light of the resulting wall distances, an iterative discipline that the inexperienced often omit to their cost.

A further meshing consideration specific to the complex geometries of real heat exchangers concerns the quality of the mesh cells themselves, as distinct from their number, because a mesh of poor quality can corrupt a solution regardless of how fine it is. Cells that are excessively skewed, stretched or twisted degrade the accuracy of the discretised gradients and can impair or prevent convergence, and the intricate passages, sharp corners and curved surfaces of finned, coiled and baffled exchangers make such cells difficult to avoid entirely. The choice between a structured mesh, whose orderly array of cells aligns with the flow and offers superior accuracy and economy where the geometry permits, and an unstructured mesh, whose flexibility accommodates complex shapes at some cost in cell quality and count, is itself a consequential decision, and hybrid strategies that use structured layers near the walls within an unstructured interior are common in heat-exchanger practice [69]. The verification of mesh quality, through the inspection of skewness and aspect-ratio measures, is a companion to the verification of mesh independence, and neglect of either undermines the credibility of the result.

The specification of boundary conditions translates the physical situation into mathematical constraints on the computed fields, and errors in this translation are among the most common and most damaging in computational practice. The inlet condition specifies the velocity, temperature and, for turbulent computations, the turbulence quantities of the entering flow, and an incorrect specification of the inlet turbulence can propagate through the domain and distort the heat-transfer prediction. The outlet condition typically specifies the pressure or imposes a fully developed condition, and its placement sufficiently far downstream of the region of interest is necessary to avoid contaminating that region. The wall condition specifies either the temperature, the heat flux or a convective coupling, and the choice must match the physical situation being modelled, because a constant-temperature and a constant-heat-flux wall yield different Nusselt numbers even for otherwise identical flows [69]. The care with which boundary conditions are specified is, like mesh independence, a determinant of the credibility of a computation that no amount of downstream sophistication can compensate.

The specification of inlet turbulence quantities deserves particular emphasis because it is at once influential and frequently guessed. A turbulent computation requires values for the turbulence quantities at the inlet, expressed most conveniently through a turbulence intensity and a length scale, and these values seed the turbulence that develops within the domain. Where the inlet flow is genuinely known, as when it issues from a long upstream duct, physically reasonable values can be assigned; but where it is uncertain, the assigned values become a source of error that, with the near-wall-integrating turbulence models especially, can propagate downstream and distort the heat-transfer prediction appreciably [69]. A prudent study examines the sensitivity of its results to the assumed inlet turbulence and, where the sensitivity is significant, either extends the domain upstream so that the flow forgets its imposed inlet condition before reaching the region of interest or obtains better inlet information from experiment or from a separate computation of the upstream passage.

The choice of thermal wall condition, between a prescribed temperature and a prescribed heat flux, is not a mere technicality but a decision about which physical situation is being represented, and it carries quantitative consequences that the analyst must anticipate. A wall held at constant temperature and a wall subjected to constant heat flux produce different temperature distributions in the fluid and hence different values of the Nusselt number even for otherwise identical flows, the difference being most pronounced in laminar and developing flows and diminishing but not vanishing in turbulent flows. When a computation is to be compared against a correlation or an experiment, the wall condition of the computation must match that of the benchmark, and a mismatch here is a common and avoidable cause of apparent disagreement [70]. In many real exchangers, moreover, the true thermal condition is neither constant temperature nor constant flux but a conjugate coupling in which the heat conducted through the solid wall and the heat convected by the fluid are solved together, and the neglect of this conjugate coupling, by imposing a simplified condition at the fluid–solid interface, is an approximation whose adequacy depends on the thermal conductivity and thickness of the wall relative to the convective resistance of the fluid.

Validation, the demonstration that the computational model reproduces trusted experimental or analytical results within an acceptable tolerance, is the final and most important pillar of computational credibility, because it tests the entire chain of modelling and numerical choices against reality. A validation study compares the computed quantities of interest with benchmark data, whether from a canonical analytical solution, a well-characterised experiment or a recognised correlation, across a range of conditions, and it establishes the range of conditions over which the model may be trusted and the magnitude of the error to be expected. A model validated for one fluid, geometry and regime cannot be assumed to hold for others, and the extrapolation of a computational model beyond its validated range is a frequent source of error [70].

It is useful to distinguish verification from validation with some care, because the two are often conflated and the conflation breeds confused claims of credibility. Verification asks whether the equations of the model are being solved correctly, and it is a matter of mathematics and numerics, addressed by mesh-independence studies, by the checking of the convergence of the iterative solver, by comparison against analytical solutions of simplified problems, and by confirming that the discretisation error has been driven acceptably small. Validation asks the different and deeper question of whether the correct equations are being solved, that is, whether the model with its chosen closures and properties represents the physical reality, and it can be addressed only by comparison against physical measurement. A computation may be impeccably verified yet invalid, because it solves the wrong model accurately, or it may fortuitously agree with an experiment while being poorly verified, so that its apparent validity rests on a cancellation of errors that will not persist under changed conditions [71]. The disciplined separation of these two questions guards against the pitfall of mistaking numerical convergence for physical correctness, a pitfall into which the visually persuasive output of modern computational tools makes it dangerously easy to fall.

The validation hierarchy provides a systematic framework for building confidence, and its logic is one of ascending complexity. Confidence is established first at the level of simple unit problems whose behaviour is known from analytical solution or from highly controlled experiment, then at the level of benchmark cases that isolate a single feature of the intended application, such as flow in a heated straight tube, and only then at the level of the full application in its geometric and physical complexity. Confidence earned at the lower levels does not transfer automatically to the higher ones, but a failure at a lower level indicates a deficiency that will certainly corrupt the higher, so that the hierarchy functions both to build confidence upward and to localise faults downward [71]. For nanofluid heat-exchanger computation this hierarchy is especially valuable, because it allows the separate validation of the property models, the single-phase or two-phase flow treatment and the heat-transfer prediction, so that when the full computation disagrees with an experiment the discrepancy can be attributed, at least in part, to the component responsible rather than being left as an unresolved global failure. For nanofluid computation, validation is complicated by the scatter in the experimental database and by the uncertainty in the effective properties, so that a computation may disagree with an experiment either because the model is deficient or because the properties or the measurements are inaccurate, an ambiguity that makes rigorous validation both more difficult and more essential [71]. The disciplined practice of verification and validation, embodied in mesh-independence studies, careful boundary-condition specification and benchmark comparison, is what distinguishes a computational result that can inform design from one that merely produces plausible-looking contours, and it is the foundation on which the use of computational data to train machine-learning models, developed in the chapters that follow, ultimately rests [72].

The peculiar difficulty of validating nanofluid computation against a scattered experimental database deserves a closing reflection, because it recasts the very meaning of validation in this field. When the benchmark measurements themselves disagree among laboratories by margins that rival or exceed the effects being modelled, a computation cannot be validated against a single authoritative value but must instead be judged against a band of plausible values, and agreement within that band is the most that can honestly be claimed. This state of affairs demands a shift from the deterministic notion of validation, in which a computation either matches the truth or does not, to a probabilistic one, in which the computation and the experiment are each attended by their own uncertainties and validation asks whether the two are consistent within those uncertainties [71]. The estimation of the computational uncertainty, aggregating the discretisation error established by the mesh study, the uncertainty in the input properties and the sensitivity to modelling choices, and its comparison against the scatter of the experimental benchmark, is the mature form of validation toward which the field is moving, and it aligns naturally with the uncertainty-aware data-driven methods introduced in later chapters [72].

Taken together, the three pillars examined in this section, mesh independence, boundary-condition fidelity and benchmark validation, constitute a discipline whose observance is what elevates a computation from an exercise in software operation to a source of engineering knowledge. Each pillar addresses a distinct way in which a computation can mislead, the mesh study guarding against numerical artefact, the boundary conditions against physical misrepresentation of the situation, and the validation against the deeper failure of the model to represent reality, and the neglect of any one leaves a gap through which error enters undetected. The chapters that follow build directly upon this foundation, for the machine-learning models they develop are only as trustworthy as the data on which they are trained, and where those data originate in computation, their trustworthiness is precisely the trustworthiness that the disciplined practice described here confers [72]. The transition from physics-based computation to data-driven modelling is thus not an abandonment of the rigour cultivated in this chapter but its extension into a new domain, carrying forward the same insistence that a result be earned through verification and validation rather than merely produced.
