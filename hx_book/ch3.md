# Chapter 3. Heat Transfer Characteristics of Nanofluids

## Abstract

Having established the thermophysical properties of nanofluids in the previous chapter, the present chapter turns to the question that ultimately determines their engineering value, namely how those properties translate into convective heat-transfer performance in flowing systems. The convective behaviour of a nanofluid is not a simple consequence of its enhanced conductivity, because the same particles that raise the conductivity also alter the viscosity, the near-wall structure of the flow and, according to a considerable body of evidence, the effective boundary conditions at the wall, so that the observed enhancement in the heat transfer coefficient frequently exceeds what the conductivity increase alone would predict. This chapter examines convective heat transfer and flow behaviour in both the laminar and turbulent regimes, discusses the mechanisms that have been proposed to account for the anomalous enhancement, and analyses the influence of the principal design variables of particle concentration, size, shape and material. It then addresses the two quantities that jointly determine the practical worth of a nanofluid in a flowing system, the Nusselt number that measures the heat-transfer benefit and the pressure drop and friction factor that measure the hydraulic cost, and it introduces the thermal-hydraulic performance criteria that combine them into a single index of merit. Throughout, the emphasis falls on the trade-offs that decide whether a nanofluid represents a genuine improvement over its base fluid, and on the reasons that closed-form correlations struggle to represent nanofluid behaviour with the accuracy required for confident design, thereby preparing the ground for the computational and data-driven approaches of the chapters that follow.

## 3.1 Convective Heat Transfer and Flow Behaviour

The transition from the equilibrium thermophysical properties of the preceding chapter to the convective behaviour of the present one is not a matter of simple substitution, and it is worth pausing at the outset to say why. The properties measured on a quiescent sample describe the fluid at rest or in uniform shear, whereas a fluid in a heat exchanger flows through a temperature field and a shear field that vary across the channel and along its length, so that the local properties vary from point to point and the particle population itself may redistribute in response to the gradients. The convective performance is an integral over this varying field, weighted by the local transport, and it therefore depends not only on the properties but on how those properties are arranged by the flow, which is a question the equilibrium measurements cannot answer. This is the deep reason that convective enhancement is not simply read off from property enhancement, and it frames the mechanistic discussion that follows, in which the arrangement of particles by the flow, through migration and micro-convection, emerges as decisive [35].

The convective heat transfer of a nanofluid flowing through a channel is characterised, as for any fluid, by the heat transfer coefficient and its dimensionless form the Nusselt number, but the interpretation of these quantities for a nanofluid is complicated by the fact that the reference conductivity used to form the Nusselt number is itself altered by the particles. It is therefore essential, when comparing a nanofluid with its base fluid, to distinguish clearly between an enhancement in the heat transfer coefficient, which is what matters for the size and performance of a heat exchanger, and an enhancement in the Nusselt number, which is a dimensionless construct that may behave differently because its defining conductivity has changed. A nanofluid can exhibit a higher heat transfer coefficient than its base fluid even when its Nusselt number, referred to its own elevated conductivity, is lower, and confusion between these two measures has been a source of apparent contradiction in the literature [35]. Clarity on this point is a prerequisite for any meaningful assessment of convective performance.

The distinction may be sharpened by recalling the definitions and following the consequences through carefully. The heat transfer coefficient is the ratio of the local wall heat flux to the temperature difference between the wall and the bulk fluid, and it is the quantity that a heat-exchanger designer actually needs, because for a given wall temperature and duty it determines the surface area required. The Nusselt number is formed by multiplying this coefficient by a characteristic length and dividing by the fluid conductivity, and it is a dimensionless measure of the ratio of convective to purely conductive transport across the same length. When the fluid conductivity in the denominator is that of the nanofluid, which exceeds that of the base fluid, a given increase in the coefficient produces a smaller increase in the Nusselt number than the same coefficient increase would produce for the base fluid; in the limiting case where the coefficient rises in exact proportion to the conductivity, the Nusselt number does not rise at all. A report that a nanofluid's Nusselt number is unchanged or even reduced is therefore entirely compatible with a genuine and useful increase in its heat transfer coefficient, and the two statements describe the same physical situation viewed through different normalisations. The careful reader must always ascertain which conductivity has been used to form a reported Nusselt number, and whether comparisons between fluids have been made on a consistent basis, before drawing any conclusion about relative performance [35].

A related and equally important subtlety concerns the basis on which two fluids are compared, an issue that recurs in more quantitative form in the treatment of performance criteria later in the chapter. Two fluids may be compared at equal flow velocity, at equal mass flow rate, at equal Reynolds number, or at equal pumping power, and because the addition of particles changes the density and viscosity, these bases are no longer equivalent as they would be for a single fluid. A comparison at equal Reynolds number, for instance, requires a higher velocity for the more viscous nanofluid, which of itself raises the heat transfer, so that part of any apparent enhancement is an artefact of the comparison basis rather than a property of the fluid. The only basis that reflects the true operating economics is equal pumping power, because that is the resource actually consumed, and enhancements reported on other bases must be regarded with corresponding caution. This is not a pedantic distinction but one that can reverse the sign of the conclusion, and much of the apparent disagreement in the early literature dissolves once the comparison bases of the conflicting studies are made explicit [36].

In the laminar regime, which prevails at low Reynolds numbers in small channels and viscous fluids, the classical theory predicts a constant Nusselt number in fully developed flow, independent of the Reynolds number and determined only by the channel geometry and the thermal boundary condition. Experiments with nanofluids in laminar flow, however, frequently report Nusselt numbers that increase with particle loading and that depend on the Reynolds number in ways the classical theory forbids for a homogeneous fluid, a finding that points to mechanisms specific to the particulate nature of the suspension. Among these mechanisms are the migration of particles across the channel under the combined action of shear, viscosity gradients and Brownian diffusion, which flattens the velocity profile and thins the thermal boundary layer near the wall, and the micro-convection induced by the Brownian motion of the particles, which enhances the effective transport of energy in the near-wall region [36]. The relative importance of these effects is greatest in the developing region near the channel entrance, where the boundary layers are thin and the particle-induced disturbances are most influential, and many studies report that the enhancement is largest in this entrance region and diminishes toward the fully developed condition.

The particle-migration mechanism repays a fuller description because it is among the most physically distinctive features of nanofluid convection and because it accounts for behaviour that a homogeneous model cannot. In a pressure-driven flow through a channel the shear rate is greatest at the wall and vanishes at the centre, and several effects drive particles down this shear gradient toward the region of lower shear at the core: shear-induced migration, arising from the greater frequency of particle interactions on the high-shear side of a particle, and migration down the gradient of viscosity, which is itself a function of the local particle concentration. The result is a concentration profile that is depleted near the wall and enriched at the core, and this redistribution has two consequences for heat transfer. First, the near-wall depletion lowers the local viscosity there, which flattens the velocity profile and steepens the temperature gradient at the wall, raising the heat transfer coefficient. Second, the non-uniform concentration makes the effective conductivity itself non-uniform across the section, further complicating the relation between the bulk properties and the wall transport. Because these migration effects develop over a finite flow length and are strongest where the boundary layers are still thin, they explain both the pronounced entrance-region enhancement and its decay toward the fully developed state, and they are among the mechanisms that a genuinely two-phase computational treatment can capture but a single-phase correlation cannot [36].

The Brownian micro-convection mechanism, the second distinctive contributor in laminar flow, operates at a far smaller scale but in the same direction. Each nanoparticle, agitated by the random impacts of the surrounding liquid molecules, executes a ceaseless irregular motion, and this motion carries with it a small parcel of liquid, generating a fluctuating micro-scale velocity field superimposed on the mean flow. In the near-wall region, where the mean-flow transport of heat is weakest, this additional agitation can appreciably augment the effective transport, and because the intensity of Brownian motion increases with temperature and with decreasing particle size, the mechanism predicts an enhancement that grows under precisely those conditions, a prediction broadly borne out by experiment. The micro-convective contribution is difficult to quantify from first principles and is easily conflated with the migration effect, since both intensify near-wall transport, but the two are physically distinct, the one a systematic drift of the particle population and the other a stochastic agitation of individual particles, and a complete account of laminar enhancement must accommodate both [37].

The near-wall region deserves special attention in the laminar analysis because it is there that the resistance to heat transfer is concentrated and there that the particle-induced modifications have their greatest leverage. In laminar duct flow the heat must cross a thermal boundary layer whose thickness grows along the channel, and the coefficient is inversely related to that thickness; any mechanism that thins the boundary layer, or that augments transport within it, raises the coefficient. The depletion of particles near the wall, produced by migration, lowers the local viscosity and thereby steepens the near-wall velocity gradient, which thins the thermal boundary layer and raises the coefficient; the micro-convection of the remaining near-wall particles augments transport within the layer directly. That both mechanisms act where they are most effective, in the resistance-controlling near-wall region, explains why laminar nanofluid enhancements can exceed the modest conductivity increase by a considerable margin, and why the laminar regime, despite its lower absolute coefficients, is often where nanofluids show their most striking proportional benefit. This observation has practical weight because many of the applications for which nanofluids are most seriously considered, the cooling of compact electronic and photonic devices among them, operate in the laminar regime, so that the regime in which nanofluids perform best is also a regime of considerable technological importance [36].

In the turbulent regime, which prevails at the higher Reynolds numbers typical of industrial heat exchangers, the base-fluid heat transfer is already vigorous and the additional enhancement offered by nanoparticles is proportionally smaller, though still often significant. The classical turbulent correlations express the Nusselt number as a product of powers of the Reynolds and Prandtl numbers, and because the addition of nanoparticles raises the Prandtl number, through the combined effect of increased conductivity and increased viscosity, these correlations predict some enhancement even for a homogeneous fluid of the nanofluid's bulk properties. Whether the measured enhancement exceeds this homogeneous-fluid prediction, thereby indicating a genuinely anomalous mechanism, is a question on which the literature is divided, with some careful studies finding that the single-phase homogeneous model, using accurately measured nanofluid properties, accounts for the observed behaviour, and others reporting enhancements beyond it [37]. This division in the literature is not merely a matter of experimental disagreement but reflects a genuine ambiguity in what constitutes the fair baseline. If the nanofluid's own measured properties are used to evaluate the homogeneous-fluid correlation, and the observed heat transfer matches that prediction, then the enhancement over the base fluid is real but is fully explained by the altered bulk properties, with no need to invoke any mechanism peculiar to the particulate nature of the suspension; the nanofluid behaves, for engineering purposes, as a new homogeneous fluid with better properties. If, on the other hand, the observed heat transfer exceeds the homogeneous prediction based on measured properties, then some additional mechanism, migration, micro-convection or a modified boundary condition, must be operative. The resolution of this question in any particular case depends critically on the accuracy of the property measurements that feed the homogeneous prediction, and given the scatter in those measurements discussed in the previous chapter, it is unsurprising that studies reach differing conclusions. The pragmatic view, adequate for much design work, is that a nanofluid may be treated as a homogeneous fluid of its measured bulk properties in the turbulent regime, with any residual anomaly folded into the uncertainty, while the more searching two-phase description is reserved for the laminar and entrance regions where the particulate mechanisms are most pronounced [37]. Figure 3.1 shows the characteristic increase of the Nusselt number with Reynolds number and with particle concentration, illustrating both the strong dependence on flow rate and the additional lift conferred by the particles. The steep rise of the Nusselt number with Reynolds number, common to the base fluid and the nanofluids, reflects the fundamental strengthening of convective transport with flow rate and dwarfs, in absolute terms, the vertical separation between the curves that represents the nanoparticle contribution; this visual proportion is itself instructive, for it reminds the designer that the flow rate is a far more powerful lever on heat transfer than the nanoparticle loading, and that a nanofluid earns its place only where the flow rate cannot be increased freely, whether because of a pumping-power ceiling, a pressure-drop limit or an erosion constraint. Where the flow rate can be raised at acceptable cost, raising it is usually the more effective route to greater heat transfer; where it cannot, the nanofluid offers a means of augmenting transport without further increasing the flow, and it is in that constrained setting that its value is properly judged [37]. Recognising this hierarchy of levers, with flow rate dominant and loading secondary, guards against the error of pursuing ever higher concentrations when a simpler adjustment of the operating point would achieve more at less cost, and it keeps the role of the nanofluid in its proper and modest proportion.

The flow behaviour of a nanofluid can itself depart from that of a simple Newtonian liquid, particularly at higher concentrations and for high-aspect-ratio particles. Some nanofluids exhibit shear-thinning behaviour, in which the apparent viscosity decreases with increasing shear rate as aggregates are broken up by the flow, while others show a yield stress below which they do not flow, and still others behave as Newtonian fluids over the range of shear rates of practical interest. This rheological complexity affects both the velocity profile and the friction factor, and it means that the Reynolds number, which for a non-Newtonian fluid must be defined with care using an appropriate apparent viscosity, may not by itself organise the data as cleanly as it does for Newtonian fluids [38]. The transition from laminar to turbulent flow may also be shifted by the presence of particles, with some evidence that nanoparticles can delay or advance transition depending on their concentration and the flow geometry. Such a shift, though easily overlooked, can have outsized consequences for design, because the heat transfer and the pressure drop both change character abruptly at transition, and a nanofluid that moves the transition Reynolds number relative to the base fluid can place a fixed operating point on a different side of the transition for the two fluids, so that a comparison at fixed Reynolds number may inadvertently compare laminar behaviour in one fluid with turbulent behaviour in the other. The mechanisms by which particles influence transition are not fully settled and appear to involve both the damping of disturbances by the increased effective viscosity, which would delay transition, and the introduction of finite-amplitude disturbances by the particles themselves, which would advance it, with the balance depending on concentration and geometry. These departures from ideal behaviour are among the reasons that correlations developed for the base fluid, or for nanofluids under one set of conditions, transfer poorly to other conditions, and they reinforce the case for models that can accommodate the full complexity of the observed behaviour. The interdependence of rheology, flow regime and heat transfer that this discussion has uncovered means that the several aspects of nanofluid convection cannot be understood in isolation from one another: the rheology determines the velocity profile, which determines the shear field, which drives the particle migration, which alters the local concentration, which feeds back on both the rheology and the local conductivity, closing a loop of mutual influence that no analysis treating the aspects separately can capture. It is the existence of such feedback loops, as much as the number of variables, that places the behaviour beyond the reach of simple correlation and that rewards the coupled treatment which computation alone can furnish.

The rheological complexity deserves elaboration because it bears directly on the reliability of any dimensionless treatment of nanofluid flow. For a Newtonian fluid the viscosity is a single number independent of the rate at which the fluid is sheared, and the Reynolds number that organises the flow behaviour is unambiguous. A shear-thinning nanofluid, by contrast, presents a lower apparent viscosity where the shear rate is high, near the wall, and a higher apparent viscosity where the shear rate is low, at the core, so that no single viscosity characterises the flow and the Reynolds number must be defined with reference to some representative apparent viscosity whose choice is to a degree arbitrary. The shear-thinning behaviour itself arises from the flow-induced breakup of the loose aggregates that form at rest, and it therefore couples the rheology to the state of dispersion, which in turn depends on concentration, temperature and the history of the sample. A yield stress, where present, means that the fluid does not flow at all until a threshold stress is exceeded, producing an unsheared plug at the core of the channel that alters the velocity profile fundamentally. These non-Newtonian features are most pronounced at high loadings and for high-aspect-ratio particles, precisely the conditions under which the largest conductivity enhancements are sought, so that the pursuit of maximum enhancement tends to carry the fluid into the rheological regime where its behaviour is least amenable to simple correlation [38]. The practical lesson is that the concentration range over which a nanofluid remains comfortably Newtonian is also the range over which its convective behaviour can be predicted with the greatest confidence, and that ventures beyond it, though sometimes rewarded with larger enhancements, demand the more sophisticated treatment that computational and data-driven methods provide.

## 3.2 Effects of Particle Concentration, Size, Shape, and Material

Having established the general character of convective enhancement and the mechanisms that underlie it, the discussion now turns to the design variables through which that enhancement is controlled, for it is in the choice of concentration, size, shape and material that the physics of the preceding section becomes a matter of engineering decision. These variables do not act independently, and one of the recurring lessons of what follows is that a change made to improve one aspect of performance almost invariably affects others, so that the variables are best conceived as the axes of a coupled design space rather than as separate dials to be turned in isolation. The account proceeds from the variable of greatest practical leverage, the concentration, through size and shape to material and thence to the hybrid combinations that open the space further still.

The volume concentration of nanoparticles is the design variable that most strongly influences convective performance, and its effect is characteristically non-monotonic when the full thermal-hydraulic balance is considered. Increasing the concentration raises the effective conductivity and, through the mechanisms discussed above, tends to raise the heat transfer coefficient, so that from the standpoint of heat transfer alone a higher loading is beneficial. But increasing the concentration also raises the viscosity, and hence the pressure drop and pumping power, more steeply than it raises the conductivity, so that beyond a certain loading the incremental pumping cost outweighs the incremental heat-transfer benefit. The existence of an optimum concentration, at which the net thermal-hydraulic benefit is greatest, is one of the most robust conclusions of the nanofluid heat-transfer literature, although the value of the optimum depends strongly on the flow regime, the geometry and the particular pairing of particle and base fluid [39]. This optimum is rarely at the highest loading that can be stably suspended, and designs that simply maximise concentration in pursuit of maximum conductivity frequently perform worse, once pumping is accounted for, than designs at more modest loadings.

The physical origin of the non-monotonic optimum lies in the different scaling of the two competing effects with concentration. Over the dilute range the conductivity rises approximately linearly with volume fraction, so that the heat-transfer benefit accrues at a roughly constant rate as loading increases. The viscosity, by contrast, rises faster than linearly, with the higher-order terms of the extended suspension theories, and still more steeply once aggregation sets in, so that the hydraulic penalty accelerates as loading increases. The consequence is that the ratio of benefit to penalty, favourable at low loading where the linear conductivity gain dominates the small viscosity increase, deteriorates as loading rises and the accelerating viscosity overtakes it, passing through unity at the optimum and falling below it thereafter. The location of the optimum is not universal but depends on how steeply the viscosity rises for the particular system, which is governed in turn by particle shape, size and the propensity to aggregate, so that a system that remains well dispersed and nearly Newtonian sustains a higher optimum concentration than one that aggregates readily. This is the quantitative content of the qualitative observation that the best loading is modest: it is the loading at which the accelerating viscosity penalty first overtakes the steady conductivity benefit, and it is generally reached well before the stability limit [39].

It is instructive to note how the optimum concentration shifts with the flow regime, because this dependence has direct design consequences. In laminar flow the heat transfer is comparatively weak and any enhancement of it is proportionally valuable, while the pressure drop, though sensitive to viscosity, is modest in absolute terms; the balance therefore tolerates, and may reward, somewhat higher loadings. In turbulent flow the base-fluid heat transfer is already vigorous, so the proportional benefit of enhancement is smaller, while the pressure drop and hence the pumping penalty are large and grow rapidly with viscosity; the balance therefore favours lower loadings, and the window within which a nanofluid confers a net benefit is correspondingly narrower. The optimum loading is, in this sense, not a property of the fluid alone but a joint property of the fluid and the regime in which it is asked to work, and it must, without exception, be re-established afresh whenever either of them changes. This regime dependence means that a nanofluid formulation optimised for a laminar application, such as a compact electronics cooler operating at low Reynolds number, may be quite unsuitable for a turbulent application, such as a large industrial exchanger, and that the transferability of a formulation between duties cannot be assumed. It is one of several respects in which the optimisation of a nanofluid is inseparable from the specification of the system in which it will operate [40].

Particle size affects convective performance through several competing routes. Smaller particles present a larger total surface area per unit volume, which strengthens the interfacial and near-surface mechanisms of conductivity enhancement and intensifies the Brownian micro-convection that contributes to convective enhancement, so that on these grounds smaller particles are favourable. Smaller particles also resist sedimentation more effectively, improving stability. Working against these benefits is the greater tendency of very small particles to aggregate, driven by their high surface energy, which can produce large effective clusters that behave hydrodynamically like larger particles and that may settle or foul surfaces. The net effect of particle size on heat transfer is therefore the resultant of these opposing influences and is not always monotonic, a complexity that the experimental data reflect and that simple correlations struggle to capture [40]. The competing influences of particle size can be made concrete by tracing them through a single change. Halving the particle diameter at fixed volume fraction multiplies the number of particles eightfold and the total interfacial area between solid and liquid roughly twofold, strengthening every mechanism that depends on interfacial contact, the nanolayer, the interfacial thermal resistance averaged over more interfaces, and the Brownian agitation, whose intensity rises as the particle shrinks. On the favourable side this argues strongly for the smallest particles obtainable. On the unfavourable side, the same eightfold increase in particle number and the same rise in specific surface area increase the frequency of interparticle collisions and the total surface energy driving aggregation, so that the smaller particles, unless vigilantly stabilised, form clusters that behave hydrodynamically as larger entities and that may negate the very advantages their smallness was meant to secure. The observed dependence of heat transfer on particle size is therefore the net resultant of a monotonic microscopic benefit and a monotonic colloidal penalty, and because the two can balance differently in different systems the net dependence is sometimes increasing, sometimes decreasing and sometimes non-monotonic, a pattern that defies representation by any single simple relation and that exemplifies the difficulties catalogued at the close of this chapter. Particle material enters through its intrinsic conductivity, its density, its specific heat and its surface chemistry, with high-conductivity metals and carbon materials offering the largest potential enhancement, oxides offering a favourable balance of enhancement, stability and cost, and the choice among them being governed as much by stability and economics as by conductivity. The dominance of the oxides in the applied convective literature, mirroring their dominance in the property studies of the previous chapter, is thus not an accident of fashion but a reflection of the fact that convective performance in service depends on a fluid remaining stable, affordable and compatible over its life, criteria on which the oxides excel and on which the more conductive metals and carbons, for all their thermal promise, frequently fall short. A high intrinsic conductivity that cannot be sustained in a stable, affordable suspension is of no use to a heat exchanger, and the material rankings that emerge from convective testing under realistic conditions consequently look very different from the rankings of bulk conductivity alone, with the moderate but dependable oxides often outperforming, in net terms, the spectacular but troublesome carbons and metals.

The influence of particle material extends beyond the intrinsic conductivity to the density and specific heat, which affect convection through less obvious but real channels. A dense particle material, such as a noble metal, raises the density of the suspension and therefore, at a given volume flow, the mass flow and the momentum, which can modestly assist heat transfer but also increases the parasitic mass the pump must accelerate and the propensity of the particles to settle or to lag the fluid in accelerating flow. A particle material of low specific heat lowers the volumetric heat capacity of the suspension, diminishing the thermal energy that a given volume can carry for a given temperature rise, which works against the enhancement in coefficient and must be weighed in the balance. Surface chemistry, finally, governs not only stability but the interfacial thermal resistance between particle and liquid, the so-called Kapitza resistance, which impedes the flow of heat across the particle-liquid boundary and which can substantially blunt the enhancement expected from a high-conductivity particle if the interface is poorly matched. The selection of material is thus a genuinely multi-attribute decision in which conductivity, though the most conspicuous attribute, is far from the only one that matters, and in which the interfacial and secondary thermal properties can determine whether the promise of a high bulk conductivity is realised in practice [41].

Particle shape exerts a strong and well-documented influence, captured qualitatively by the shape factor of the Hamilton–Crosser conductivity model and manifest in convective experiments as a systematic advantage of elongated and platelet morphologies over spheres at equal loading. Figure 3.2 summarises the relative heat-transfer enhancement observed for several representative particle morphologies, showing that cylindrical, blade-like and platelet particles outperform spheres, a result usually attributed to their higher aspect ratio, which increases both the conductive pathways through the suspension and the disturbance they impart to the flow. The figure should be read, however, with the same caution that attends every enhancement reported at fixed volume concentration rather than at fixed pumping power, for the elongated morphologies that lead in heat transfer also lead in viscosity, and a ranking that would look decisive on a heat-transfer axis alone becomes far more equivocal once the hydraulic cost of each morphology is set against its thermal benefit. The platelet and cylindrical particles that head the heat-transfer ranking may therefore not head the net-performance ranking, and the ordering of morphologies by merit can differ according to whether the merit is defined by heat transfer alone or by the thermal-hydraulic balance, a distinction that the single-axis presentation of the figure necessarily suppresses and that the designer must supply from the fuller analysis developed later in the chapter. The advantage of high-aspect-ratio particles is, however, accompanied by a steeper rise in viscosity, so that the shape that maximises heat transfer is not necessarily the shape that maximises the net thermal-hydraulic benefit, and the selection of morphology, like the selection of concentration, is a trade-off rather than a simple maximisation [41]. The mechanism by which elongated particles enhance conductivity more effectively than spheres is worth stating plainly: a high-aspect-ratio particle spans a greater distance across the insulating liquid for a given volume of solid, so that a smaller number of such particles suffices to form a percolating network of touching or nearly touching particles through which heat can flow with little interruption by the resistive liquid. The same geometric spanning that builds conductive networks at low loading, however, causes the particles to interfere with one another's rotation and translation in the flow at low loadings too, which is why the viscosity of a suspension of rods or platelets rises more steeply with concentration than that of a suspension of spheres. The morphology therefore shifts both the conductivity and the viscosity curves in the same direction, and the net effect on performance depends on which shifts more in the particular regime of interest, a question that cannot be settled by geometry alone and that generally requires experiment or computation to resolve.

The single-component nanofluids considered so far already present a design space of considerable dimension, spanning concentration, size, shape and material, but that space is enlarged dramatically once the constraint of a single particle species is relaxed. Hybrid nanofluids, which combine two or more particle species, offer an additional degree of freedom through which these competing effects can be balanced. By pairing a high-conductivity but expensive or unstable component with a cheaper or more stable one, or by combining particles of different shapes, the designer can seek a composition whose net performance exceeds that of either single-component nanofluid, and a growing body of work reports synergistic effects in which the hybrid outperforms the linear combination of its constituents. The mechanisms of such synergy are not fully understood and may involve favourable packing, mutual stabilisation or complementary contributions to the several conductivity-enhancement mechanisms, and the multidimensional composition space of hybrid nanofluids, spanning the identities, proportions, sizes and shapes of two or more components, is far too large to explore exhaustively by experiment [42]. This combinatorial explosion is one of the clearest illustrations of why data-driven surrogate models, which can interpolate across a high-dimensional composition space from a limited set of measurements, have become attractive tools in nanofluid research.

The reported synergy of hybrid nanofluids, where it is genuine, admits several physical interpretations that are not mutually exclusive. One class of explanation is geometric: particles of different sizes or shapes may pack together more effectively to form conductive networks than particles of a single kind, so that a small addition of a spanning high-aspect-ratio component bridges the gaps between larger, cheaper spherical particles and completes percolation paths that neither component would form alone. A second class is colloidal: one component may adsorb on or otherwise stabilise the other, arresting the aggregation that would degrade a single-component suspension, so that the hybrid remains better dispersed and retains its enhancement longer. A third class concerns the several conductivity mechanisms themselves, with different components engaging the interfacial, Brownian and percolation contributions to differing degrees, so that a mixture accesses a more favourable combination than either constituent. Against these possibilities of positive synergy must be set the real risk of antagonism, in which two components destabilise one another or interfere with one another's conductive contribution, producing a hybrid inferior to its better constituent. The literature reports both outcomes, and the difficulty of predicting which will occur for an untried combination, from the properties of the constituents alone, is exactly the kind of high-dimensional interpolation problem at which data-driven methods excel and at which mechanistic reasoning, unaided, struggles [42]. Table 3.1 collects representative Nusselt-number correlations that have been proposed for nanofluid flows, illustrating the diversity of functional forms and the consequent difficulty of selecting a single reliable expression. The very multiplicity of the correlations gathered there is itself the most eloquent commentary on the state of the field, for if any one expression were reliably general the others would have fallen into disuse; that several coexist, each fitted to a particular family of fluids and conditions and each departing from the others outside its calibration range, is the direct evidence that no closed-form relation has succeeded in capturing the behaviour across the full space of variables. Some of the correlations simply take a classical single-phase form and substitute nanofluid bulk properties, embodying the homogeneous assumption discussed above; others append an explicit concentration-dependent enhancement factor whose exponent and coefficient are fitted empirically; still others introduce the Peclet number to represent the particle transport that the homogeneous forms omit. Each performs creditably on the data that shaped it and indifferently elsewhere, and the practitioner who must choose among them is left to match the calibration range of a correlation to the intended application as best the sparse documentation allows, an unsatisfactory situation that is precisely the motivation for the more flexible predictors developed later.

## 3.3 Nusselt Number, Pressure Drop, and Thermal Performance

The preceding sections have treated the heat-transfer benefit of nanofluids and the design variables that govern it, but a benefit assessed in isolation from its cost is no basis for a decision, and the present section restores the cost side of the ledger by bringing the hydraulic penalty into explicit view and combining it with the benefit into criteria of net worth. This combination is the intellectual heart of the chapter, for it is only when benefit and cost are weighed on a common footing that the conditional and optimum-bearing character of nanofluid performance, glimpsed repeatedly in the foregoing discussion, comes fully into focus.

The two quantities that jointly decide the practical value of a nanofluid in a flowing system are the Nusselt number, which measures the heat-transfer benefit, and the friction factor or pressure drop, which measures the hydraulic cost, and a sound assessment must weigh them together. The pressure drop in a channel is proportional to the friction factor, the length-to-diameter ratio and the dynamic pressure of the flow, and the pumping power required to overcome it is the product of the pressure drop and the volumetric flow rate. Because the addition of nanoparticles raises the viscosity and, in turbulent flow, may modestly raise the friction factor beyond the viscosity effect, the pressure drop of a nanofluid exceeds that of its base fluid at the same flow rate, and the excess grows with concentration. Since the pumping power is the product of this elevated pressure drop and the volumetric flow rate, and since it is drawn as high-quality mechanical work, the penalty it represents weighs heavily in any honest accounting, and it is the quantity that the performance criteria developed below are designed to hold in check. Figure 3.3 shows the characteristic increase of the pressure-drop ratio with concentration at several Reynolds numbers, quantifying the hydraulic penalty that must be set against the heat-transfer gain [43].

The structure of the pressure-drop penalty differs between the laminar and turbulent regimes in a way that materially affects the design calculus. In laminar flow the friction factor is inversely proportional to the Reynolds number, so that the pressure drop is directly proportional to the viscosity; a nanofluid whose viscosity exceeds that of its base fluid by a given fraction incurs a pressure-drop penalty of the same fraction at equal flow rate, a penalty that is real but that scales gently and predictably. In turbulent flow the friction factor depends only weakly on the Reynolds number, so the direct dependence on viscosity is diluted, but the higher velocities and the larger absolute pressure drops of turbulent flow mean that even a modest fractional penalty translates into a substantial absolute pumping cost. Moreover, some studies report that in turbulent flow the friction factor of a nanofluid slightly exceeds the value its bulk viscosity would predict, an excess attributed to the interaction of the particles with the turbulent eddies and the near-wall structure, so that the hydraulic penalty in turbulent flow may exceed the viscosity increase alone. The combination of a diluted viscosity dependence with a possible eddy-interaction excess makes the turbulent pressure-drop penalty harder to predict than its laminar counterpart, and it is one more reason that turbulent nanofluid flows reward the more detailed treatment that computation affords [43].

It follows from the foregoing that the pressure-drop penalty and the heat-transfer benefit do not scale together as concentration or flow rate is varied, and it is this divergence in their scaling that gives rise to the optima and the conditional advantages that characterise the field. If benefit and penalty grew in fixed proportion, the choice of concentration and flow rate would be a matter of indifference from the standpoint of net performance; it is precisely because the penalty accelerates relative to the benefit, as loading and flow rate increase, that a genuine optimum exists and that the identification of the operating conditions under which the benefit outweighs the penalty becomes the central design question. The remainder of this section develops the criteria by which that question is answered [44].

To combine the heat-transfer benefit and the hydraulic cost into a single index, several performance-evaluation criteria have been proposed, of which the most widely used compares the heat transfer achieved by the nanofluid with that of the base fluid at equal pumping power rather than at equal flow rate or equal Reynolds number. The rationale is that pumping power is the true operating cost, and a fair comparison holds it constant; under this constraint a nanofluid delivers a net benefit only if its heat-transfer enhancement exceeds the enhancement in pumping power that its higher viscosity and friction factor entail. When the comparison is made instead at equal Reynolds number or equal velocity, a nanofluid may appear beneficial simply because its higher Prandtl number raises the Nusselt number, while at equal pumping power the same fluid may show no advantage or even a penalty [44]. The choice of comparison basis is therefore not a technicality but a decision that can reverse the apparent conclusion, and the careful specification of the basis is essential to any credible claim of nanofluid superiority.

The rationale for the equal-pumping-power basis rewards a fuller statement because it is so frequently misunderstood. The purpose of a heat exchanger is to transfer a required duty, and the cost of operating it is dominated, over its service life, by the energy expended in pumping the working fluid through it. A fair comparison between a candidate nanofluid and its base fluid must therefore ask which fluid transfers more heat for the same expenditure of pumping energy, because that is the question the operating budget actually poses. To compare at equal Reynolds number is to compare at conditions that require different pumping powers, since the more viscous nanofluid needs more power to reach the same Reynolds number, and any heat-transfer advantage so obtained has been purchased with an unaccounted increase in pumping cost. When the comparison is corrected to equal pumping power, the nanofluid must be run at a lower velocity, or the channel geometry adjusted, to hold the power constant, and the heat-transfer advantage that survives this correction is the advantage that is genuinely available to the operator. It is a common and sobering finding that an enhancement impressive at equal Reynolds number shrinks markedly, and sometimes vanishes or reverses, when re-expressed at equal pumping power, and this correction alone accounts for a large part of the gap between the optimistic and the sceptical strands of the literature [44].

The thermal-hydraulic performance factor, commonly defined as the ratio of the heat-transfer enhancement to a suitable power of the friction enhancement, provides a compact criterion whose value exceeding unity indicates a net benefit at fixed pumping power. Studies that report this factor find that it exceeds unity for many nanofluids at low to moderate concentrations and in appropriate flow regimes, confirming that nanofluids can offer genuine net advantages, but that it falls below unity at high concentrations or in regimes where the friction penalty dominates, confirming that the advantage is conditional. The conditional character of the advantage is worth stating in the strongest terms, because it is the single most important corrective to the uncritical enthusiasm that attended the early literature. A nanofluid is not, in general, a better working fluid than its base fluid; it is a better working fluid under particular conditions of concentration, flow regime and geometry, and a worse one outside those conditions. The task of the designer is therefore not to decide whether to use a nanofluid in the abstract but to determine whether the specific duty at hand falls within the window in which a specific nanofluid confers a net benefit, and to select the concentration and operating point that place it as favourably within that window as the constraints allow. Posed this way, the adoption of a nanofluid becomes an ordinary engineering optimisation subject to constraints rather than an act of faith in a superior fluid, and the performance factor, correctly constructed and honestly reported, is the instrument by which that optimisation is conducted [45]. The value of the performance factor depends on the concentration, the flow regime, the geometry and the particle characteristics in a coupled and often non-monotonic manner, which means that identifying the conditions under which a nanofluid is beneficial is itself a multivariable optimisation problem [45]. That this optimisation must be performed anew for each combination of fluid, geometry and duty, rather than resolved once and for all, is a direct consequence of the coupling that pervades the subject, and it is the practical reason that the fast surrogate models of later chapters, which can evaluate the performance factor across the design space at negligible cost once trained, promise so much to the designer who would otherwise face a forbidding programme of experiment or simulation for every new case.

The precise form of the performance factor merits comment, because the power to which the friction enhancement is raised encodes an assumption about the comparison basis and is not arbitrary. When the criterion is constructed so that the heat-transfer enhancement is divided by the friction enhancement raised to the one-third power, the resulting index expresses the heat-transfer ratio at constant pumping power for turbulent flow in a fixed geometry, the exponent following from the way friction and heat transfer each depend on velocity in that regime. Different regimes and different constraints yield different exponents, and a performance factor quoted without its underlying assumptions is as ambiguous as a Nusselt number quoted without its reference conductivity. The value of the factor, properly constructed, has an immediate interpretation: a value above unity means the nanofluid delivers more heat than the base fluid for the same pumping power, and a value below unity means it delivers less. Reported values cluster above unity at low to moderate loadings in favourable regimes and fall below unity at high loadings or in friction-dominated regimes, and the concentration at which the factor is maximised is the concentration a rational design would select, absent other constraints. That this thermal-hydraulic optimum broadly coincides, as later chapters show, with the optima identified by the second-law analyses of exergy and entropy is a reassuring sign that the several criteria are measuring facets of the same underlying trade-off [45]. The table repays study not for any single entry but for the pattern its columns reveal, namely that almost every variable which raises the heat transfer also raises the pressure drop, so that the net-performance column is, in nearly every row, the outcome of a contest between two same-signed influences of differing magnitude rather than a straightforward benefit. Every other variable presents the designer with a compromise, and the recognition that net performance is generically the small difference between two larger opposing effects explains both why the advantages of nanofluids are real yet modest and why they are so sensitive to the precise conditions of operation that general prescriptions are hazardous [45].

The difficulty of predicting nanofluid convective performance from closed-form correlations, evident throughout this chapter, has several roots that together make the case for computational and data-driven methods compelling. First, the underlying property behaviour, particularly for conductivity and viscosity, resists first-principles prediction, so that any convective correlation built upon assumed property relations inherits their inaccuracy. Second, the convective enhancement appears to involve mechanisms, such as particle migration and Brownian micro-convection, that are absent from the homogeneous-fluid theory on which classical correlations rest, so that the functional forms of those correlations may be structurally inadequate. Third, the performance depends on a large number of coupled variables, including concentration, size, shape, material, temperature, flow regime and geometry, whose joint influence is difficult to represent with a small number of fitted constants. Fourth, the experimental database on which correlations are fitted is itself scattered, owing to differences in preparation, characterisation and measurement, so that even a structurally adequate correlation would be limited by the quality of its training data [46].

Each of these four difficulties deserves to be drawn out, because together they explain why the field has moved toward computation and data-driven modelling rather than persisting with ever more elaborate closed-form correlations. The first difficulty, the resistance of the property behaviour to first-principles prediction, means that a convective correlation rests on a foundation that is itself uncertain; an error in the assumed conductivity or viscosity propagates directly into the predicted Nusselt number and friction factor, and no refinement of the convective correlation can compensate for an erroneous property input. This is the sense in which the convective problem inherits the property problem, and it is why the accurate property models developed later in the book are a prerequisite for accurate convective prediction rather than a separate concern. The second difficulty, the presence of mechanisms absent from the homogeneous theory, means that the functional form of a classical correlation may be structurally wrong for a nanofluid, not merely miscalibrated; a correlation that expresses the Nusselt number as a fixed power of the Reynolds and Prandtl numbers cannot represent an entrance-region enhancement that depends on particle migration, however its constants are adjusted, because the physics it omits has no place in its structure. The third difficulty, the large number of coupled variables, means that even a correlation of the right structure would require many fitted constants to span the space of concentration, size, shape, material, temperature, regime and geometry, and that the constants fitted in one region of the space would not be expected to hold in another. The fourth difficulty, the scatter of the training data, sets a ceiling on the accuracy achievable by any model fitted to that data, mechanistic or empirical, and it means that progress depends as much on the disciplined generation of high-quality data as on the sophistication of the models fitted to it [46].

The response of the field to these difficulties has been twofold, and it defines the structure of the remainder of the book. On the one hand, computational fluid dynamics offers to resolve the flow and temperature fields directly, incorporating the two-phase mechanisms of migration and micro-convection through appropriate multiphase models and thereby dispensing with the structural assumptions of the homogeneous correlations; its cost is the expense of each simulation and its dependence, still, on accurate property inputs and appropriate closure models. On the other hand, machine-learning models offer to learn the joint influence of the many variables directly from the assembled experimental and computational data, without positing any functional form in advance, and thereby to represent dependences that no closed-form correlation captures; their cost is a hunger for data and a tendency, if unconstrained, to extrapolate unphysically beyond the region in which they were trained. The two approaches are complementary, computation generating high-quality data under controlled conditions and machine learning distilling that data, together with experiment, into fast and flexible predictors, and the physical understanding assembled in this chapter is the indispensable guide to both, informing the closures and boundary conditions of the computations and the feature selection and sanity-checking of the learned models. A reader who has grasped why the convective behaviour of nanofluids is so resistant to simple correlation is well prepared to appreciate why the more powerful methods that follow are not a fashion but a response to a genuine and well-defined difficulty [46]. Each of these difficulties is addressed, in a different way, by the methods developed in the remainder of the book: computational fluid dynamics, treated in Chapter 5, can resolve the flow and temperature fields and incorporate two-phase mechanisms directly, while machine-learning models, treated in Chapters 7 through 9, can learn the joint influence of many variables from data without requiring a closed-form mechanistic theory. The physical understanding assembled in this chapter remains the indispensable foundation for both, guiding the formulation of computational models and the selection of features for data-driven ones, and providing the physical benchmarks against which their predictions must be judged.
