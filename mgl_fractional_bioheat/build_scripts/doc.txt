Abstract

Thermal therapies deposit heat in a tumour that is surrounded by normal tissue with different thermal, mechanical and vascular properties, and the resulting temperature and stress fields depend on how heat conduction, strain rate and blood perfusion are modelled. This paper extends the layered tumour and normal tissue slab of Jangid, Singh and Mukhopadhyay [13] in three directions. The strain-rate and temperature-rate operators of the modified Green–Lindsay (MGL) theory are used; the temperature-rate lag in the energy equation is made fractional by a Caputo derivative of order $1+\alpha$ with $0<\alpha\le1$; and blood perfusion depends on temperature through a law that first increases and then shuts down above a critical temperature rise, with an optional linear dependence of conductivity on temperature handled by a Kirchhoff transform. The model is solved by a Galerkin finite element method with linear elements, a second-order backward-differentiation convolution quadrature for all time derivatives including the fractional one, and Picard iteration for the nonlinear terms. A Legendre wavelet collocation method was not implemented, and the finite element route is the one reported. For the linear model the exact layered solution is derived in the Laplace domain, where the Caputo term becomes $s^{1+\alpha}$, and is inverted numerically. For $\alpha=0.6$ the finite element temperature, displacement and stress agree with it to 0.049, 0.58 and 0.62 per cent at $t=0.4$, and a smooth-data test shows second-order convergence in time. For $\alpha=1$ the discontinuous thermal front limits the accuracy to several per cent. In the source dimensionless scaling the perfusion and laser numbers are of order $10^{-13}$ and $10^{-12}$, so they are negligible, and the perfusion studies use an explicitly rescaled perfusion number. Decreasing $\alpha$ from 1 to 0.45 removes the sharp thermal front, lowers the tumour-edge stress peak from 3.51 to 1.37 and lets the temperature reach $x=0.5$ much earlier. The field does not vanish beyond the interface once $t$ exceeds about 0.063. All results are dimensionless model predictions and carry no clinical claim.

1. Introduction

Heating of living tissue by lasers, focused ultrasound or other sources is used to destroy tumours, and the design of such treatments needs predictions of the temperature and of the thermomechanical stress that the heating produces. The classical model is the Pennes bioheat equation, in which Fourier conduction is combined with a heat sink proportional to the blood perfusion rate and to the local temperature rise. That model is parabolic and therefore gives an infinite speed of heat propagation. For very short heating times, or for tissue with a microstructure that delays the heat flux, a finite speed of thermal waves and a relaxation of the heat flux are introduced in the form of dual-phase-lag, Cattaneo–Vernotte and Moore–Gibson–Thompson (MGT) bioheat models. Because heated tissue also expands, the temperature field is coupled to the displacement field, and generalised thermoelasticity theories supply the framework for this coupling. Reviews of generalised thermoelasticity and of its modified versions are given in [9].

Among the generalised theories, the Green–Lindsay theory introduces two relaxation times through temperature-rate terms in the constitutive law and in the energy equation. Its well known defect is a displacement that can jump at the wavefront. Yu, Xue and Tian (2018) repaired this by adding a strain-rate term that uses the same relaxation time as the temperature rate, and Quintanilla (2018) studied the resulting modified Green–Lindsay (MGL) system. Recent work has clarified the mathematical significance of the strain-rate and temperature-rate terms [1], their infinite-speed behaviour [2], the fundamental solutions [3] and a Galerkin-type representation [4]. Finite element solutions of MGL problems exist for functionally graded geometries [5] and photothermal problems have been treated within the same theory [6]. The MGT theory provides a competing third-order description whose line-source and wave properties have been compared with those of MGL [7, 8]. The present paper stays within the MGL family, and the MGT model is used only as a comparison.

The source of this work is the study of Jangid, Singh and Mukhopadhyay [13]. They consider a one-dimensional slab in which a tumour occupies $0<x<h$ and normal tissue occupies $h<x<L$, with a temperature step at the surface of the tumour, a clamped and isothermal far face, continuity of displacement, stress, temperature and heat flux at the interface, and a laser source with an exponential depth profile. The thermal part is an MGT bioheat equation with a temperature-dependent conductivity that is active in the tumour only, and the system is solved by a Legendre wavelet collocation method. Their reported findings are that the MGT and Lord–Shulman responses are close, that the Biot and Green–Naghdi type III responses are larger, that the variable conductivity matters in the tumour, and that the fields vanish beyond the interface for the times considered.

Three aspects of that model call for extension. First, the elastic part of the source model is classical, whereas the MGL theory supplies the strain-rate regularisation that is known to remove the displacement discontinuity at the front. Second, memory and non-local effects in biological tissue are often described by fractional derivatives, and a Caputo derivative of order $1+\alpha$ interpolates between the diffusive limit and the integer-order hyperbolic model. The fractional-order literature in thermoelasticity and bioheat transfer is large, with contributions by Ezzat (2020), Li et al. (2019, 2020), Zhang et al. (2021), Marin et al. (2021), Ragab et al. (2021) and Hobiny et al. (2020), among others, and the MGT bioheat models of Quintanilla (2019) and Singh and co-workers (2023), and the wavelet analysis of Jangid and Mukhopadhyay (2023) are directly related. These works are named here by author and year only, because their digital object identifiers could not be verified during the preparation of this manuscript. Third, the perfusion rate of living tissue is not constant. It rises with temperature through vasodilation, and it falls when the vasculature is damaged or collapses at higher temperature, which is the physical background of the Weinbaum–Jiji type models and of the observed vascular shutdown in hyperthermia.

The contribution of the paper is a complete, verified computational treatment of this extended problem. The model (Section 2) combines the MGL operators, a fractional temperature-rate lag, a perfusion law with a critical temperature, optional temperature-dependent conductivity and the laser source, and it states exactly which terms are fractional. The solution (Section 3) is a finite element method with a convolution quadrature in time, together with an independent exact Laplace-domain solution of the linear layered problem that is used for validation. The results (Section 4) contain convergence and limit checks, a comparison of the Pennes, MGT, MGL and fractional MGL models, and studies of the fractional order, the strain-rate lag, the perfusion law, the interface position and the laser source. Section 5 summarises the findings.

Several modelling choices were made for the sake of a definite and testable problem, and they are repeated here because they affect interpretation. The preferred solution method for the extension was a Legendre wavelet collocation method with a Caputo operational matrix, as in the source study. That method was not implemented here; the finite element and convolution quadrature route was chosen instead because it could be validated against an exact solution in pure Python within the available effort. Next, the source nondimensionalisation makes the length unit about 1.45 nm and the time unit about 11.4 ps, so that the literal perfusion and laser numbers are negligible, and this fact must be understood before any perfusion result is read. Finally, the relaxation times, the perfusion law and the parameters of the law are illustrative and are not taken from a cited measurement. The numerical results are therefore properties of the model and are expressed in dimensionless form.

The Caputo derivative of order $1+\alpha$ of a field at rest equals a Riemann–Liouville integral of order $1-\alpha$ of the second time derivative, so that the fractional lag term is a weighted average of the past accelerations of the temperature with a power-law kernel $(t-s)^{-\alpha}$. A weight that decays slowly gives the tissue a long memory of its heating history, and the parameter $\tau_0^{\alpha}$ sets the strength of this memory. For $\alpha=1$ the kernel collapses to a delta function and the memory disappears, leaving the instantaneous second derivative. This is why $\alpha=1$ is the integer-order hyperbolic limit and why smaller $\alpha$ moves the model towards diffusion in the sense of a heavier tail of the response, although the model does not reduce to the Pennes equation for any $\alpha>0$ when $\tau_0>0$.

Two properties of the law should be recorded. 3. Modelling

Two solution routes are built. The first is an exact solution of the linear layered problem in the Laplace domain, inverted numerically, and it is used as the reference. The second is a finite element method with convolution quadrature in time, which handles the full nonlinear problem and is the source of all the parametric results. The preferred route of the task, a Legendre wavelet collocation method with a Caputo operational matrix, was not implemented; the reasons are given at the end of Section 3.5.

Two properties of this time discretisation are relevant to the accuracy statements of Section 4. The convolution quadrature based on BDF2 has order two for data that are smooth and compatible with the zero initial state, which means that the field and its first time derivative vanish at $t=0$ in a sufficiently regular way. The step in the surface temperature violates this condition at the surface, and the observed first-order behaviour is the known consequence of a missing starting correction. 4. Results and discussion

4.2 Validation against the exact Laplace-domain solution

Figure 2 compares the finite element temperature, displacement and stress histories with the numerically inverted Laplace-domain solution for $\alpha=1$ and $\alpha=0.6$, and Table 3 lists the relative $L_2$ errors over 19 points. The curves are sampled every $0.02$ time units, which draws the thermal front as a ramp. For $\alpha=0.6$ the agreement is close at all times: at $t=0.4$ the errors in $\theta$, $u$ and $\Sigma$ are 0.049, 0.58 and 0.62 per cent. The error is larger at the earliest time shown ($t=0.1$: 0.84, 2.9 and 1.9 per cent) because the start-up error of the step boundary data has not yet been dispersed, and the stress error rises again at $t=0.6$ (4.1 per cent) presumably when the reflected waves from the clamped face arrive. For $\alpha=1$ the errors are larger: 2.6 per cent in $\theta$ and 14 per cent in $\Sigma$ at $t=0.4$, and 17 and 42 per cent at $t=0.1$. This is not a failure of the time stepping alone. The integer-order solution has a jump in temperature at the thermal front and a jump in stress at the elastic front, and a discrete solution with 400 elements and a second-order dissipative scheme smears a jump over several elements, which gives an $L_2$ error of order of the jump width divided by the sampled length. 

Table 3 shows this directly. When the points within 0.08 of the estimated thermal and elastic fronts are excluded, the errors for $\alpha=1$ fall to 1.4 per cent in $\theta$, 1.2 per cent in $u$ and 4 per cent in $\Sigma$ at $t=0.2$, and to 0.53, 0.61 and 5.8 per cent at $t=0.4$. The masked errors at $t=0.1$ are larger for the displacement (6.3 per cent). The remaining error of the integer-order case is not fully explained. Part of it is the Laplace reference itself, whose own uncertainty near the fronts at $t=0.5$ was quantified in Section 3.1, and part is the first-order start-up error described in Section 4.3. The integer-order stress, which contains a jump at the elastic front, is the least accurate quantity, and the stress peaks reported below for $\alpha=1$ should be read as mesh-dependent magnitudes.

The laser-driven linear problem ($\theta_0=0$, $a_7=2$, $\alpha=0.7$) has errors of 0.59, 1.4 and 2.1 per cent at $t=0.2$, and 0.12, 0.85 and 1.3 per cent at $t=0.4$, which validates the load vector and the exponential particular solution. The error in $\theta$ at $t=0.6$ is larger (2.5 per cent) because the temperature there is small after the pulse has ended and the relative measure magnifies absolute differences; the pulse end at $t=0.2$ is a discontinuity of the source that the time stepping resolves with first-order accuracy. The Pennes limit ($\tau_0=\tau_1=0$) and the case $\tau_1=0$ with $\alpha=0.8$ give temperature errors of 0.019 and 0.04 per cent, which shows that the finite element code reproduces the parabolic limit and the LS-type limit of the exact solution. The stress errors for these two cases are about 11 to 12 per cent, because without the strain-rate lag the stress contains the unregularised elastic jump.

4.3 Convergence in time and space

Table 4 and Figure 3 give the errors for decreasing $\Delta t$ and $\Delta x$. For the fractional case $\alpha=0.6$ with the step (Heaviside) boundary data the error in $u$ halves with every halving of $\Delta t$, with observed orders 0.95, 0.98, 1.00, i.e. first order, whereas the same scheme with a smooth ramp $\theta_0(1-e^{-t/0.05})$ shows observed orders 2.27, 2.14, 2.09 for $u$ and 2.03, 2.01, 2.01 for $\theta$, i.e. second order. 

The loss of order for the step data is a start-up effect of the convolution quadrature: the surface value jumps in one step, the solution near the surface is presumably singular in time at $t=0$, and the quadrature, which assumes smooth data, makes an error proportional to $\Delta t$ there. The effect of centring the step on $t=0$ (Eq. (73)) was tested and changes the error by less than 1 per cent, so the first-order behaviour is a property of the singular start and not of the sampling of the step. For the integer-order case the step data again give about first order in $u$ (1.08, 0.92, 1.01), while the ramp data give an order between 1 and 1.3 only in $u$, because the ramp still produces a front across which the derivative jumps. The mesh study at $\Delta t=0.0025$ shows second-order convergence in space until the time-stepping error is reached. For the smooth data the error in $\theta$ falls from 0.036 per cent at $\Delta x=0.04$ to $8.8\times10^{-3}$ per cent at $0.02$ and $2.1\times10^{-3}$ per cent at $0.01$, the ratios being 4.1 and 4.3, and it then stays at the level of the time error. The error in $\Sigma$ drops more erratically because the stress is evaluated on element mid-points, whose positions change with the mesh. A mesh of 200 elements is already sufficient for the temperature, and the production mesh of 400 elements is conservative. The conclusion for the later studies is that the time step $\Delta t=0.0025$ gives an error in $u$ of about 0.59 per cent for $\alpha=0.6$ at $t=0.4$, and the source value $\Delta t=0.01$ gives 2.3 per cent.

4.4 Limit checks and the nonlinear terms

Four limits were checked. First, the fractional model approaches the integer-order model as $\alpha\to1$: the largest nodal difference in $\theta$ at $t=0.4$ between $\alpha=0.999$ and $\alpha=1$ is $2.79\times10^{-3}$, and between $\alpha=0.9$ and $\alpha=1$ it is $6.36\times10^{-2}$. The ratio of the two numbers corresponds to a sensitivity

which is large near $\alpha=1$ because the sharp front of the integer-order model is replaced by a smooth precursor as soon as $\alpha<1$; the limit is continuous in an integral sense but not uniformly at the front. Second, the limit of zero perfusion is exact: with the literal perfusion number $a_6=9.72\times10^{-14}$ the solution is identical to the solution with $a_6=0$ to the last digit of double precision, which confirms that the literal setup is a perfusion-free problem. Third, the nonlinear code path with $c_w$ reduced to $10^{-12}$ reproduces the linear solution exactly (maximum difference zero) after 2 iterations, so that $c_w=0$ recovers the linear case exactly. Fourth, the tumour-edge stress with $\tau_1=0$ is smaller by a factor 2.37 than with $\tau_1=0.05$, which is discussed in Section 4.7.

The nonlinear terms are verified by the steady-state check of Eq. (81). For the perfusion law with $\Pi=20$ the finite element solution at $t=8$ (obtained with $\Delta x=0.005$ and $\Delta t=0.05$) differs from the independent finite volume solution by $8.10\times10^{-5}$ in the maximum norm, and for the case with a variable tumour conductivity $\chi_1=0.2$ in addition by $7.68\times10^{-5}$. These differences are consistent with the spatial error of the coarse finite element mesh. The Picard iteration converged at every step. 4.5 Comparison of the Pennes, MGT, MGL and fractional MGL models

The linear models are compared with the Laplace-domain solution. Figure 4 gives temperature profiles at $t=0.1$ and $t=0.4$ and the stress profile at $t=0.1$ for the Pennes model ($\tau_0=\tau_1=0$), the MGT model of [13] with $\tau_q=0.1$, the LS-type model ($\tau_1=0$), the MGL model, and the fractional MGL model with $\alpha=0.75$ and $0.5$. The result that matters most is that the MGT and LS-type curves coincide: the largest difference of any field over the profile at $t=0.4$ is $1.3\times10^{-8}$. The reason is the MGT parameter $a_3=k^{*}l/(k_{10}\nu)$. With the source values this parameter is $2.49\times10^{-10}$, and the additional term $a_3/s$ in $\kappa_e$ has no effect:

This agrees with the observation of the source study that the MGT and LS responses are close. It is a consequence of the nanoscale length unit and not a general property of the MGT model: for a length unit near a millimetre $a_3$ would be of order $10^{-3}$ to $10^{-2}$ times the dimensional ratio $k^{*}/(k\nu)$, and the term would only then become relevant. 

The Pennes temperature at $t=0.1$ is 0.527 at $x=0.3$ against 0.671 for the hyperbolic models, and at $x=0.5$ it is 0.270 against zero. This is the infinite-speed property of the parabolic model, which carries a heat signal far ahead of the thermal wave. At $t=0.4$ the temperature at $x=0.5$ is 0.505 (Pennes), 0.610 (MGL), 0.576 ($\alpha=0.75$) and 0.529 ($\alpha=0.5$). The hyperbolic models are hotter than the Pennes model at this time presumably because the wave has reflected from the isothermal far face and the lag retains heat, and the fractional models lie between the Pennes and the integer-order curves, as expected from an operator that interpolates between diffusion and a wave. The MGL and LS-type temperatures are the same to plotting accuracy, as they should be since $\tau_1$ enters only the elastic equation and the weak coupling lets the displacement feed back on the temperature only at order $a_5$. The strain-rate lag changes the stress. At $t=0.1$ and $x=0.1$ the stress is -0.561 without the lag, -0.290 with $\tau_1=0.05$, -0.283 for $\alpha=0.75$ and -0.263 for $\alpha=0.5$, with Pennes at -0.415. 

A word on the size of the thermoelastic feedback is in order. The coupling coefficient $a_{51}=1.60\times10^{-4}$ is the ratio of the heat absorbed by the volumetric deformation to the heat stored thermally, and with its value the effect of the displacement on the temperature is below the plotting accuracy in all figures: switching the coupling off changes the temperature by a relative amount of the order of $a_5$ times the dilatation, which is about $10^{-4}$ here. The temperature field is therefore a thermal problem on its own, and the displacement and the stress are driven by it. This property simplifies the interpretation of the fractional and perfusion effects, since they act on the stress only through the temperature, but it also shows that the coupled character of the MGL theory is not tested by the dimensionless setup inherited from the source study. A dimensional setup with a length scale of millimetres would not change $a_5$ much, since it depends on the product $l\nu$, which is the thermal diffusivity of the tumour, and not on $l$ alone, so the weakness of the feedback is a property of the material data of Table 1.

The second effect is that decreasing $\alpha$ lowers the temperature behind the early part of the disturbance. The ordering reverses with time: the fractional models heat the edge more slowly at first because the slower relaxation spreads the heat, and they are slightly warmer later. The third effect is on the stress. The peak tumour-edge stress magnitude of Eq. (80) is 3.51, 2.86, 2.07, 1.67, 1.37 and 1.28 for $\alpha=1,\dots,0.3$, a monotone reduction by a factor 2.75 over the range, because the smooth precursor replaces the steep thermal front that drives the stress. The integer-order value is the most mesh-dependent of the group, as noted in Section 4.2. The relation between the arrival time of the front at a distance $x$ and the order is not a simple power in the present parameters, and no scaling law is claimed.

4.7 Effect of the strain-rate lag

The strain-rate lag $\tau_1$ changes the stress and the displacement but leaves the temperature unchanged to plotting accuracy (the temperature at $x=0.5$ and $t=0.4$ is 0.6098 for $\tau_1=0.05$ and 0.6098 for $\tau_1=0$), as expected for a weakly coupled system. The explanation is the shutdown part of the law. The tumour edge is heated above the critical value $\theta_{c1}=0.6$ (about 316 K), at which the tumour perfusion multiplier falls through one half and then to nearly zero, so that the heat sink is removed exactly where the temperature is highest. It follows from the assumed law, whose parameters are illustrative and which has no experimental support in this paper. With the vasodilation slope alone (a law without shutdown) the effect would have the opposite sign. 4.9 Interface position and the fields beyond the interface

The source study reports that the fields vanish beyond the interface for the times considered. In the present solution this holds only for very short times. The thermal front of the integer-order model reaches the interface at $t_h=0.063$ (Eq. (84)), and at $t=0.05$ the temperature at $x=0.3$ is 0.0000 for $\alpha=1$, while for $\alpha=0.75$ it is 0.0057 and for $\alpha=0.45$ it is 0.1023. After the arrival time the temperature is large beyond the interface: at $x=0.3$ and $t=0.1$ it is 0.342, at $x=0.5$ and $t=0.2$ it is 0.492 and at $t=0.4$ it is 0.610 for $\alpha=1$. The statement of the source study is therefore not reproduced for $\tau_0=0.1$. It could hold for the source parameters if their relaxation time is much smaller than the one assumed here, or if the times shown are shorter than $h/c_{\mathrm{th}}$, but this cannot be checked because the relaxation times of the source calculations are not transferable. 4.11 Limitations

The results are subject to the following limits, all of which affect interpretation. The wavelet collocation method requested for the extension was not implemented, and the finite element scheme has first-order accuracy for the step boundary data. The integer-order case suffers from front smearing and from a degraded Laplace reference near fronts. The relaxation times, the vasodilation and shutdown parameters, the conductivity slope and the rescaled perfusion and laser numbers are illustrative, and a different choice would change the numbers, although not the structure of the findings. The nanometre-scale length unit and the picosecond time unit mean that the dimensionless slab is not a clinical geometry, and the literal perfusion and laser terms vanish in it. Nothing in this paper is a clinical prediction, and the stress magnitudes in particular are dimensionless model values without a failure criterion.

5. Conclusion

The layered tumour and normal tissue model of Jangid, Singh and Mukhopadhyay [13] was extended to a fractional-order modified Green–Lindsay bioheat thermoelasticity with a temperature-dependent perfusion law and an optional temperature-dependent conductivity. The fractional terms are the Caputo derivatives of order $1+\alpha$ of temperature and dilatation in the energy equation; the strain-rate lag, the perfusion sink and the source are integer-order. The model was solved by a Galerkin finite element method with linear elements, a BDF2 convolution quadrature for all time derivatives and a Picard iteration for the nonlinear terms, and a Legendre wavelet collocation method was not implemented. An exact Laplace-domain solution of the linear layered problem, in which the Caputo term becomes $s^{1+\alpha}$, supplied the validation.

The main findings are the following. The code reproduces the Laplace-domain solution to 0.049 per cent in temperature and about 0.58 per cent in displacement for $\alpha=0.6$ at $t=0.4$, converges at second order in time for smooth data and at first order for the step surface temperature, and is accurate to several per cent for the integer-order case where jump fronts limit it. The nonlinear terms were verified by a steady-state comparison with an independent code to $8.1\times10^{-5}$, and the Picard iteration converged superlinearly with a mean of about 3.60 iterations per step. In the source scaling the perfusion and laser numbers are about $10^{-13}$ and $10^{-12}$, so that they have no effect, and the MGT model coincides with the LS-type model for the same reason.

Decreasing the fractional order removes the sharp thermal front and lets a small temperature precursor reach distant points earlier; it lowers the tumour-edge stress peak from 3.51 at $\alpha=1$ to 1.28 at $\alpha=0.3$. The strain-rate lag leaves the temperature unchanged and raises the early edge stress, while a perfusion law that shuts down above a critical temperature reduces the cooling effect of perfusion at the hottest part of the tumour from 17 per cent (constant perfusion, $\Pi=10$) to 2.7 per cent. The fields do not vanish beyond the interface once the thermal front has arrived, which in the present parameters happens at $t=0.063$. These are properties of an illustrative model in dimensionless form.

Future work should implement the wavelet collocation variant and compare it with the present validated solution, should replace the illustrative relaxation times and perfusion parameters with values taken from measurements with verified sources, and should treat the length and time scales of a real treatment instead of the nanometre-scale dimensionless setup. The reference list must also be completed to the full set of recent publications.
