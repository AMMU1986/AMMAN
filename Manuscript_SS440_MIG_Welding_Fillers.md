# Comparative Analysis of Microstructural Evolution and Mechanical Properties of AISI Grade 440 Stainless Steel Welds Fabricated by Metal Inert Gas Welding Using Austenitic 309L, Martensitic 410, and Super Duplex 2594 Filler Metals

---

## Abstract

The present work studies the microstructural evolution, hardness distribution, impact toughness, and fracture behaviour of multi-pass welded SS440 martensitic stainless steel using three different filler metals, namely F309L, F410, and F2594. Chromium and nickel equivalents were calculated to predict the weld metal constitution and solidification behaviour using the Schaeffler constitution diagram and the pseudo-binary solidification diagram, respectively. The F309L weld metal solidified in the austenitic–ferritic (AF) mode with Cr_eq/Ni_eq = 1.46; the F2594 weld metal, with Cr_eq/Ni_eq = 1.62, solidified in the ferritic–austenitic (FA) mode; and the F410 weld metal, with Cr_eq/Ni_eq = 3.19, solidified in the fully ferritic (F) mode and was therefore predominantly martensitic. Optical microscopy and SEM analysis of the cross-sections of the fusion zone (FZ) and heat-affected zone (HAZ) revealed columnar dendrites, ferritic–austenitic duplex regions, martensitic regions, and carbide and intermetallic precipitates. The formation of carbon-supersaturated martensite resulted in the highest weld-zone hardness for F410 (about 345–365 VHN), while soft austenite dominated the weld-zone hardness of F309L (195–220 VHN). The F2594 weld had intermediate hardness values of 285–310 VHN and a balanced duplex microstructure. Charpy impact test results showed that the toughness of F2594 was greater than that of the base metal (FZ ~275 J, HAZ ~290 J). Fractographic analysis confirmed ductile microvoid coalescence fracture in F2594 welds and quasi-cleavage brittle fracture in F410 welds owing to their martensitic structures. The results confirm that filler metal F2594 provides the best combination of toughness, moderate hardness, and ductile fracture stability for welding SS440 material.

**Keywords:** AISI 440 martensitic stainless steel; MIG welding; filler metal; Schaeffler diagram; microstructure; impact toughness; fractography

## 1. Introduction

Martensitic stainless steels find applications in engineering components that require high strength and wear resistance, good hardenability, and moderate corrosion resistance. Owing to these properties, they are widely used in the manufacture of surgical instruments, valves, bearings, pump shafts, turbine components, hydraulic rams, and many other applications [1,2]. AISI 440 stainless steel, due to its high carbon and chromium content, exhibits excellent hardness, corrosion resistance, and abrasion resistance after proper heat treatment [3]. For these reasons, AISI 440 steel is mostly preferred for engineering applications where superior wear resistance is required under a corrosive environment [4,5].

In spite of possessing excellent tribological and mechanical properties, the welding of AISI 440 steel is challenging because of its martensitic transformation characteristics as well as its high carbon content. Rapid cooling during welding promotes the formation of untempered martensite within the heat-affected zone (HAZ) and fusion zone (FZ), inducing high residual stresses that reduce toughness and increase hardness. This increases the susceptibility to solidification cracking at the welded joint. In the case of AISI 440 steel, rapid cooling drives a diffusionless transformation to martensite [6]. This is preferable for obtaining high strength after proper heat treatment, but it poses difficulties during welding. Fusion welding results in localized heating with steep thermal gradients, creating a heterogeneous microstructural region referred to as the HAZ. The regions near the weld line attain temperatures greater than the austenitization limit, resulting in grain growth. After cooling, these coarsened regions become areas of higher hardness and lower ductility, and these hardness variations increase the possibility of cold cracking [7,8].

Furthermore, this heat cycle causes additional microstructural degradation during welding. Grain coarsening near the solidus temperature lowers the toughness of the heat-affected material [9]. Moreover, during post-weld heat treatment or in multi-pass welds, embrittling intermetallic phases such as the sigma phase can form, further degrading joint toughness [10]. Owing to these metallurgical instabilities, the welded joint becomes weaker and less stable than the base metal, which is a serious problem for the long-term structural integrity of AISI 440. Hence, the selection of a robust base-metal and filler combination is necessary to minimize cold cracking, ensure sufficient toughness and hardness control, and achieve adequate corrosion resistance without requiring excessive preheat and post-weld heat treatment [11,12].

Metal inert gas (MIG) welding is extensively used in manufacturing because of its high deposition rate, its capacity to produce continuous welds, and its good process control. However, in MIG welding, the high heat input and rapid thermal cycles may considerably affect the solidification behaviour and mechanical performance of stainless steel welds, and welding parameters significantly influence weld performance. MIG welding of martensitic steels poses serious problems because of their tendency toward brittle-phase formation, hydrogen-induced cracking, and excessive grain growth. Along with the welding parameters, the metallurgical characteristics and chemical composition of the filler materials play a significant role in determining the integrity, mechanical properties, and microstructure of the final weld [13,14].

The selection of filler metal is very important in welding martensitic stainless steels because it influences solidification mode, dilution, phase transformation, and microstructural development [2]. In the current study, the effects of three different filler metals, namely ER309L, ER410, and ER2594, are investigated. The austenitic filler ER309L possesses good corrosion resistance and the ability to reduce cracking susceptibility, which makes it suitable for welding dissimilar stainless steels. Studies reveal that ER309L welds generally possess higher fusion-zone toughness than martensitic fillers, but dilution effects may lead to microstructural heterogeneities at the interface [15,16]. When a martensitic material such as AISI 440 is welded with ER309L, a two-phase structure forms, normally austenite with a small amount of delta ferrite, together with a narrow and partially diluted zone at the fusion front. This austenitic matrix prevents the formation of untempered martensite, which is a major cause of cold cracking [17].

The martensitic filler ER410 promotes ferritic and martensitic phases, which often results in greater hardness, but welding with improper parameters can lead to micro-cracking [18]. Moreover, during cooling, the formation of hard untempered martensite reduces ductility and toughness and increases the chance of weld cracking [19]. The super duplex filler ER2594 has received considerable attention because it is specifically designed to produce a two-phase microstructure (austenite and ferrite) that provides a favourable combination of good toughness, high strength, and excellent resistance to stress-corrosion cracking. However, the phase balance and properties of super duplex welds are very sensitive to cooling rate, heat input, and chemical dilution during welding [20,21]. The high chromium, molybdenum, nitrogen, and nickel contents of ER2594 contribute to its high pitting-resistance potential [22].

In spite of advances in welding metallurgy, the fillers ER309L, ER410, and ER2594 have not been systematically compared for MIG welding of AISI 440 grade under identical process parameters. The present study investigates the microstructural evolution and mechanical properties of AISI Grade 440 stainless steel welds using these three filler metals. Characterization was performed using metallographic examination, SEM, EDS, and XRD to evaluate the microstructural features, phase evolution, and elemental distribution in the weld region. The mechanical performance was assessed through hardness measurements and impact testing.

## 2. Materials and Methods

To conduct the experimental investigation, American Iron and Steel Institute (AISI) grade 440 martensitic stainless steel plates of size 300 mm × 75 mm × 12 mm were used as the substrate. Three types of filler metal were used: martensitic stainless steel 410, austenitic stainless steel 309L, and super duplex stainless steel 2594. The filler diameter was chosen as 1.2 mm to ensure compatibility with the MIG welding process. Austenitic 309L was chosen because its high nickel and chromium content provides superior corrosion resistance and ductility. The martensitic filler 410 was selected because of its metallurgical compatibility with the base metal (martensitic AISI 440). Super duplex 2594 was selected because of its balanced ferritic–austenitic microstructure, resistance to pitting corrosion, and high mechanical strength.

In order to quantify the energy input during welding, the heat-input values were calculated using Eq. (1):

Heat Input (kJ/mm) = (η × V × I) / (S × 1000)   (1)

where η is the arc efficiency (taken as 0.8), V is the voltage, I is the current in amperes, and S is the welding speed in mm/s. The arc efficiency value of η = 0.8 was chosen by referring to established literature for the gas metal arc welding (GMAW/MIG) process. Du Pont determined that arc efficiencies range between 0.75 and 0.85 for MIG welding [23]. Recommended ranges of other parameters such as voltage, current, and travel speed were also taken from the literature corresponding to MIG welding of 12 mm thick martensitic stainless steel [12,24].

To conduct the welding trials, a plate of AISI grade SS440 stainless steel with dimensions 300 mm × 75 mm × 12 mm was first machined to produce a single-V groove with a 60° included angle and a 2 mm root gap in order to maintain joint integrity and ensure adequate penetration, as shown in Fig. 1. Afterwards, butt joints were fabricated using MIG welding, as shown in Fig. 2. MIG welding was selected owing to its ease of use in industry, high deposition rate, stable arc performance, and ease of automation. Prior to welding, all surfaces were properly cleaned to remove contaminants (oxides and oils). Each trial was conducted three times to ensure accuracy of the results. Hence, a total of nine weld coupons were manufactured (three coupons per filler-metal type).

**Fig. 1.** Single-V groove joint preparation of the AISI 440 plate (60° included angle, 2 mm root gap).

**Fig. 2.** MIG-welded butt joint configuration of the AISI 440 plates.

After welding, specimens were prepared to perform the various tests. To perform the Charpy impact test at the fusion zone (FZ) and heat-affected zone (HAZ), two specimens were prepared per coupon with dimensions of 55 mm × 10 mm × 10 mm. A V-groove was then cut at the centre at a 45° angle, as shown in Fig. 3. To study the microstructure, transverse weld specimens with dimensions of 25 mm × 10 mm × 10 mm were cut from each coupon. In order to obtain a mirror-like surface, each sample was polished sequentially with emery papers (grit sizes: 100, 150, 400, 800, 1200, 1500, 2000, and 3000) and finally polished using a 0.3 µm alumina suspension. Before conducting the microstructural studies on an inverted metallurgical microscope and by scanning electron microscopy, each specimen was chemically etched in a solution of 10% oxalic acid so that the grain boundaries were properly exposed.

**Fig. 3.** Charpy impact test specimen: (a) 3D view and (b) front view.

### 2.1 Microstructural and Mechanical Characterization

A metallographic specimen containing all the important weld regions, i.e., base metal (BM), heat-affected zone (HAZ), and fusion zone (FZ), in a single plane was prepared for this purpose. The samples were gradually ground on silicon-carbide abrasive papers of grades 400 to 1200 and then polished with alumina suspension (0.3 µm) in distilled water to give a mirror-like surface finish. Electrochemical etching for the microstructural study of the AISI 440 martensitic stainless steel welds was carried out in Nital reagent at a current density of 1.6 A/cm² and a DC voltage of 6 V for 10–15 s. This etching procedure improved grain-boundary visibility and allowed visualization of the secondary-phase constituents in the weld microstructure. Microstructural analysis was performed using optical microscopy (OM) with a Dewinter metallurgical microscope, together with SEM for detailed morphological and phase analysis. The weld microstructure was analyzed in detail with special attention to features developed during welding, such as columnar dendritic structures, phase transformations in the fusion zone, grain coarsening, and carbide precipitation in the HAZ caused by the large temperature difference during welding.

The mechanical properties of the welded joints were evaluated by Vickers microhardness and Charpy impact tests. For the transverse cross-section of the weld, microhardness mapping was performed using a loading force of 500 gf and a dwell time of 10 s. Indentations were made across the BM, HAZ, and weld zone (WZ) at intervals of 0.25 mm to obtain a continuous hardness-distribution profile across the microstructural gradient. The impact toughness was estimated by the Charpy V-notch method in accordance with ASTM E23 / ISO 148-1. Standard specimens of 55 mm × 10 mm × 10 mm with a 2 mm deep V-notch of 45° angle and 0.25 mm root radius were cut from the BM, HAZ, and FZ regions. The notch position was chosen carefully to ensure it was set in the middle of the FZ for weld-metal specimens and in the near vicinity of the fusion boundary (within the coarse-grained HAZ) for HAZ specimens. To relate the absorbed impact energy to the fracture morphology and failure mechanisms, post-fracture fractography was also conducted by SEM, thereby establishing a relationship between the microstructural evolution and the dynamic load-bearing behaviour of the welded joints.

## 3. Results and Discussion

### 3.1 Prediction of Weld Metal Constitution and Solidification Mode

The Schaeffler constitution diagram shown in Fig. 4(a) was used to predict the stainless steel weld microstructures using the chromium-equivalent (Cr_eq) and nickel-equivalent (Ni_eq) values. The alloy positions were determined from the compositions listed in Table 1. SS440 (Cr_eq ≈ 20, Ni_eq ≈ 28) lies in the primary austenite (A) region because its high carbon content strongly promotes austenite formation. F309L (Cr_eq ≈ 25.05, Ni_eq ≈ 17.2) and F2594 (Cr_eq ≈ 29.75, Ni_eq ≈ 18.4) are located in the austenite + ferrite (A + F) region, indicating mixed microstructures containing both ferrite and austenite phases. In contrast, F410 (Cr_eq ≈ 13.4, Ni_eq ≈ 4.2) falls within the martensite + ferrite (M + F) region, predicting predominantly martensitic weld metal.

The solidification mode was also predicted by the pseudo-binary solidification diagram, given in Fig. 4(b), using the Cr_eq/Ni_eq ratio. The solidification mode of SS440 (ratio 0.72) is austenitic (A); that of F309L (ratio 1.46) is austenitic–ferritic (AF); F2594, with a ratio of 1.62, solidifies in the ferritic–austenitic (FA) mode; and F410, with a ratio of 3.19, solidifies as fully ferritic (F). Such differences in the solidification mode profoundly affect the final solidification structure, hardness, and toughness of the welds.

**Fig. 4.** (a) Schaeffler constitution diagram and (b) pseudo-binary solidification diagram showing the predicted positions and solidification modes of the SS440 base metal and the F309L, F410, and F2594 weld metals.

### 3.2 Microhardness Distribution

The microhardness distribution, shown in Fig. 5, supports the phase constitution predicted by the Schaeffler and pseudo-binary diagrams in Fig. 4. F410 exhibited a higher weld-zone hardness (around 345–365 VHN) in the fusion zone (0–2 mm from the weld centre) than the other two filler metals. This high hardness is attributed to the formation of carbon-supersaturated martensite in the weld metal during the fast cooling that is characteristic of martensitic stainless steel with low nickel content [25–28]. The duplex ferritic–austenitic structure arising from the FA solidification mode of F2594 resulted in intermediate hardness values ranging between 285 and 310 VHN [29–31]. In contrast, the weld-zone hardness of F309L was the lowest, at approximately 195–220 VHN, owing to the presence of soft FCC austenite and the absence of a martensitic transformation [32–34].

In the HAZ region, hardness values were found to be close to the base-metal hardness of ~155–170 VHN at a distance of ~2.1–2.3 mm from the weld centre. This softening response is known to be related to carbide coarsening and over-tempering resulting from the multiple heating and cooling cycles associated with multi-pass welding [26–28,35].

**Fig. 5.** Vickers microhardness distribution across the base metal, HAZ, and weld zone for the F309L, F410, and F2594 welds.

### 3.3 Impact Toughness

The impact toughness values shown in Fig. 6 also support the variation in the observed microstructures and hardness values. The impact toughness of F2594 in the FZ (~275 J) and HAZ (~290 J) was the highest and exceeded the base-metal toughness (~260 J). This enhanced toughness is explained by the crack-arresting property of the ferrite–austenite phase interfaces in the duplex structure [31,36,37]. The impact toughness of F410 showed intermediate values (~237 J in the FZ and ~252 J in the HAZ), consistent with the limited toughness commonly observed in martensitic weld structures [4,11]. F309L exhibited lower impact toughness (~228 J in the FZ and ~223 J in the HAZ), primarily because of intermetallic precipitation and thermal-cycling effects in the austenitic weld matrix during multi-pass welding [32–34,38].

For all filler materials, the HAZ had slightly higher or equal toughness compared with the fusion zone. This behaviour indicates that the interpass temperature of 150 °C used in the present study is appropriate for tempering the HAZ and alleviating the residual-stress concentration in the thermally affected zone [27,35,37].

**Fig. 6.** Charpy impact toughness of the fusion zone and heat-affected zone for the F309L, F410, and F2594 welds compared with the base metal.

### 3.4 Fractography and Microstructure–Property Correlation

The fractographic analysis in Fig. 7 provides an understanding of the fracture behaviour in the FZ and HAZ of the SS440 weldments obtained using the three filler metals. When combined with the impact-strength measurements (Fig. 6) and hardness profiles (Fig. 5), together with the corresponding SEM microstructures in Fig. 8, a consistent picture of the relationships between phases, fracture, and properties emerges.

The FZ fracture surface of the F309L weld in Fig. 7(a) exhibits well-developed deep dimples and a clear cellular dimple pattern at ×1000, which are unambiguous characteristics of ductile microvoid coalescence (MVC) fracture originating at fine intermetallic precipitate sites within the austenitic weld matrix. This cellular pattern is characteristic of the nucleation and growth of microvoids followed by coalescence at the finely dispersed intermetallic precipitates seen within the austenite matrix in Fig. 8(a), and it is consistent with the AF solidification mode predicted for F309L from Fig. 4(b) [32]. For F309L, the HAZ fracture surface in Fig. 7(b) changes to a wave-like, undulating morphology with granular features near the partially transformed base metal, which is characteristic of a mixed ductile–brittle fracture mode. This behaviour is related to chromium-rich carbide precipitation at the grain boundaries, as evidenced by the SEM microstructure in Fig. 8(b) [33]. The mixed fracture mode accounts for the slight decrease in impact strength for the HAZ (~223 J) compared with the FZ (~228 J), while the comparatively low hardness (195–220 VHN) reflects the predominantly austenitic matrix with reasonable toughness [39].

The FZ fracture surface of the F410 weld shown in Fig. 7(c) exhibits rough, granular features with irregular morphologies at ×1500, typical of quasi-cleavage and brittle intergranular fracture in a weld structure containing a high proportion of martensite. This microstructure results from F410 lying in the M + F region of the Schaeffler diagram with a high Cr_eq/Ni_eq ratio of 3.19. The corresponding SEM microstructure (Fig. 8(c)) shows precipitates formed at the grain boundaries, fine intermetallic particles, and coarser intermetallic clusters distributed in the martensitic matrix. These precipitates, which form because of the rapid cooling during multi-pass welding, serve as preferential crack-initiation sites and directly contribute to the quasi-cleavage fracture appearance and the reduced FZ impact strength (~237 J) [38]. The HAZ fracture surface in Fig. 7(d) also shows a well-developed cleavage-like appearance at ×2500 magnification, indicating continued brittle fracture in the heat-affected zone. In the partially tempered martensitic matrix, secondary intermetallic phases and fine precipitates are visible in the SEM images in Fig. 8(d). This martensitic microstructure and brittle fracture response are well matched to the extremely high hardness (345–365 VHN) of the weld, as high hardness and lowered toughness are intrinsic characteristics of carbon-supersaturated martensitic stainless steel welds [37].

The F2594 weld system shows the most favourable combination of microstructure, fracture characteristics, and mechanical performance among the investigated fillers. The FZ fracture surface shown in Fig. 7(e) at ×1500 magnification features a highly uniform and finely interconnected cellular dimple network, which is characteristic of fully ductile MVC fracture with high energy-absorption capacity. The duplex austenitic–ferritic microstructure offers several crack-deflection interfaces and phase-boundary blunting mechanisms that favour this fracture mode [27]. The SEM microstructure in Fig. 8(e) indicates that the columnar dendrites and needle-like ferritic structures formed during FA-mode solidification (Cr_eq/Ni_eq = 1.62) are well developed, with no coarse clustering of intermetallic precipitates that could trigger cracking [31]. The austenite islands that appear in the ferritic dendritic matrix provide the primary toughening mechanism by slowing crack propagation through localized plastic deformation, as has been widely reported in ER2594 weld deposits [29].

After thermal cycling, the HAZ fracture surface shown in Fig. 7(f) exhibits a mixed morphology with tearing ridges and locally ductile fracture characteristics, indicating that most of the material remained ductile. In agreement, the SEM microstructures in Fig. 8(f) show needle-like dendrites with fine secondary precipitates in interdendritic areas, identified as secondary austenite (γ₂) produced by solid-state transformation on cooling [30]. These features directly account for the highest impact-strength values found for the F2594 welds (~275 J in the FZ and ~290 J in the HAZ, the latter higher than the base metal at ~260 J) and the intermediate hardness range (285–310 VHN) that corresponds to a balanced duplex constitution [40].

Thus, the overall correlation between the predicted solidification mode, precipitate morphology, fracture mode, and mechanical response clearly indicates that F2594 is the most suitable filler metal for SS440 weldments, providing superior toughness, moderate hardness, and a stable ductile fracture mode in both the FZ and the HAZ of the weldments.

**Fig. 8.** SEM microstructures of the weld regions: (a) F309L FZ, (b) F309L HAZ, (c) F410 FZ, (d) F410 HAZ, (e) F2594 FZ, and (f) F2594 HAZ.

## 4. Conclusions

The microstructural evolution, hardness distribution, impact toughness, and fracture behaviour of multi-pass MIG welds of AISI 440 martensitic stainless steel were investigated using F309L, F410, and F2594 filler metals under identical welding parameters. The main conclusions are:

1. The Schaeffler and pseudo-binary diagrams predicted an AF solidification mode for F309L (Cr_eq/Ni_eq = 1.46), an FA mode for F2594 (Cr_eq/Ni_eq = 1.62), and a fully ferritic mode for F410 (Cr_eq/Ni_eq = 3.19), the last producing a predominantly martensitic weld metal; these predictions were confirmed by OM and SEM observations.
2. F410 produced the highest weld-zone hardness (345–365 VHN) due to carbon-supersaturated martensite, F2594 showed intermediate hardness (285–310 VHN) from its balanced duplex structure, and F309L showed the lowest hardness (195–220 VHN) due to its soft austenitic matrix.
3. F2594 delivered the highest impact toughness (FZ ~275 J, HAZ ~290 J), exceeding the base metal (~260 J), owing to crack arrest at ferrite–austenite interfaces; F410 (FZ ~237 J, HAZ ~252 J) and F309L (FZ ~228 J, HAZ ~223 J) were lower.
4. Fractography confirmed ductile microvoid coalescence in F2594 welds and quasi-cleavage brittle fracture in F410 welds, consistent with their respective microstructures and hardness levels.
5. Overall, F2594 is the most suitable filler metal for welding SS440, offering the best balance of toughness, moderate hardness, and stable ductile fracture behaviour in both the FZ and HAZ.
