# Comparative Analysis of Microstructural Evolution and Mechanical Properties of AISI Grade 440 Stainless Steel Welds Fabricated by Metal Inert Gas Welding Using Austenitic 309L, Martensitic 410, and Super Duplex 2594 Filler Metals

## Abstract

The present work studies the microstructural evolution, hardness distribution, impact toughness, and fracture behaviour of multi-pass welded SS440 martensitic stainless steel using three different filler metals, namely F309L, F410, and F2594. Chromium (Cr) and nickel (Ni) equivalents were calculated to predict the weld-metal constitution and solidification behaviour using the Schaeffler constitution diagram and the pseudo-binary solidification diagram, respectively. The solidification mode for F309L was austenitic–ferritic (AF) with Cr_eq/Ni_eq = 1.46; F2594 solidified in a ferritic–austenitic (FA) mode with Cr_eq/Ni_eq = 1.62; and F410 solidified fully ferritic (F) with Cr_eq/Ni_eq = 3.19, and hence the F410 weld metal was predominantly martensitic. Optical microscopy and SEM analysis of the fusion zone (FZ) and heat-affected zone (HAZ) cross-sections revealed columnar dendrites, ferritic–austenitic duplex regions, martensitic regions, and carbide and intermetallic precipitates. The formation of carbon-supersaturated martensite resulted in the highest weld-zone hardness for F410 (about 345–365 VHN), while soft austenite dominated the weld-zone hardness of F309L (195–220 VHN). The F2594 weld had intermediate hardness values of 285–310 VHN and a balanced duplex microstructure. Charpy impact test results showed that the toughness of F2594 was greater than that of the base metal (FZ ≈ 275 J, HAZ ≈ 290 J). Fractographic analysis confirmed ductile microvoid-coalescence fracture in F2594 welds and quasi-cleavage brittle fracture in F410 welds due to their martensitic structure. The results confirm that filler metal F2594 provides the best toughness, moderate hardness, and ductile fracture stability for welding SS440 material.

## 1. Introduction

Martensitic stainless steels are used in engineering components that require high strength and wear resistance, good hardenability, and moderate corrosion resistance. Owing to these properties, they are widely used in surgical instruments, valves, bearings, pump shafts, turbine components, hydraulic rams, and many other applications [1,2]. AISI 440 stainless steel, due to its high carbon and chromium content, exhibits excellent hardness, corrosion resistance, and abrasion resistance after proper heat treatment [3]. Because of these properties, AISI 440 steel is mostly preferred for engineering applications where superior wear resistance is required under corrosive environments [4,5].

Despite possessing excellent tribological and mechanical properties, the welding of AISI 440 steel is challenging because of its martensitic transformation characteristics and high carbon content. Rapid cooling during welding promotes the formation of untempered martensite within the heat-affected zone (HAZ) and the fusion zone (FZ), inducing high residual stresses that reduce toughness and increase hardness. This raises the susceptibility to solidification cracking at the welded joint. In AISI 440 steel, when the temperature is lowered rapidly, it transforms to martensite in a diffusionless manner [6]. This is beneficial for obtaining high strength after proper heat treatment, but it poses difficulties during welding.

Fusion welding results in localized heating with steep thermal gradients, creating a heterogeneous microstructural region known as the HAZ. Areas near the weld line reach temperatures above the austenitization limit, resulting in grain growth. After cooling, these coarsened regions become areas of higher hardness and lower ductility. These hardness variations increase the possibility of cold cracking [7,8]. Owing to this thermal cycle, additional microstructural degradation occurs during welding. Grain coarsening near the solidus temperature lowers the toughness of the heat-affected material [9]. Moreover, during post-weld heat treatment or in multi-pass welds, embrittling intermetallic phases such as the sigma phase can form, further degrading joint toughness [10]. Because of these metallurgical instabilities, welded joints become weaker and less stable than the base metal, which is a serious concern for the long-term structural integrity of AISI 440. Hence, selection of a robust base-metal/filler combination is necessary to minimize cold cracking, ensure sufficient toughness and hardness control, and achieve adequate corrosion resistance without requiring excessive preheat and post-weld heat treatment [11,12].

Metal inert gas (MIG) welding is extensively used in manufacturing owing to its high deposition rate and its capacity to produce continuous welds with good process control. However, in MIG welding, high heat input and rapid thermal cycles may considerably affect the solidification behaviour and mechanical performance of stainless steel welds, and welding parameters significantly influence weld performance. MIG welding of martensitic steels poses serious problems because of their tendency for brittle phase formation, hydrogen-induced cracking, and excessive grain growth. Along with the welding parameters, the metallurgical characteristics and chemical composition of the filler materials play a significant role in determining the integrity, mechanical properties, and microstructure of the final weld [12,13].

The selection of filler metal is very important in welding martensitic stainless steels because it influences solidification mode, dilution, phase transformation, and microstructural development [2]. In the current study, the effect of three different filler metals, namely ER309L, ER410, and ER2594, is studied. Austenitic filler ER309L possesses good corrosion resistance and the capability to reduce cracking susceptibility, which makes it suitable for welding dissimilar stainless steels. Studies reveal that ER309L welds generally possess higher fusion-zone toughness than martensitic fillers, but dilution effects may lead to microstructural heterogeneities at the interface [14,15]. When a martensitic material such as AISI 440 is welded with ER309L, it forms a two-phase structure, typically austenite with a little delta ferrite, along with a narrow, partially diluted zone at the fusion front. This austenitic matrix prevents the formation of untempered martensite, which is the major cause of cold cracking [16].

Martensitic stainless steel filler ER410 promotes ferritic and martensitic phases, which often result in greater hardness; however, welding with improper parameters can lead to micro-cracking [17]. Moreover, during cooling, the formation of hard untempered martensite reduces ductility and toughness and increases the chance of weld cracking [18]. Super duplex stainless steel filler ER2594 has received considerable attention because it is specifically designed to produce a two-phase microstructure (austenite and ferrite). It provides a favourable combination of good toughness, high strength, and excellent resistance to corrosion cracking. However, the properties and phase balance of super duplex welds are very sensitive to cooling rate, heat input, and chemical dilution during welding [19,20]. The high chromium, molybdenum, nitrogen, and nickel contents of ER2594 contribute to its high pitting-resistance potential [21].

Despite advances in welding metallurgy, fillers ER309L, ER410, and ER2594 have not been compared systematically for MIG welding of AISI 440 grade under identical process parameters. The present study investigates the microstructural evolution and mechanical properties of AISI Grade 440 stainless steel welds using these three filler metals. Characterization was performed using metallographic examination, SEM, EDS, and XRD to evaluate the microstructural features, phase evolution, and elemental distribution in the weld region. Mechanical performance was assessed through hardness measurements and impact testing.

## 2. Materials and Methods

### 2.1 Base Metal and Filler Materials

AISI Grade 440 martensitic stainless steel plates of size 300 mm × 75 mm × 12 mm were used as the substrate. Three filler metals were used: martensitic stainless steel ER410, austenitic stainless steel ER309L, and super duplex stainless steel ER2594. A filler diameter of 1.2 mm was chosen to ensure compatibility with the MIG welding process. The austenitic ER309L was chosen because its high nickel and chromium content provide superior corrosion resistance and ductility. Martensitic filler ER410 was selected for its metallurgical compatibility with the martensitic AISI 440 base metal. Super duplex ER2594 was selected for its balanced ferritic–austenitic microstructure, pitting-corrosion resistance, and high mechanical strength.

### 2.2 Welding Procedure and Parameters

The MIG welding parameters were selected by combining welding guidelines for martensitic stainless steels with preliminary trials. Experiments were conducted with DCEP (direct current electrode positive) polarity, a current of 110 A, and a shielding-gas flow rate of 7 L/min to ensure arc stability and atmospheric protection. The complete welding parameters are listed in Table 3.

**Table 3. MIG welding parameters**

| Welding parameter | Value/Description |
|-------------------|-------------------|
| Shielding gas flow rate | 7 L/min |
| Root pass voltage (V) | 14 |
| Root pass current (A) | 150 |
| Root pass welding speed (mm/min) | 90 |
| Root pass average heat input (kJ/mm) | 0.84 |
| Filler pass voltage (V) | 12 |
| Filler pass current (A) | 135 |
| Filler pass welding speed (mm/min) | 80 |
| Filler pass average heat input (kJ/mm) | 0.729 |
| Cumulative heat input (kJ/mm) | 2.98 |

To quantify the energy input during welding, the heat-input values were calculated using Eq. (1):

> Heat Input (kJ/mm) = (η × V × I) / (S × 1000)  (1)

where η is the arc efficiency (taken as 0.8), V is the voltage (V), I is the current (A), and S is the welding speed (mm/s). The arc efficiency of η = 0.8 was chosen based on established literature for the gas metal arc welding (GMAW/MIG) process. DuPont determined arc efficiencies in the range of 0.75–0.85 for MIG welding [22]. The recommended ranges of the other parameters, such as voltage, current, and travel speed, were also taken from literature reports corresponding to MIG welding of 12 mm thick martensitic stainless steel [12,23].

For the welding trials, an AISI Grade SS440 plate (300 mm × 75 mm × 12 mm) was machined to produce a single-V groove with a 60° included angle and a 2 mm root gap to maintain joint integrity and ensure adequate penetration (Fig. 1). Butt joints were then fabricated using MIG welding (Fig. 2). MIG was selected owing to its industrial ease of use, high deposition rate, stable arc performance, and ease of automation. Prior to welding, all surfaces were cleaned to remove contaminants (oxides and oils). Each trial was conducted three times to ensure the accuracy of results; hence, a total of nine weld coupons were manufactured (three coupons per filler-metal type).

### 2.3 Specimen Preparation

After welding, specimens were prepared for testing. For the Charpy impact test at the fusion zone (FZ) and heat-affected zone (HAZ), two specimens were prepared per coupon with dimensions of 55 mm × 10 mm × 10 mm. A V-notch was cut at the centre at a 45° angle (Fig. 3). For microstructural study, transverse weld specimens of 25 mm × 10 mm × 10 mm were cut from each coupon. To obtain a mirror-like surface, samples were polished sequentially with emery papers (grit: 100, 150, 400, 800, 1200, 1500, 2000, and 3000) and finally with 0.3 µm alumina suspension. Before microstructural examination on the inverted metallurgical microscope and the scanning electron microscope, specimens were chemically etched in a 10% oxalic acid solution to reveal the grain boundaries.

**Figure 3.** Charpy impact test specimen: (a) 3D view; (b) front view.

### 2.4 Microstructural and Mechanical Characterization

A metallographic specimen containing all the important weld regions, i.e. base metal (BM), heat-affected zone (HAZ), and fusion zone (FZ), in a single plane was prepared. The samples were ground on silicon-carbide abrasive papers from grade 400 to 1200, then polished with alumina suspension (0.3 µm) in distilled water to obtain a mirror-like finish. Electrochemical etching of the AISI 440 welds was carried out in Nital reagent at a current density of 1.6 A/cm² and a DC voltage of 6 V for 10–15 s. This etching procedure improved grain-boundary visibility and the visualization of secondary-phase constituents in the weld microstructure. Microstructural analysis was performed using optical microscopy (OM, Dewinter metallurgical microscope) together with SEM for detailed morphological and phase analysis. Special attention was given to features developed during welding, such as columnar dendritic structures, phase transformations in the fusion zone, and grain coarsening and carbide precipitation in the HAZ resulting from the large thermal gradients during welding.

Mechanical properties of the welded joints were evaluated using Vickers microhardness and Charpy impact tests. For the transverse cross-section of the weld, microhardness was measured using a load of 500 gf and a dwell time of 10 s. Indentations were made across the BM, HAZ, and weld zone (WZ) at intervals of 0.25 mm to obtain a continuous hardness-distribution profile across the microstructural gradient. Impact toughness was estimated by the Charpy V-notch method per ASTM E23/ISO 148-1. Standard specimens of 55 mm × 10 mm × 10 mm with a 2 mm deep V-notch (45° angle, 0.25 mm root radius) were cut from the BM, HAZ, and FZ regions. The notch was positioned in the middle of the FZ for weld-metal specimens and near the fusion boundary within the coarse-grained HAZ for HAZ specimens. To relate the absorbed impact energy to the fracture morphology and failure mechanisms, post-fracture fractography was conducted with SEM, enabling a relationship between the microstructural evolution and the dynamic load-bearing behaviour of the welded joints.

## 3. Results and Discussion

### 3.1 Prediction of Weld Microstructure and Solidification Mode

The Schaeffler constitution diagram shown in Fig. 4(a) was used to predict the stainless steel weld microstructures using chromium-equivalent (Cr_eq) and nickel-equivalent (Ni_eq) values. The alloy positions were determined from the compositions listed in Table 1. SS440 (Cr_eq ≈ 20, Ni_eq ≈ 28) lies in the primary austenite (A) region because its high carbon content strongly promotes austenite formation. F309L (Cr_eq ≈ 25.05, Ni_eq ≈ 17.2) and F2594 (Cr_eq ≈ 29.75, Ni_eq ≈ 18.4) are located in the austenite + ferrite (A + F) region, indicating mixed microstructures containing both ferrite and austenite phases. In contrast, F410 (Cr_eq ≈ 13.4, Ni_eq ≈ 4.2) falls within the martensite + ferrite (M + F) region, predicting a predominantly martensitic weld metal.

The solidification mode was predicted by the pseudo-binary solidification diagram, Fig. 4(b), using the Cr_eq/Ni_eq ratio. The solidification mode of SS440 (0.72) is austenitic (A), and that of F309L (1.46) is austenitic–ferritic (AF). F2594, with a ratio of 1.62, solidifies in a ferritic–austenitic (FA) mode, and F410, with a ratio of 3.19, solidifies as fully ferritic (F). These differences in solidification mode profoundly affect the weld's final solidification structure, hardness, and toughness.

**Figure 4.** (a) Schaeffler constitution diagram showing the positions of SS440 and the three filler metals; (b) pseudo-binary solidification diagram indicating solidification modes based on the Cr_eq/Ni_eq ratio.

### 3.2 Microhardness Distribution

The microhardness distribution shown in Fig. 5 supports the phase constitution predicted by the Schaeffler and pseudo-binary diagrams in Fig. 4. F410 had the highest weld-zone hardness (about 345–365 VHN) in the fusion zone (0–2 mm from the weld centre). This high hardness is attributed to the formation of carbon-supersaturated martensite in the weld metal during the rapid cooling characteristic of low-nickel martensitic stainless steel [24–27]. The duplex ferritic–austenitic structure resulting from the FA solidification mode of F2594 produced intermediate hardness values of 285–310 VHN [28–30]. In contrast, the weld-zone hardness of F309L was the lowest, at about 195–220 VHN, owing to the presence of soft FCC austenite and the absence of a martensitic transformation [31–33].

Hardness values in the HAZ region, at a distance of about 2.1–2.3 mm from the weld centre, approached the base-metal hardness of about 155–170 VHN. This softening is related to carbide coarsening and over-tempering caused by the multiple heating and cooling cycles associated with multi-pass welding [25–27,34].

**Figure 5.** Vickers microhardness distribution across the base metal, HAZ, and weld zone for the three filler metals.

### 3.3 Impact Toughness

The impact-toughness values shown in Fig. 6 are consistent with the observed microstructures and hardness values. The impact toughness of F2594 in the fusion zone (≈ 275 J) and HAZ (≈ 290 J) was the highest and exceeded the base-metal toughness (≈ 260 J). This enhanced toughness is attributed to the crack-arresting property of the ferrite–austenite phase interfaces in the duplex structure [30,35,36]. F410 showed intermediate values (≈ 237 J in the FZ and ≈ 252 J in the HAZ), commonly observed for the limited toughness of martensitic weld structures [4,11]. F309L exhibited lower impact toughness (≈ 228 J in the FZ and ≈ 223 J in the HAZ), primarily because of intermetallic precipitation and thermal-cycling effects in the austenitic weld matrix during multi-pass welding [31–33,37]. For all filler materials, the HAZ had slightly higher or similar toughness compared with the fusion zone. This behaviour indicates that the interpass temperature of 150 °C used in the present study is appropriate for tempering the HAZ and relieving residual-stress concentration in the thermally affected zone [26,34,36].

**Figure 6.** Charpy impact toughness of the fusion zone and HAZ for the three filler metals, compared with the base metal.

### 3.4 Fractographic Analysis

The fractographic analysis in Fig. 7 provides insight into the fracture behaviour in the fusion zone (FZ) and heat-affected zone (HAZ) of the SS440 weldments produced with the three filler metals. When combined with the impact-strength measurements (Fig. 6) and hardness profiles (Fig. 5), a consistent picture of the phase–fracture–property relationships emerges.

The FZ fracture surface of the F309L weld, Fig. 7(a), exhibits well-developed deep dimples and a clear cellular dimple pattern at ×1,000, which are unambiguous characteristics of ductile microvoid-coalescence (MVC) fracture originating at fine intermetallic-precipitate sites within the austenitic weld matrix. This cellular pattern is characteristic of nucleation, growth, and coalescence of microvoids at finely dispersed intermetallic precipitates in the austenite matrix seen in the SEM microstructure of Fig. 8(a), consistent with the predicted AF solidification mode for F309L from Fig. 4(b) [31]. For F309L, the HAZ fracture surface in Fig. 7(b) changes to a wave-like undulating morphology with granular features, characteristic of a mixed ductile–brittle fracture mode near the partially transformed base metal. This behaviour is related to chromium-rich carbide precipitation at grain boundaries, evidenced by the SEM microstructure in Fig. 8(b) [32]. The mixed fracture mode accounts for the slight decrease in impact strength in the HAZ (≈ 223 J) compared with the FZ (≈ 228 J); the comparatively low hardness (195–220 VHN) is due to the predominant austenitic matrix with reasonable toughness [38].

The FZ fracture surface of the F410 weld, Fig. 7(c), exhibits rough, granular features with irregular morphologies at ×1,500, typical of quasi-cleavage and brittle intergranular fracture in a weld structure containing a high proportion of martensite. This microstructure results from F410 being in the M + F region of the Schaeffler diagram with a high Cr_eq/Ni_eq ratio of 3.19. The corresponding SEM microstructure, Fig. 8(c), shows precipitates at the grain boundaries, fine intermetallic particles, and coarser intermetallic clusters distributed in the martensitic matrix. These precipitates, which form because of the rapid cooling during multi-pass welding, serve as preferential crack-initiation sites and directly contribute to the quasi-cleavage fracture appearance and reduced FZ impact strength (≈ 237 J) [37]. The HAZ fracture surface in Fig. 7(d) shows a well-developed cleavage-like appearance at ×2,500, indicating continued brittle fracture in the heat-affected zone. In the partially tempered martensitic matrix, secondary intermetallic phases and fine precipitates are visible in the SEM image of Fig. 8(d). This martensitic microstructure and brittle fracture response correspond to the very high hardness (345–365 VHN) of the weld, as high hardness and lowered toughness are intrinsic characteristics of carbon-supersaturated martensitic stainless steel welds [36].

The F2594 weld system shows the most favourable combination of microstructure, fracture characteristics, and mechanical performance among the investigated fillers. The FZ fracture surface in Fig. 7(e), at ×1,500, features a highly uniform and finely interconnected cellular dimple network, characteristic of fully ductile MVC fracture with high energy-absorption capacity. The duplex austenitic–ferritic microstructure offers several crack-deflection interfaces and phase-boundary blunting mechanisms that favour this fracture mode [26]. The SEM microstructure in Fig. 8(e) indicates well-developed columnar dendrites and needle-like ferritic structures formed during FA-mode solidification (Cr_eq/Ni_eq = 1.62), with no coarse clustering of intermetallic precipitate that could trigger cracking [30]. The austenite islands in the ferritic dendritic matrix provide the primary toughening mechanism by slowing crack propagation through localized plastic deformation, as widely reported for ER2594 weld deposits [28]. After thermal cycling, the HAZ fracture surface in Fig. 7(f) exhibits a mixed morphology with tearing ridges and locally ductile features, indicating that most of the material remained ductile. In agreement, the SEM microstructure in Fig. 8(f) shows needle-like dendrites with fine secondary precipitates in interdendritic areas, identified as secondary austenite (γ₂) produced by solid-state transformation on cooling [29]. These features account for the highest impact-strength values of the F2594 welds (≈ 275 J in the FZ and ≈ 290 J in the HAZ), the latter exceeding the base metal (≈ 260 J), and the intermediate hardness range (285–310 VHN) corresponding to a balanced duplex constitution [39].

**Figure 7.** SEM fractographs of the Charpy fracture surfaces: (a) F309L FZ; (b) F309L HAZ; (c) F410 FZ; (d) F410 HAZ; (e) F2594 FZ; (f) F2594 HAZ.

**Figure 8.** SEM microstructures of the corresponding weld regions: (a) F309L FZ; (b) F309L HAZ; (c) F410 FZ; (d) F410 HAZ; (e) F2594 FZ; (f) F2594 HAZ.

Overall, the correlation between the predicted solidification mode, precipitate morphology, fracture mode, and mechanical response clearly indicates that F2594 is the most suitable filler metal for SS440 weldments, providing superior toughness, moderate hardness, and a stable ductile fracture mode in both the FZ and the HAZ.

## 4. Conclusions

1. The weld-metal constitution and solidification mode predicted by the Schaeffler and pseudo-binary diagrams were F309L: austenitic–ferritic (AF, Cr_eq/Ni_eq = 1.46); F2594: ferritic–austenitic (FA, Cr_eq/Ni_eq = 1.62); and F410: fully ferritic (F, Cr_eq/Ni_eq = 3.19), yielding a predominantly martensitic F410 weld.
2. F410 produced the highest weld-zone hardness (345–365 VHN) due to carbon-supersaturated martensite, F2594 gave intermediate hardness (285–310 VHN) from its balanced duplex structure, and F309L showed the lowest hardness (195–220 VHN) owing to soft austenite.
3. F2594 exhibited the highest impact toughness (FZ ≈ 275 J, HAZ ≈ 290 J), exceeding the base metal (≈ 260 J); F410 (FZ ≈ 237 J, HAZ ≈ 252 J) and F309L (FZ ≈ 228 J, HAZ ≈ 223 J) were lower.
4. Fractography confirmed ductile microvoid-coalescence fracture for F2594, mixed ductile–brittle fracture for F309L, and quasi-cleavage brittle fracture for F410.
5. Overall, F2594 is the most suitable filler metal for MIG welding of SS440, offering the best combination of toughness, moderate hardness, and ductile fracture stability.
